## 1.0 Constitutional Development — Framework

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Constitutional Development | The cumulative process by which British statutes, charters, and reforms created the institutional skeleton of Indian government from Company rule to the Constitution of India |
| Regulating / Charter / GOI Acts | Successive British Parliamentary laws that reorganised Company authority, Crown control, legislatures, and federal design in India |
| Dyarchy | Dual rule in provinces (1919) splitting subjects between ministers and the Governor |
| Provincial Autonomy | Responsible government in provincial subjects under the Government of India Act, 1935, ending provincial Dyarchy |
| Constituent Assembly | Body that framed the Constitution of India (first met 9 December 1946) |

### How It Works

- Constitutional development in India is best read as a continuous chain of British Parliamentary interventions that progressively centralised authority, then partially decentralised it, and finally supplied many structures that the Constitution borrowed or adapted.
- Early Acts (1773–1858) answered crises of Company misrule by placing political control under the British Crown while keeping commercial machinery until Charter reforms ended trading functions.
- Mid-phase Councils Acts (1861–1909) associated Indians with legislation without granting responsible government, and 1909 communalised representation through separate electorates.
- The 1919 settlement introduced Dyarchy and direct elections as a limited experiment in responsible government at the provincial level.
- The 1935 Act created the most detailed blueprint—Provincial Autonomy, three legislative lists, a Federal Court, and a proposed All-India Federation—much of which shaped the 1950 Constitution even where federation never operated.
- For UPPCS, chronology, firsts (first GG of India, first open competition, first separate electorates), and “what never came into force” traps matter more than narrative detail.
- Constituent Assembly debates and Constitution-making complete the arc from colonial statute to republican supreme law, with adoption on 26 November 1949 and enforcement on 26 January 1950.
- Borrowed features questions often secretly test this historical background: Concurrent List and Emergency design echo 1935; parliamentary form echoes British practice refined through these Acts.

> **Exam note:** Treat 1773→1935 as one syllabus unit; UPPCS frequently mixes “which Act first introduced X” with “which provision never operated.”

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Regulating Act 1773 is the first landmark step toward central administration in British India.
- Pitt’s India Act 1784 established Dual Government of Company and Crown.
- Charter Act 1833 created the Governor-General of India and ended Company commercial functions.
- Charter Act 1853 introduced open competition for the Indian Civil Service.
- Government of India Act 1858 transferred rule from Company to Crown.
- Indian Councils Act 1861 introduced the portfolio system.
- Morley–Minto Reforms 1909 introduced separate electorates for Muslims.
- Government of India Act 1919 introduced Dyarchy in provinces and the Chamber of Princes.
- Government of India Act 1935 introduced Provincial Autonomy and provided for an All-India Federation that never came into being.
- Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and enforced on 26 January 1950.

## 1.1 Regulating Act, 1773

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Regulating Act, 1773 | British Parliamentary statute that first attempted systematic regulation of East India Company rule in India |
| Governor-General of Bengal | Chief executive created for Bengal Presidency, with supervisory authority over Bombay and Madras |
| Supreme Court at Calcutta | Royal court established at Calcutta in 1774 under the Regulating Act framework |
| Court of Directors | Company’s directing body in Britain, required under the Act to report on revenue, civil, and military affairs |

#### Causes

1. Company servants’ private trade and corruption after Plassey–Buxar created political scandal in Britain.
2. The Bengal famine and administrative breakdown exposed the Company’s unfitness for unsupervised territorial rule.
3. Conflict between Company authorities and the need for Parliamentary oversight of a trading corporation that had become a territorial power.
4. Financial distress of the Company forced it to seek British government support, enabling statutory regulation.
5. Absence of a clear hierarchy among Presidencies produced incoherent policy across Bengal, Bombay, and Madras.

#### Course — Step by Step

1. The Act designated a Governor-General of Bengal with a four-member Executive Council.
2. Warren Hastings became the first Governor-General of Bengal.
3. Governors of Bombay and Madras were made subordinate to the Governor-General of Bengal in specified matters.
4. A Supreme Court was established at Calcutta (effective 1774) with Sir Elijah Impey as the first Chief Justice.
5. The Court of Directors was required to submit reports on civil, military, and revenue matters to the British government.
6. Company servants were restricted regarding private trade and presents to curb corruption.
7. The Act attempted to control the Company’s Indian administration through Parliamentary legislation rather than purely internal Company rules.
8. Ambiguities remained between the Supreme Court’s jurisdiction and the Governor-General-in-Council’s authority, producing later conflicts.

### How It Works

- The Regulating Act converted ad hoc Company conquest into a legally regulated political administration answerable in some degree to Parliament.
- Centralisation began by elevating Bengal’s Governor-General above the other two Presidencies for war, diplomacy, and related control.
- The Supreme Court introduced English-style royal justice into Calcutta, creating a dual authority problem with the Company’s executive government.
- Reporting duties of the Court of Directors opened a channel of British governmental supervision even before Pitt’s fuller Dual Government.
- The Act did not create a Governor-General of India; that title and all-India executive unity came only with the Charter Act of 1833.
- Personnel reform was limited; private trade curbs were significant in intent but uneven in early enforcement.
- For exams, lock three identifiers together: first GG of Bengal, Warren Hastings, Supreme Court Calcutta 1774.
- Subordination of Bombay and Madras is a standard “first step toward central administration” phrasing in prelims keys.
- Jurisdictional clashes after 1774 explain why the **Act of 1781 (Amending Act)** had to clarify executive–judicial boundaries between the Supreme Court and the Governor-General-in-Council.
- The Act is the chronological starting gun for the entire constitutional development syllabus in Indian polity.

| Administrative | GG of Bengal + Council; Presidency subordination begins |
| Judicial | Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774) |
| Structural gap | Dualism and jurisdictional conflict with Company executive remained |

#### Key Persons

| Person | Role |
|--------|------|
| Warren Hastings | First Governor-General of Bengal |
| Sir Elijah Impey | First Chief Justice of the Supreme Court at Calcutta |

- First Governor-General of Bengal was Warren Hastings.
- Supreme Court at Calcutta was established in 1774.

- Court of Directors had to report to the British government.
- Private trade and presents by Company servants were restricted.
- This Act is regarded as the first step toward central administration in India.
- It did not end Company rule; it regulated it.
- Jurisdictional conflict between Supreme Court and Council was a major weakness.

## 1.2 Pitt's India Act, 1784

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Pitt’s India Act, 1784 | Statute that established Dual Government by separating the Company’s commercial management from Crown political control |
| Board of Control | Body of six Commissioners (including Cabinet ministers) created to supervise civil, military, and revenue affairs of British possessions in India |
| Court of Directors | Company’s commercial governing body that continued to manage trade and patronage within limits |
| Dual Government (Company–Crown) | System in which Crown controlled political affairs through the Board while Company retained commercial functions |

#### Causes

1. Defects of the Regulating Act—especially executive–judicial conflict and weak home control—demanded a clearer supervisory mechanism.
2. Political controversy in Britain over Company power required William Pitt’s government to assert Crown supremacy in Indian politics.
3. The Company remained commercially active, so a complete takeover was avoided in favour of Dual Government.
4. Need to use the phrase and concept of “British possessions in India” to acknowledge territorial sovereignty under British political control.
5. Administrative inefficiency and factionalism in India after 1773 made home-government direction urgent.

#### Course — Step by Step

1. A Board of Control was created to guide and control Company’s civil, military, and revenue administration.
2. The Court of Directors was left with commercial functions and a reduced but still important role in patronage.
3. Dual Government institutionalised Crown political supremacy alongside Company commercial identity.
4. The Act distinguished political affairs from commercial affairs more sharply than 1773.
5. Indian territories were described in a manner that treated them as British possessions for purposes of political control.
6. The Governor-General-in-Council remained the key Indian executive, now under stronger home political direction.
7. The Board could send secret orders on political and military matters through a confidential mechanism.
8. The settlement remained the constitutional basis of Company–Crown relations until 1858.

### How It Works

- Pitt’s India Act answered Regulating Act failures by placing Indian political governance under a ministerial Board in Britain.
- The Company did not disappear; it continued as a commercial corporation while losing autonomous political mastery.
- Board of Control versus Court of Directors is the classic comparison pair for this Act.
- “British possessions in India” language is an exam keyword signalling Crown’s political claim.
- Dual Government here means Company commercial + Crown political, which must not be confused with Dyarchy of 1919.
- Home control tightened without immediate annexation of administration into a Crown colony form.
- Conflicts of authority could still arise, but the constitutional principle of political supremacy of the British government was settled.
- For chronology questions, 1784 sits between Regulating 1773 and the reforming Charter Acts.
- Patronage and commerce remaining with Directors explains why Company influence survived into the nineteenth century.
- The 1858 Act finally ended this Dual Government by transferring authority wholly to the Crown.

|--------|--------|
| Political | Crown supremacy over Indian political affairs via Board of Control |
| Commercial | Company retained trading/commercial management |

| Longevity | Dual Government lasted until GOI Act 1858 |

## 1.3 Charter Acts

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Charter Acts | Periodic British statutes renewing and revising the East India Company’s charter and Indian governance arrangements |
| Charter Act, 1813 | Act that ended the Company’s monopoly of Indian trade except tea and China trade, and allotted funds for education |
| Charter Act, 1833 | Act that created the Governor-General of India, ended Company commercial functions, and added a Law Member |
| Charter Act, 1853 | Act that introduced open competition for the ICS, separated legislative and executive functions of the Council, and ended automatic 20-year renewal logic |
| Law Member | Fourth/legislative member of the Governor-General’s Council (Macaulay was the first), associated with Charter Act 1833 |

### How It Works

- Charter Acts functioned as renewal-cum-reform statutes through which Parliament reshaped Company privileges and Indian constitutional machinery.
- The Charter Act of 1813 ended the Company’s monopoly over Indian trade (except tea and trade with China) and permitted Christian missionary activity, while providing one lakh rupees for education.
- The Charter Act of 1833 completed a decisive centralising leap by converting the Governor-General of Bengal into the Governor-General of India.
- Under 1833, the Company’s commercial activities ended and it became a purely administrative/political body under Crown superintendence.
- Charter Act 1833 introduced a Law Member in the Governor-General’s Council; Lord Macaulay was the first Law Member.
- Open competition for civil services was envisaged in the 1833 discussions/provisions in principle, but effective open competition is credited in standard polity teaching to the Charter Act of 1853.
- The Charter Act of 1853 separated the legislative machinery from the executive Council by expanding and distinguishing the Legislative Council.
- Charter Act 1853 introduced open competition for recruitment to the Indian Civil Service, weakening the Directors’ old patronage monopoly over appointments.
- Unlike earlier renewals, the 1853 Charter did not guarantee a fixed twenty-year renewal in the old style, signalling that Company rule was nearing its end.
- Local representation in the enlarged legislative wing after 1853 included representation connected with provinces, marking a step toward legislative institutionalisation.
- For UPPCS/UPSC, 1833 and 1853 carry almost all Charter-related marks; 1813 appears mainly for monopoly-end and education-grant facts.
- Always pair: 1833 = GG of India + end of commerce + Law Member; 1853 = open competition + legislative separation + no routine 20-year renewal.

#### Charter Act, 1833 — full focus

##### Causes (1833)

1. Administrative need for a single all-India executive authority instead of Presidency-centred GG of Bengal.
2. Decline of Company’s commercial rationale after 1813 erosion of monopoly.
3. Demand for systematic law-making and legal codification in British India.
4. British free-trade ideology favouring end of Company commercial privileges.
5. Need to clarify central legislative authority over British India.

##### Course — Step by Step (1833)

1. Governor-General of Bengal became Governor-General of India.
2. Governor-General-in-Council received exclusive legislative powers for British India in a strengthened central form.
3. Company’s commercial functions were ended.
4. A Law Member was added to the Council (Macaulay).
5. Presidency Governments lost independent law-making parity relative to the new central legislative design.
6. Attempts toward open competition for services were indicated, though practical enforcement is tied to later 1853 settlement in standard answers.
7. Centralisation of administration and legislation deepened.

## 1.4 Government of India Act, 1858

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Government of India Act, 1858 | Statute that ended East India Company rule and transferred Indian administration to the British Crown |
| Secretary of State for India | British Cabinet minister responsible for Indian affairs after 1858, exercising powers formerly associated with Company home authorities |
| Council of India | Advisory/controlling council assisting the Secretary of State (often remembered as a council of fifteen in standard treatments) |
| Viceroy | Title used for the Governor-General as the Crown’s personal representative, especially in relation to princely states |

#### Causes

1. The Revolt of 1857 destroyed political confidence in Company governance.
2. Dual Government under Pitt’s system was blamed for diffused responsibility.
3. British public and Parliamentary opinion demanded direct Crown responsibility for India.
4. Company commercial identity had already ended in substance after 1833, making Company political rule anomalous.
5. Need for a single clear chain of command from British Cabinet to Indian executive.

#### Course — Step by Step

1. Territories and revenues of India were transferred from the Company to the Crown.
2. The office of Secretary of State for India was created as a Cabinet minister.
3. A Council of India was established to assist the Secretary of State.
4. The Governor-General received the additional title of Viceroy as Crown representative.
5. Court of Directors and Board of Control were abolished, ending Dual Government.
6. Appointment and control channels for Indian administration passed into Crown-ministerial hands.
7. Indian administration became directly responsible to the British Parliament through the Secretary of State.
8. Princely-state relations were thereafter conducted in the idiom of Crown paramountcy via the Viceroy.

### How It Works

- The 1858 Act is the constitutional death certificate of Company rule and the birth certificate of Crown rule.
- Secretary of State for India in London became the decisive home authority, replacing Board of Control–Directors dualism.
- The Council of India provided institutional advice and continuity around the Secretary of State.
- In India, the same officer remained Governor-General for administration while “Viceroy” expressed representative sovereignty of the Crown.
- Lord Canning is associated as the first Governor-General who was also Viceroy under the new order.
- The **Queen’s Proclamation (1 November 1858)** announced Crown assumption of Indian government, promised non-interference in religion, and equal treatment of subjects — treat it as the political companion to the 1858 Act.
- Parliamentary responsibility meant Indian issues could be raised in the British Parliament through ministerial accountability.
- The Act reorganised authority without yet creating modern representative legislatures; that came through later Councils Acts.
- Ending Board of Control and Court of Directors is the clean institutional marker of 1858 in one-liners.
- Paramountcy over princely states becomes clearer in Crown-Viceroy language after this Act.
- All later reforms (1861–1935) operate inside this Crown-rule constitutional envelope until independence legislation.

## 1.5 Indian Councils Acts & Morley–Minto Reforms, 1909

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Indian Councils Act, 1861 | Act that associated Indians with law-making, introduced the portfolio system, and restored legislative powers to Bombay and Madras |
| Portfolio system | System (under Canning) by which each member of the Executive Council was assigned specific departmental charge |
| Indian Councils Act, 1892 | Act that enlarged Councils, allowed budget discussion and interpellation-type questioning, and introduced a form of indirect election/recommendation |
| Indian Councils Act, 1909 (Morley–Minto Reforms) | Act that further enlarged Councils and introduced separate electorates for Muslims |
| Separate electorate | Electoral system in which a community elects its own representatives through a separate voters’ roll |

### How It Works

- After 1858, Councils Acts gradually widened Indian association with legislation without conceding responsible government.
- The Indian Councils Act of 1861 introduced the portfolio system under Lord Canning, making executive business departmental and efficient.
- The 1861 Act provided for nomination of Indians to the expanded Legislative Council, beginning official Indian participation in law-making.
- Legislative powers of Bombay and Madras Presidencies, earlier centralised away, were restored in significant measure by the 1861 settlement.
- The Governor-General received ordinance-making power for emergencies under the 1861 framework.
- The Indian Councils Act of 1892 enlarged the legislative councils and permitted discussion of the budget.
- Under 1892, members could ask questions, increasing limited legislative oversight without control over the executive.
- The 1892 system used indirect election through recommendations by local bodies and associations rather than full direct popular election.
- The Indian Councils Act of 1909 (Morley–Minto Reforms) substantially enlarged Imperial and Provincial Legislative Councils.
- The 1909 Act introduced separate electorates for Muslims, communalising representation at the constitutional level.
- Satyendra Prasad Sinha became the first Indian member of the Governor-General’s Executive Council in the Morley–Minto phase.
- Morley was Secretary of State and Minto was Viceroy; together their names brand the 1909 reforms.
- These Acts must be read as “association, not responsibility”: executives remained irremovable by Indian legislative majorities.
- For prelims, 1861 = portfolio; 1892 = budget discussion/questions/indirect election; 1909 = Muslim separate electorates.

> **Exam note:** UPPCS has directly tested the 1861 portfolio system; do not migrate that fact to 1892 or 1909.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Indian Councils Act 1861 introduced the portfolio system.
- Lord Canning is associated with the portfolio system.
- 1861 restored legislative powers to Bombay and Madras.
- 1861 allowed nomination of Indians to the Legislative Council.
- Indian Councils Act 1892 allowed budget discussion.
- 1892 introduced an element of indirect election.
- Members could ask questions after 1892.
- Morley–Minto Reforms were embodied in the Indian Councils Act 1909.
- 1909 introduced separate electorates for Muslims.
- S.P. Sinha was the first Indian Executive Councillor at the Centre in this era.
- 1909 enlarged legislative councils significantly.
- Responsible government was not introduced by 1861, 1892, or 1909.

## 1.6 Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms & Government of India Act, 1919 (Dyarchy)

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms | Reform scheme named after Secretary of State Edwin Montagu and Viceroy Lord Chelmsford, enacted as the Government of India Act, 1919 |
| Dyarchy | Dual system of provincial government dividing subjects into Transferred and Reserved categories |
| Transferred subjects | Subjects administered by Indian ministers responsible to the provincial legislature |
| Reserved subjects | Subjects retained by the Governor and his Executive Councillors, not responsible to the legislature |
| Chamber of Princes (Narendra Mandal) | Consultative body of princely rulers established under the 1919 settlement |

#### Causes

1. Indian nationalist demand after World War I for greater self-government could no longer be ignored.
2. Montagu’s August 1917 declaration promised increasing association of Indians in every branch of administration and progressive realisation of responsible government.
3. Limitations of Morley–Minto Councils (association without responsibility) had become politically obsolete.
4. British need to reward Indian wartime cooperation while retaining imperial control over key subjects.
5. Administrative experience suggested provinces as the laboratory for limited responsible government.

#### Course — Step by Step

1. The Government of India Act, 1919 introduced Dyarchy in the provinces.
2. Provincial subjects were classified into Transferred and Reserved lists.
3. Transferred subjects were entrusted to ministers responsible to the legislative council.
4. Reserved subjects remained with the Governor and official Executive Councillors.
5. Bicameralism was introduced at the Centre (Council of State and Legislative Assembly).
6. Direct elections were introduced for an enlarged number of seats under a restricted franchise.
7. A Public Service Commission was provided for to recruit for services.
8. The Chamber of Princes was established as a consultative forum for princely states.

### How It Works

- The 1919 Act is the first major statutory experiment in responsible government in British India, confined to provincial Transferred subjects.
- Dyarchy split the provincial executive so that ministers could be answerable to the legislature only in a defined subject zone.
- Governors retained special powers and control over Reserved subjects such as key law-and-order and revenue-related domains in the classical Dyarchy design.
- At the Centre, the government remained largely responsible to the British Parliament through the Secretary of State, not to an Indian ministry in the full responsible sense.
- Bicameralism at the Centre created an upper **Council of State (~60)** and a lower **Legislative Assembly (~140)** — standard size tags in matching questions.
- Direct elections expanded political participation compared with the indirect devices of 1892, though franchise remained narrow.
- Classic **Transferred** examples: education, health, local self-government, agriculture, public works (ministers’ zone).
- Classic **Reserved** examples: police, justice, land revenue, irrigation, press control (Governor’s zone) — exact lists vary by textbook wording, but the police/law-and-order vs education/local-self-govt contrast is the exam filter.
- The Act authorised a Statutory Commission to review the working of the reforms after ten years, which later linked to the Simon Commission pathway.
- Separation of **central and provincial subjects** advanced federal thinking even before the 1935 Act.
- The Chamber of Princes (Narendra Mandal) gave princely rulers a collective consultative platform and is a high-value UPPCS fact tied specifically to 1919.
- Public Service Commission provision marks institutionalisation of merit recruitment supervision beyond the bare open competition rule of 1853.
- Dyarchy was widely criticised as unworkable because ministers lacked control over finance and key Reserved levers.
- Failure and unpopularity of Dyarchy prepared the political case for Provincial Autonomy under the 1935 Act.

| Centre | Bicameral legislature; limited responsible government not fully realised |
| Political participation | Direct elections under restricted franchise |
| Princely states | Chamber of Princes established |
| Institutional | Provision for Public Service Commission |

#### Key Persons

| Person | Role |
|--------|------|

## 1.7 Government of India Act, 1935 (Provincial Autonomy)

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Government of India Act, 1935 | Most detailed British constitutional statute for India; introduced Provincial Autonomy and provided for an All-India Federation |
| Provincial Autonomy | System ending provincial Dyarchy and establishing responsible government in provincial subjects, subject to Governor’s special powers |
| All-India Federation | Proposed federation of British Indian provinces and princely states that never came into being |
| Federal / Provincial / Concurrent Lists | Threefold distribution of legislative subjects under the 1935 Act |
| Federal Court | Court established under the 1935 Act to decide federal disputes and hear specified appeals |
| Dyarchy at the Centre | Proposed central dual system under 1935 that never operated |

#### Causes

1. Failure of Dyarchy and nationalist demand for fuller provincial responsibility required a new settlement.
2. Simon Commission report and Round Table Conferences sought a federal constitutional design including princely states.
3. Communal and princely bargaining needed an elaborate legal distribution of powers.
4. British desire to retain central imperial controls while conceding provincial self-government.
5. Administrative need for modern institutions such as a federal court and reserve bank framework linked to constitutional reorganisation.

#### Course — Step by Step

1. Provincial Dyarchy was abolished and Provincial Autonomy was introduced.
2. An All-India Federation was provided for, comprising provinces and acceding princely states.
3. Dyarchy was proposed at the Centre, but this central Dyarchy never came into operation.
4. Legislative subjects were distributed among Federal, Provincial, and Concurrent Lists.
5. A Federal Court was established (inaugurated later in the late 1930s) as the federal judicial organ.
6. Burma was separated from India and Aden was separated under the broader 1935 reorganisation scheme.
7. The Act provided for the establishment of a Reserve Bank of India framework linkage in the constitutional-economic settlement of the period.
8. Rigid Governor’s special responsibilities and central safeguards retained British control despite provincial ministries.

### How It Works

- The 1935 Act is the single most important colonial statute for understanding the institutional ancestry of the Constitution of India.
- Provincial Autonomy meant provincial ministers responsible to provincial legislatures in provincial subjects, replacing the Transferred/Reserved split.
- In practice, Provincial Autonomy operated from 1937 with Congress and other ministries, proving the Act’s provincial half was real.
- The All-India Federation never materialised because princely states did not accede in the required manner and political conditions blocked implementation.
- Proposed Dyarchy at the Centre likewise remained a paper arrangement and did not function.
- The three lists created a federal-style distribution later mirrored, with changes, in the Constitution’s Union–State–Concurrent scheme — standard count tags: **Federal 59 / Provincial 54 / Concurrent 36**.
- **Residuary powers** under the 1935 design lay with the **Viceroy** (not with provinces) — strong-centre federalism.
- The Act **abolished the Council of India** that had assisted the Secretary of State under the 1858 system.
- The Federal Court was established (**inaugurated 1937** at Delhi) as the federal judicial organ — institutional bridge toward the Supreme Court of India.
- Separation of **Burma** and **Aden** is a territorial-administrative marker frequently used in matching questions.
- Bicameralism in **six** of eleven provinces, enlarged franchise (~**10%** of population in standard teaching), and communal representation devices continued and complicated politics.
- Provision for **RBI**, Federal/Provincial/Joint **Public Service Commissions**, and All-India Services continuity are high-yield institutional locks.
- Governor’s overriding powers / special responsibilities meant autonomy was substantial but not absolute sovereignty.
- For exams, always separate what operated (Provincial Autonomy from **1937**, lists, Federal Court 1937, RBI era, Burma/Aden separation, Council of India abolished) from what never came (All-India Federation; Central Dyarchy).

| Centre | Dyarchy proposed but never operated |
| Lists & judiciary | Three lists; Federal Court established |
| Territory | Burma and Aden separated from India |

#### Key Persons

| Person | Role |
|--------|------|

## 1.8 Indian Civil Service

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Indian Civil Service (ICS) | Elite higher civil service of British India, often called the “steel frame” of administration |
| Covenanted Civil Service | Higher service whose members entered a covenant with the Company/Crown; essentially the ICS elite stream |
| Uncovenanted Civil Service | Lower/provincial services without the same covenanted elite status |
| Haileybury College | Training institution in England where Company civil servants were trained before the open-competition era fully displaced patronage pathways |
| Open competition | Merit examination system for ICS recruitment associated with Charter Act 1853 |

### How It Works

- The ICS formed the administrative backbone through which British policies were executed across districts and provinces.
- Under Company patronage, appointments were heavily controlled by the Court of Directors before competitive recruitment matured.
- Haileybury College trained young civilian recruits in England in languages, law, and administration in the pre-open-competition formation phase.
- The Charter Act of 1853 introduced open competition, transforming the ideological basis of recruitment from patronage to examination merit.
- Competitive examinations were held in Britain for a long period, which practically disadvantaged Indian candidates despite formal eligibility claims.
- The **Macaulay Committee (1854)** on the Indian Civil Service is the companion tag to Charter 1853’s open-competition principle.
- Indians entered the ICS only gradually; **Satyendranath Tagore** is the standard “first Indian ICS” awareness name.
- Covenanted status marked the superior service with better pay, authority, and career ceiling than uncovenanted cadres.
- District administration—collector, magistrate, and related roles—made ICS officers the everyday face of imperial government.
- After 1919, Public Service Commission arrangements further institutionalised recruitment and service control mechanisms.
- Indianisation of services became a major nationalist demand through the twentieth century.
- Continuity into independent India’s higher civil services means ICS history still appears in polity and modern India questions.
- For prelims, lock the sequence: patronage → Haileybury training era → open competition after 1853 → slow Indian entry → PSC institutionalisation after 1919.

> **Exam note:** “Open competition = Charter Act 1853” remains the first filter; Haileybury is the training-era keyword for the earlier patronage-to-college phase.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- ICS was known as the steel frame of British administration in India.
- Covenanted Civil Service referred to the higher ICS stream.
- Haileybury College trained Company civil servants in England.
- Charter Act 1853 introduced open competition for the ICS.
- Early examinations being London-centred limited Indian entry.
- Indianisation of ICS was gradual.
- Uncovenanted services were subordinate to the covenanted elite.
- Public Service Commission provision appears in the 1919 Act framework.
- District collector–magistrate roles exemplified ICS power in the field.
- Satyendranath Tagore is often cited among the earliest Indians in the ICS in standard modern-India teaching.

## 1.9 Separate Electorates

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Separate electorate | System where voters of a community elect representatives of that community from a separate electoral roll |
| Joint electorate | Common electoral roll where all voters vote together, sometimes with reserved seats for communities |
| Communal Award, 1932 | Ramsay MacDonald’s award granting separate electorates to several communities including Depressed Classes |
| Poona Pact, 1932 | Agreement between Gandhi and Ambedkar replacing separate electorates for Depressed Classes with reserved seats in joint electorates |
| Morley–Minto separate electorates | 1909 introduction of Muslim separate electorates as a constitutional principle |

### How It Works

- Separate electorates mean that only members of a specified community vote for candidates of that community, sealing political identity into electoral law.
- The Indian Councils Act of 1909 introduced separate electorates for Muslims, inaugurating communal electorates in British Indian constitutional practice.
- Later reforms extended communal representation devices to additional groups in varying forms, intensifying identity-based politics.
- The Communal Award of 1932 granted separate electorates to Depressed Classes, which Gandhi opposed through a fast.
- The Poona Pact of 1932 replaced Depressed Class separate electorates with reserved seats under a joint electorate arrangement.
- Joint electorates with reserved seats keep a common roll while guaranteeing representation, differing structurally from separate rolls.
- Separate electorates were criticised for fragmenting the nation and rewarding communal bargaining over common citizenship.
- In Constituent Assembly debates, leaders such as Sardar Patel argued strongly against continuing separate electorates in free India’s Constitution.
- The Constitution of India rejected separate electorates and adopted joint electorates, with political safeguards taking other forms such as temporary reserved seats for SC/ST in legislatures.
- Understanding 1909 → Communal Award → Poona Pact → CA rejection is the full arc needed for both polity and modern India questions.
- UPPCS can test the concept through Constitution-making personality/debate questions as much as through 1909 chronology.
- Separate versus joint electorate remains a must-know comparison row for quick elimination in prelims.

> **Exam note:** 1909 starts Muslim separate electorates; Poona Pact blocks Depressed Class separate electorates; Constitution finally rejects the device.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Separate electorates for Muslims were introduced by the Indian Councils Act, 1909.
- Morley–Minto Reforms are synonymous with this communal electorate landmark.
- Communal Award was announced by Ramsay MacDonald in 1932.
- Poona Pact was signed in 1932 between Gandhi and Ambedkar.
- Poona Pact provided reserved seats for Depressed Classes within joint electorates.
- Separate electorates were not adopted by the Constitution of India.
- Constituent Assembly rejected separate electorates after intense debate.
- Sardar Patel was a leading voice against separate electorates in the CA.
- Joint electorate means a common electoral roll.
- Communal electorates are treated as a major cause of political communalisation in standard historiography.

## 1.10 Simon Commission & Nehru Report

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Simon Commission** | All-British statutory commission (1927) under Sir John Simon to review the working of the GOI Act 1919 |
| **Nehru Report** | 1928 constitutional proposals of an all-parties committee chaired by Motilal Nehru |
| **Jinnah’s Fourteen Points** | 1929 Muslim League counter-demands rejecting the Nehru Report’s unitary bias |
| **Boycott of Simon** | Congress–League–public protest slogan “Simon Go Back” against an all-white commission |

#### Causes

1. The GOI Act 1919 required a statutory review after ten years; the British advanced the review to **1927**.
2. No Indian was appointed to the Commission, which nationalists read as a racial insult and denial of self-determination.
3. Congress and large sections of public opinion wanted Indians themselves to draft India’s constitutional future.
4. Rival communal and princely stakes made any purely British review politically explosive.
5. Failure of Dyarchy had already discredited the 1919 settlement and created pressure for a new framework.

#### Course — Step by Step

1. **November 1927:** Simon Commission is announced with seven British members and **zero Indians**.
2. Almost all major Indian parties resolve to **boycott** the Commission.
3. Nationwide protests erupt; Lala Lajpat Rai is injured in a Lahore lathi charge (later dies, 1928) — a memory tag for the boycott phase.
4. An all-parties committee under **Motilal Nehru** drafts an alternative constitution — the **Nehru Report (1928)**.
5. The Report demands **Dominion Status**, a parliamentary system, fundamental rights, and joint electorates with reserved seats for minorities.
6. **Jinnah** rejects the Report and issues **Fourteen Points (1929)** seeking stronger Muslim safeguards and federal/provincial guarantees.
7. Simon Report (1930) later feeds into Round Table Conferences and the path to the **GOI Act 1935**.
8. The episode shifts politics from petitioning British drafts to Indian counter-drafts and communal bargaining.

### How It Works

- The Simon Commission’s core exam identity is **all-British composition**, not its later report text.
- Advancing the review to 1927 was meant to undercut Labour’s expected softer line; Indians still saw exclusion as decisive.
- “Simon Go Back” is the mass-politics tag; constitutional substance sits in the Nehru Report reply.
- The Nehru Report proposed **Dominion Status** (not immediate Purna Swaraj — that comes via Lahore 1929 Congress).
- It favoured **joint electorates** with reserved seats, which clashed with League separate-electorate politics.
- Linguistic provinces, responsible government at Centre and provinces, and a bill of rights appear in standard Nehru Report summaries.
- Jinnah’s Fourteen Points demanded residual powers with provinces, Muslim representation guarantees, and separate electorates — the communal counter-pole.
- Simon’s findings and RTCs together shape the **1935 Act**, so Simon is a bridge event, not an end-state constitution.
- Chronology trap with Sharda Act (1929) and Dandi (1930) is repeatedly tested (UPPCS 2021 Q28).
- Death of Ferozshah Mehta (1915) → Jallianwala (1919) → Simon (1927) → Dandi (1930) is another UPPCS chronology set (2019 Q20).

> **Exam note:** Simon = **1927 + all-British + boycott**. Nehru Report = **1928 + Motilal Nehru + Dominion Status draft**. Do not swap years with Communal Award 1932.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Simon Commission appointed in **1927** (arrived India 1928).
- Chairman: **Sir John Simon**; members: all British.
- Indian response: boycott; slogan **Simon Go Back**.
- Nehru Report: **1928**; chairman **Motilal Nehru**; secretary often tagged as Jawaharlal Nehru in committee work awareness.

- Jinnah’s Fourteen Points: **1929**.

- Trap: Nehru Report is **not** drafted by the Simon Commission.
- Trap: “Simon Commission had Indian members” = FALSE.

## 1.11 Communal Award & Poona Pact

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Communal Award** | Ramsay MacDonald’s **16 August 1932** award granting separate electorates to several communities including Depressed Classes |
| **Poona Pact** | **24 September 1932** agreement between Gandhi and Ambedkar replacing Depressed Class separate electorates with reserved seats in **joint electorates** |
| **Depressed Classes** | Colonial-era label for communities later called Scheduled Castes in constitutional usage |

#### Causes

1. Round Table bargaining left minority representation unresolved; Britain imposed an award.
2. Ramsay MacDonald sought to settle communal seats including Muslims, Sikhs, Europeans, and Depressed Classes.
3. Gandhi opposed separate electorates for Depressed Classes as a permanent split within Hindu society.
4. Ambedkar initially supported separate electorates as a safeguard for Depressed Class political power.
5. Gandhi’s fast unto death in Yerwada Jail forced urgent Hindu–Depressed Class negotiation.

#### Course — Step by Step

1. **16 August 1932:** Communal Award announced — separate electorates for Depressed Classes among other groups.
2. Gandhi begins a fast against Depressed Class separate electorates.
3. Hindu leaders and Ambedkar negotiate under intense public pressure.
4. **24 September 1932:** Poona Pact signed — reserved seats for Depressed Classes within **joint electorates**.
5. Number of reserved seats for Depressed Classes rises sharply under the Pact — standard teaching lock: Communal Award separate seats **71** → Poona Pact reserved seats **148** in joint electorates (exact textbook figure used in many UPSC notes).
6. Separate electorates for Depressed Classes are dropped; other Award communities’ arrangements largely continue in the 1935 framework.
7. The Pact becomes a landmark in Dalit political history and constitutional representation debates.
8. Exam trap year **1933** for Communal Award is planted in UPPCS matching (2019 Q19).

### How It Works

- Communal Award is a **British fiat**; Poona Pact is an **Indian bilateral settlement** modifying only the Depressed Classes part.
- Separate electorate = separate roll; Poona Pact switches Depressed Classes to **joint roll + reserved seats**.
- Gandhi’s moral pressure and Ambedkar’s hard bargaining are both part of the standard narrative — do not erase either side.
- Poona Pact year is **1932**, same year as the Award — not 1933.
- Chronology with Gandhi–Irwin Pact (Mar 1931), Second RTC (1931), Poona Pact (Sep 1932), end of CDM (1934) is a 2025 ask.
- The Pact feeds into GOI Act 1935 seat arrangements for Depressed Classes in modified form.
- Constituent Assembly later rejects separate electorates entirely for free India.
- Never confuse Poona Pact with Gandhi–Irwin Pact (Irwin = 1931, salt/CDM truce).

> **Exam note:** Award **16 Aug 1932**; Pact **24 Sep 1932**. “Communal Award 1933” = classic wrong match.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Communal Award: **Ramsay MacDonald**, **16 August 1932**.
- Poona Pact: **24 September 1932**, Gandhi–Ambedkar.
- Poona Pact = reserved seats + **joint electorates** (not separate); seat count tag **148** vs Award’s **71**.
- Award also covered Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans (standard list awareness).
- Yerwada fast is the pressure event behind the Pact.
- UPPCS 2019: Communal Award year **1933** is **incorrect**.
- Do not swap Poona Pact with Lucknow Pact (1916) or Gandhi–Irwin Pact (1931).
- Depressed Classes separate electorates die with Poona Pact; Muslim separate electorates continue until Constitution.

## 1.12 Round Table Conferences

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Round Table Conferences (RTC)** | Three London conferences (1930–32) to discuss India’s constitutional future after Simon Report |
| **First RTC** | **Nov 1930 – Jan 1931**; Congress absent (in jail/CDM phase) |
| **Second RTC** | **Sep–Dec 1931**; Gandhi attends as sole Congress representative after Gandhi–Irwin Pact |
| **Third RTC** | **Nov–Dec 1932**; Congress again largely absent; smaller attendance |

### Round Table Conferences — How It Works

- RTCs were Britain’s forum to convert Simon findings and Indian demands into a constitutional deal including princes and minorities.
- **First RTC (1930–31):** Congress boycotts; Liberals, League, princes attend; discussion without the largest mass party.
- **Gandhi–Irwin Pact (March 1931)** enables Congress participation in the Second RTC.
- **Second RTC (1931):** Gandhi represents Congress; deadlock on minority representation and federation design.
- Leaders such as **Sarojini Naidu**, **Madan Mohan Malaviya** attend Second RTC; **Rajendra Prasad did NOT** (UPPCS 2020 Q24).
- **Third RTC (1932):** weak Congress presence; British move toward White Paper → Joint Select Committee → **GOI Act 1935**.
- Communal Award (Aug 1932) falls between Second and Third RTC politically and chronologically.
- Princes’ role in federation talks foreshadows the 1935 All-India Federation clause (never implemented).
- RTC is not a single event — always identify **which** conference the stem means.
- Chronology lock: 1st RTC → Gandhi–Irwin → 2nd RTC → Communal Award/Poona Pact → 3rd RTC.

> **Exam note:** Second RTC = Gandhi present. Who did **NOT** go is the UPPCS trap (Rajendra Prasad).

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Three RTCs: **1930–31, 1931, 1932**.
- Congress absent in First RTC; Gandhi in Second; Third weakened.
- Gandhi–Irwin Pact bridges CDM and Second RTC.
- Outcome pathway: White Paper (1933) → GOI Act 1935.
- UPPCS 2020: Rajendra Prasad did **not** attend Second RTC.
- B.R. Ambedkar attended RTCs representing Depressed Classes (awareness tag).
- Do not confuse RTC with Cabinet Mission talks (1946).

## 1.13 August Offer, Cripps Mission & Wavell Plan

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **August Offer** | **8 August 1940** Linlithgow offer: Dominion Status objective, postwar body to frame constitution, expanded Viceroy’s Council |
| **Cripps Mission** | **March 1942** Stafford Cripps mission offering Dominion Status after war with right of provinces to secede from the union |
| **Wavell Plan** | **June 1945** plan for interim executive reconstruction; discussed at **Shimla Conference** |
| **Shimla Conference** | **June–July 1945** talks under Wavell that failed mainly on League’s claim to nominate all Muslim members |

#### Causes

1. World War II and Congress resignation of provincial ministries (1939) created a political vacuum Britain needed to manage.
2. August Offer tried to secure Indian cooperation without transferring real power during the war.
3. Japanese threat in 1942 forced a fuller British constitutional offer — the Cripps Mission.
4. Post-war pressure and Labour politics pushed Wavell to attempt an interim settlement in 1945.
5. Congress–League deadlock on parity and nominations blocked every intermediate plan.

#### Course — Step by Step

1. **Oct–Nov 1939:** Congress ministries resign over India being dragged into WWII without consent.
2. **March 1940:** Ramgarh Congress session (chronology tag with August Offer).
3. **8 August 1940:** August Offer announced; Congress and League reject it for opposite reasons.
4. **March 1942:** Cripps arrives; offers postwar Dominion Status + constitution-making body + provincial non-accession option.
5. Congress rejects Cripps (immediate national government absent; secession clause disliked); League also unsatisfied.
6. **June 1945:** Wavell Plan proposes balanced interim Executive Council with equal caste-Hindu and Muslim representation framing.
7. **Shimla Conference (1945)** collapses when League insists it alone must nominate all Muslim members.
8. Failure of Wavell/Shimla leads toward Cabinet Mission 1946.

### How It Works

- August Offer is **Linlithgow 1940** — not Cripps and not Wavell.
- Cripps’s distinctive trap is **Dominion Status after war** plus **right to secede/non-accede** for provinces — Congress called it a post-dated cheque.
- “Quit India” (Aug 1942) follows Cripps failure — neighbouring modern-India tag.
- Wavell Plan and Shimla Conference are paired: Plan = proposal; Shimla = negotiation venue.
- Chronology 2025 Q41: Cripps **1942** → Wavell Plan **1945** → Shimla **1945** → Cabinet Mission **1946**.
- Chronology 2024 Q133: Congress ministry resignations **1939** → Ramgarh **1940** → August Offer **1940** → Cripps **1942**.
- Never place Cabinet Mission before Cripps/Wavell.
- Individual responsibility vs parity fights at Shimla preview Partition politics.

## 1.14 Cabinet Mission & Interim Government

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Cabinet Mission** | **1946** British mission (Pethick-Lawrence, Cripps, Alexander) to plan transfer of power and Constituent Assembly |

| **Interim Government** | Provisional central government (announced **24 August 1946**; assumes office **2 September 1946**) under the Viceroy |

#### Causes

1. Failure of Cripps and Wavell left Britain needing a final transfer framework after WWII.
2. Labour government under Attlee sought negotiated exit rather than indefinite repression.
3. Congress wanted a strong centre; League wanted Pakistan — Mission tried a compromise union with groupings.
4. Administrative collapse risks (RIN revolt, mass politics) forced speed.
5. Princely states and provincial diversity required a CA route rather than a London-imposed Act alone.

#### Course — Step by Step

1. **January 1946:** UK Parliamentary Delegation visits India (UPPCS 2024 chronology start).
2. **18–23 February 1946:** Royal Indian Navy Revolt.

7. **24 August 1946:** Interim National Government announced; **2 September 1946:** Nehru-led Interim Government takes office.

### How It Works

- Cabinet Mission proposes a **three-tier** structure: Union → Groups (A/B/C) → Provinces.

- Constituent Assembly is elected indirectly by provincial assemblies under the Mission’s scheme.

- Interim Government announcement date **24 August 1946** is a direct UPPCS ask (2022 Q79).
- Chronology 2024 Q16: Delegation → RIN → Cabinet Mission announcement → Interim formation.
- Mountbatten later abandons the Mission’s united-India hope for Partition.

## 1.15 Mountbatten Plan & Indian Independence Act, 1947

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Mountbatten Plan / 3 June Plan** | **3 June 1947** plan accepting Partition, two Dominions, and rapid transfer of power |
| **Indian Independence Act, 1947** | British Parliament Act (**18 July 1947**) creating two Dominions — India and Pakistan — from **15 August 1947** |
| **Radcliffe Boundary** | Boundary commissions’ lines for Punjab and Bengal (awarded mid-August 1947) |

#### Causes

1. Cabinet Mission’s united India collapses under Congress–League deadlock and communal violence.
2. Britain under Attlee sets an early exit deadline; Mountbatten accelerates transfer.
3. League’s Pakistan demand becomes non-negotiable after 1946–47 bloodshed.
4. Princely states’ future needs a legal frame of lapse of paramountcy and accession.
5. Administrative need for a clean statutory end to British rule via Parliament’s Act.

#### Course — Step by Step

1. Mountbatten arrives as last Viceroy (**March 1947**) with a brief to transfer power quickly.
2. Negotiations with Nehru, Patel, Jinnah, and others concede Partition as the workable exit.
3. **3 June 1947:** Plan announced — Partition, referendums/votes in specified areas, two Dominions.
4. Congress and League accept the Plan under duress of time and violence.
5. **18 July 1947:** Indian Independence Act receives Royal Assent.
6. Act provides for two Dominions, lapse of British paramountcy over princely states, and CA empowerment to frame constitutions.
7. **15 August 1947:** Transfer of power; India and Pakistan become Dominions.
8. GOI Act 1935 (adapted) continues as provisional constitutional framework until **26 January 1950**.

### How It Works

- 3 June Plan is the political deal; Independence Act is the **legal instrument** passed by the British Parliament.
- Independence Act does **not** itself enact the Constitution of India — it creates Dominions and empowers CAs to frame constitutions.
- Key statutory locks: two **independent Dominions** from **15 August 1947**; British **paramountcy lapses** over princely states; **Secretary of State for India abolished**; Viceroy/Governors become **constitutional (nominal) heads**; Constituent Assemblies get **dual powers** (constituent + legislative) as sovereign Dominion legislatures; till new constitutions commence, **GOI Act 1935** (adapted) continues.
- Princely states may accede to India or Pakistan (integration politics follow after lapse of paramountcy).
- Mountbatten becomes first Governor-General of Dominion of India; Jinnah becomes GG of Pakistan; later **C. Rajagopalachari** is the first Indian GG of India.
- Boundary awards (Radcliffe) come late and fuel Partition migration violence — neighbouring history tag.
- From 15 Aug 1947 to 26 Jan 1950, India is a Dominion with CA also acting as legislature.
- Exam trap: Independence Act date **18 July 1947** vs independence **15 August 1947** vs Constitution enforcement **26 Jan 1950**.

> **Exam note:** **3 June** = Plan; **18 July** = Act; **15 August** = Dominion Day; **26 January 1950** = Republic. SoS abolished; paramountcy lapses; CA gets dual powers.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Mountbatten Plan: **3 June 1947**.
- Indian Independence Act: **18 July 1947**.
- Two Dominions from **15 August 1947**.
- Act passed by British Parliament.
- Secretary of State for India **abolished**.

## 1.16 Constituent Assembly — Composition & Constitution Making Process

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Constituent Assembly (CA)** | Body that framed the Constitution of India; first met **9 December 1946** |
| **Cabinet Mission scheme (CA election)** | Indirect election by provincial assemblies; seats by population and community proportions |
| **Constitution making process** | Objectives Resolution → committees → drafting → readings → adoption → enforcement |

### How It Works

- CA is constituted under the **Cabinet Mission Plan 1946**, not under the Independence Act alone (Act later empowers Dominions’ CAs).
- Total strength originally **389** = **296** from British India + **93** from princely states (standard Laxmikanth split); after Partition, membership falls to **299**.
- Seats allotted roughly in proportion to population; communities got representation under Mission communal proportions; election by provincial assemblies used **proportional representation by single transferable vote**.
- Princely states’ representatives were **nominated**, not elected by provincial assemblies.
- Muslim League initially boycotts; early sessions run largely without League.
- First meeting **9 Dec 1946**; temporary President **Dr Sachchidananda Sinha** (oldest member convention); permanent President **Dr Rajendra Prasad** (11 Dec 1946).
- About **15 women** members served in the CA (awareness figure).
- CA also functions as Dominion Legislature after 15 Aug 1947 until the new Parliament forms (**dual functions**).
- Work method: Objectives Resolution sets philosophy; specialised committees report; Drafting Committee prepares text; Assembly debates clause by clause through three readings.
- Roughly **2 years 11 months 18 days** from first sitting to adoption; **11 sessions**; **165 days** of actual sitting (standard Laxmikanth duration tags).
- Constitution adopted **26 Nov 1949**; enforced **26 Jan 1950**; some provisions operate from adoption date.
- National Flag adopted by CA on **22 July 1947** (UPPCS 2020 Q134).
- On **24 January 1950** (last meeting): **Jana Gana Mana** adopted as National Anthem; **Vande Mataram** given National Song status; Rajendra Prasad elected first President.
- Elephant was the **seal** of the Constituent Assembly (optional GK tag).

> **Exam note:** First met **9 Dec 1946**; Flag **22 Jul 1947**; Adopted **26 Nov 1949**; Last meet + Anthem **24 Jan 1950**; Enforced **26 Jan 1950**. Strength **389 → 299**.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- CA first meeting: **9 December 1946**.
- Temporary President: Sachchidananda Sinha; President: Rajendra Prasad.
- Vice-President often tagged: **H.C. Mukherjee**.
- Original strength **389** (296 + 93); after Partition **299**.
- Election method: indirect; PR-STV by provincial assemblies.
- Duration ≈ **2 yr 11 mo 18 days**; ~**11 sessions**; ~**165** sitting days.
- About **15** women members.
- Flag: **22 July 1947**.
- National Anthem + National Song decisions: **24 Jan 1950**.
- Last meeting: **24 Jan 1950**.
- Constitutional Adviser: **B.N. Rau**.
- First Law Minister / Drafting Chair: **B.R. Ambedkar**.
- CA seal: Elephant.

## 1.17 Committees, Objectives Resolution, B.N. Rau, Drafting Committee & Timeline

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Objectives Resolution** | Moved by **Nehru on 13 Dec 1946**; adopted **22 Jan 1947**; philosophical base of the Preamble |
| **B.N. Rau** | Constitutional Adviser to the CA; prepared the initial draft |
| **Drafting Committee** | Appointed **29 August 1947**; Chairman **Dr B.R. Ambedkar**; seven members |
| **Union Constitution Committee** | Chaired by **Jawaharlal Nehru** |
| **Rules of Procedure Committee** | Chaired by **Dr Rajendra Prasad** |
| **Fundamental Rights Sub-Committee** | Chaired by **J.B. Kripalani** (UPPCS 2025 match) |

### How It Works

- Objectives Resolution declares India as an independent sovereign republic and lists justice, liberty, equality, and minority safeguards — later distilled into the **Preamble**.
- **B.N. Rau** tours and studies constitutions, advises the Assembly, and prepares a working draft before the Drafting Committee’s detailed revision.
- Drafting Committee members (standard seven): Ambedkar (Chair), N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar, K.M. Munshi, Syed Mohammad Saadulla, B.L. Mitter (later N. Madhava Rau), D.P. Khaitan (later T.T. Krishnamachari).
- Ambedkar is remembered as **Chief Architect** for piloting the draft through debates — not as sole author in isolation from Rau and committees.
- Major committees (raata set): Union Powers (**Nehru**), Union Constitution (**Nehru**), Provincial Constitution (**Sardar Patel** — not Azad), Drafting (**Ambedkar**), Rules (**Rajendra Prasad**), Steering (**Rajendra Prasad**), Advisory Committee on FR/Minorities/Tribal Areas (**Sardar Patel**), with FR Sub-Committee (**Kripalani**) and Minorities Sub-Committee (**H.C. Mukherjee**).
- UPPCS 2025 Q82 match lock: Union Constitution → **Nehru**; Rules of Procedure → **Rajendra Prasad**; Drafting → **Ambedkar**; FR Sub-Committee → **Kripalani**.
- Timeline lock (2023 Q3): CA first met → Drafting Committee appointed → Constitution adopted → Constitution commenced.
- Draft published **21 Feb 1948**; people/comments phase; final reading late 1949.

> **Exam note:** Drafting Committee date **29 Aug 1947**. Do not assign Drafting chair to Nehru or Rajendra Prasad.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Objectives Resolution: Nehru, **13 Dec 1946**; adopted **22 Jan 1947**.
- B.N. Rau = Constitutional Adviser (not Drafting Chairman).
- Drafting Committee: **29 Aug 1947**; Ambedkar Chair; **7** members.
- Union Constitution Committee: **Nehru**.
- Rules of Procedure Committee: **Rajendra Prasad**.
- FR Sub-Committee: **J.B. Kripalani**.
- Advisory Committee on FR/Minorities/Tribal Areas: **Sardar Patel**.
- Provincial Constitution Committee: **Sardar Patel** (not Maulana Azad).
- Minorities Sub-Committee: **H.C. Mukherjee**; Excluded Areas Sub-Committee: **A.V. Thakkar**.

## 1.18 Sources of Constitution & Borrowed Features

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Sources of Constitution** | Historical Acts, foreign constitutions, CA debates, and Indian political experience feeding the text |
| **Borrowed features** | Specific institutional ideas consciously taken from other constitutions (Laxmikanth match lists) |
| **GOI Act 1935 as source** | Largest single structural donor — federal scheme, offices, emergency lineage, PSC, Federal Court |

### How It Works

- The Constitution is not a photocopy of one country; it is a **selective synthesis** plus original Indian choices (universal adult franchise, FR–DPSP balance, independent judiciary with review).
- **GOI Act 1935** contributes federal lists pattern, office of Governor, emergency concepts lineage, bicameralism experience, and administrative continuity.
- **UK** contributes parliamentary executive, rule of law culture, cabinet system, single citizenship, law-making procedures, and prerogative writs tradition.
- **USA** contributes written FR, independent judiciary, judicial review, impeachment, and Vice-President office ideas.
- **Ireland** contributes DPSP, Rajya Sabha nomination method, and Presidential election method ideas.
- **Canada** contributes federation with a **strong Centre** and **residuary powers with the Centre**.
- **Australia** contributes **Concurrent List**, freedom of trade/commerce, and joint sitting idea.
- **Weimar Germany** contributes suspension of Fundamental Rights during Emergency.
- **Japan** contributes “procedure established by law” wording originally associated with Article 21.
- **USSR** awareness tags: Fundamental Duties (later 42nd Amendment) and justice ideals in Preamble teaching.
- Classic UPPCS trap: Residual powers matched to **Australia** (wrong) instead of **Canada** (2021 Q54).
- Ambedkar’s defence: borrowing useful institutions is normal; the test is fitness for Indian needs.

> **Exam note:** **Residuary → Canada**. **Concurrent List → Australia**. **DPSP → Ireland**. **FR → USA**. Never swap Canada/Australia.

### Borrowed Features Master Table

| Feature | Source |
|---------|--------|
| Parliamentary government, Rule of Law, Cabinet, single citizenship, bicameralism, prerogative writs | **UK** |
| Fundamental Rights, Judicial Review, Independent judiciary, Impeachment, Vice-President | **USA** |
| Directive Principles, RS nomination, President election method | **Ireland** |
| Strong-Centre federation, Residuary powers with Centre, Governor appointment idea | **Canada** |
| Concurrent List, Freedom of trade/commerce, Joint sitting | **Australia** |
| Suspension of FR during Emergency | **Germany (Weimar)** |
| Procedure established by law | **Japan** |
| Republic; Liberty–Equality–Fraternity ideals | **France** |
| Fundamental Duties; Preamble justice ideals (teaching tags) | **USSR / socialist thought** |
| Amendment procedure ideas / RS election elements (teaching tags) | **South Africa** |
| Federal scheme, lists lineage, emergency structure lineage, PSC, ordinance habits | **GOI Act 1935** |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Residual powers: **Canada** (Centre), not Australia, not USA states.
- Concurrent List: **Australia**.
- DPSP: **Ireland**.
- FR + Judicial Review: **USA**.
- Parliamentary system: **UK**.
- Emergency FR suspension: **Germany**.
- Procedure established by law: **Japan**.
- Largest structural source: **GOI Act 1935**.
- UPPCS 2021 Q54 wrong pair: Residual → Australia.

## 1.19 Adoption, Enforcement, First President & First Prime Minister

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Adoption of Constitution** | CA passes/enacts the Constitution on **26 November 1949** (Constitution Day) |
| **Enforcement / Commencement** | Constitution comes into force on **26 January 1950** (Republic Day) |
| **First President** | **Dr Rajendra Prasad** — first President of the Republic of India |
| **First Prime Minister** | **Jawaharlal Nehru** — first PM (continues from Interim/Dominion leadership into the Republic) |

### How It Works

- **26 November 1949:** Constitution is adopted and enacted; some articles (citizenship, elections, provisional Parliament provisions) commence at once.
- **26 January 1950:** Full commencement; India becomes a **Republic**; colonial Dominion status ends.
- Date **26 January** deliberately recalls **Purna Swaraj Day 1930** (Lahore Congress resolution observance).
- **Dr Rajendra Prasad** is elected first President; takes oath on 26 Jan 1950.
- **Jawaharlal Nehru** is the first Prime Minister of the Republic (already heading the Dominion government).
- Last CA meeting **24 Jan 1950** elects Prasad as President under the new Constitution’s transitional arrangements.
- Do not confuse CA President (Rajendra Prasad from Dec 1946) with President of India (from 26 Jan 1950) — same person, different offices/phases.
- Adoption ≠ Enforcement: classic two-date trap pair.
- Constitution Day (Samvidhan Diwas) marks **26 November**, not 26 January.

> **Exam note:** Adopted **26 Nov 1949**; Enforced **26 Jan 1950**; Last CA meet **24 Jan 1950**. First President **Rajendra Prasad**; First PM **Nehru**.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Adoption: **26 November 1949**.
- Enforcement: **26 January 1950**.
- Last CA meeting: **24 January 1950**.
- First President: **Dr Rajendra Prasad**.
- First Prime Minister: **Jawaharlal Nehru**.
- 26 January links to **Purna Swaraj 1930**.
- Some provisions operate from 26 Nov 1949 itself.
- Trap: “Constitution came into force on 15 August 1947” = FALSE.
- Trap: “Constitution adopted on 26 January 1950” = FALSE.

1. **(UPPCS Prelims 2023, Q3)** — see §1.17 (adopted vs commencement order).

2. **(UPPCS Prelims 2018, Q147)** — last CA meeting **24 Jan 1950** (bridge to Presidential election eve).

## Common Traps — Don't Fall For These

1. **Dyarchy vs Provincial Autonomy** — Dyarchy = **1919 provinces**; Autonomy = **1935 provinces**. 1935 Dyarchy at Centre **never operated**.
2. **GG of Bengal vs GG of India** — Bengal title till **1833**; “of India” from Charter **1833**.
3. **Open competition ICS** — Lock **1853**, not 1833 (only proposed earlier).
4. **Communal Award year** — **1932**, never 1933 (UPPCS 2019).
5. **Residuary powers source** — **Canada**, not Australia (UPPCS 2021).
6. **Concurrent List source** — **Australia**, not Canada.
7. **Adoption vs Enforcement** — Adopted **26 Nov 1949**; enforced **26 Jan 1950**.
8. **Last CA meeting** — **24 Jan 1950**, not 26 Nov 1949 (UPPCS 2018).
9. **National Flag by CA** — **22 July 1947**, not 1950 dates (UPPCS 2020).
10. **Interim announcement** — **24 Aug 1946**, not 2 Sep (office date) (UPPCS 2022).
11. **Second RTC absentee** — **Rajendra Prasad** did not go; Gandhi did (UPPCS 2020).
12. **Chamber of Princes** — **GOI 1919**, not 1909/1853 (UPPCS 2023).
13. **B.N. Rau ≠ Ambedkar** — Rau = Adviser; Ambedkar = Drafting Chair.
14. **Simon all-British** — Zero Indians; boycott is the identity.
15. **Cripps before Wavell before Cabinet Mission** — 1942 → 1945 → 1946 (UPPCS 2025).
16. **League A/R 1946** — Withdrawal from Plan and joining Interim are both true but **R does not explain A** (UPPCS 2018).
17. **Federation 1935** — Provided for, **never implemented**.
18. **Separate electorates start** — **1909 Muslims**, not 1932 as “first”.
19. **Poona Pact mechanism** — Reserved seats + **joint** electorates (not separate).
20. **15 Aug 1947 ≠ Republic Day** — Dominion transfer ≠ Constitution enforcement.
21. **Provincial Constitution Committee = Patel**, not Azad.
22. **Residuary under 1935 = Viceroy**; Concurrent List counts **59/54/36**.
23. **Poona Pact seats** — **148** reserved (joint), not the Award’s **71** separate seats.
24. **National Anthem decision = 24 Jan 1950** (same day as last CA meeting), not 26 Jan.
25. **Queen’s Proclamation 1858** accompanies GOI Act 1858 — do not skip it in Crown-rule questions.
26. **Independence Act abolishes Secretary of State** and gives CA dual powers — not merely “India free”.
27. **B.N. Rau ≠ Drafting Chair**; Ambedkar chairs Drafting; Rau is Constitutional Adviser.
28. **Federal Court year = 1937** (under 1935 Act), not 1935 itself as inauguration year.
