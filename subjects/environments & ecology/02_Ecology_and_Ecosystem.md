## 2.1 Ecology – Meaning

### Definitions (learn all — exams pick different ones)

| Source | Definition |
|--------|------------|
| **General** | Branch of biology studying interactions among organisms and between organisms and their physical environment |
| **NCERT (Class 12)** | Study of the relationships of living organisms with each other and with their environment ← **Most asked** |
| **Etymology** | Greek *Oikos* (house/home) + *Logos* (study) = "study of the household of nature" |
| **Ernst Haeckel (1866)** | Coined "Ecology" — study of organisms in relation to their environment |

### Ecology — How It Works

Ecology is the **scientific lens** through which we understand why organisms live where they do, how they interact, and what happens when those interactions are disrupted. Unlike "environment studies" (which includes policy, law, and economics), ecology is fundamentally a **biological science** grounded in observation, experimentation, and mathematical models. It operates at **seven hierarchical levels** — from a single organism's physiology to global biogeochemical cycles — and each level has its own questions, methods, and exam traps.

At the **organism level**, ecology asks how an individual tolerates temperature, moisture, and predators. At **population level**, it tracks birth/death rates and growth curves. **Community ecology** studies species interactions (competition, predation, mutualism). **Ecosystem ecology** (Odum's focus) integrates energy flow and nutrient cycling. **Landscape, biome, and biosphere** levels scale up to regional and planetary processes. Exams frequently test whether students confuse these levels — "organ" is anatomy, not an ecological level.

**Autoecology** studies one species in its environment; **synecology** studies communities and ecosystems. India honours **Ramdeo Misra** as the father of ecology in India (first PhD in ecology, established ecology teaching at BHU). **Eugene P. Odum** (*Fundamentals of Ecology*, 1953) is called the father of modern ecosystem ecology. **Ernst Haeckel** coined the term in **1866**.

> **Exam note:** Ecology = **study** (पारिस्थितिकी); Ecosystem = **functional unit**; Environment = **surroundings**. UPPCS loves this three-way distinction.

### Levels of Ecological Organisation

| Level | Definition | Example |
|-------|------------|---------|
| **1. Organism** | Individual and its adaptations | Camel's water conservation in desert |
| **2. Population** | Same species, same area, interbreeding | Tiger population in Dudhwa TR |
| **3. Community** | All populations of different species | Fish + birds + plants in a wetland |
| **4. Ecosystem** | Community + abiotic environment + interactions | Ganga river stretch at Varanasi |
| **5. Landscape** | Mosaic of ecosystems | Terai forest + grassland + agricultural fields |
| **6. Biome** | Large climate-defined region | Tropical deciduous forest of India |
| **7. Biosphere** | Global sum of all ecosystems | Earth's life-supporting zone |

| Branch | Focus |
|--------|-------|
| **Autoecology** | Single species–environment relationship |
| **Synecology** | Communities and ecosystems |
| **Population Ecology** | Population dynamics, growth, regulation |

## 2.2 Ecosystem

### Definitions (learn all — exams pick different ones)

| Source | Definition |
|--------|------------|
| **A.G. Tansley (1935)** | Basic functional unit of ecology where living organisms interact with each other and with their physical environment |
| **NCERT** | Functional unit of nature where living organisms interact among themselves and with the surrounding physical environment |
| **Odum** | Any unit that includes all organisms in a given area interacting with the physical environment, producing and transferring energy |

### Ecosystem — How It Works

An ecosystem is not just a collection of species — it is a **functional system** with energy input, nutrient cycling, and biological regulation. **Solar energy** enters through producers (green plants, algae, cyanobacteria), flows through consumers, and exits as **heat** at each trophic transfer. **Nutrients** (carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus) cycle within the system — unlike energy, matter is recycled through decomposers. This dual flow — **unidirectional energy, cyclic nutrients** — is the defining functional characteristic exams test repeatedly.

Ecosystems vary enormously in size: a **pond** is an ecosystem, but so is a **forest**, a **river**, or the entire **biosphere**. They can be **natural** (forest, lake, ocean) or **anthropogenic/artificial** (crop field, aquarium, sewage treatment wetland). They are generally **open systems** — exchanging energy and matter with surroundings — though some (aquarium, bottle ecosystem) approximate closed systems for short periods.

**Tansley (1935)** introduced "ecosystem" to emphasise that organisms and their physical environment form an **integrated whole**, not separable parts. Before this, ecologists studied communities (biotic) and habitats (abiotic) separately. The ecosystem concept unified ecology and made energy-flow studies possible (leading to Lindeman's 10% rule in 1942).

> **Exam note:** Ecosystem = **functional unit** (not just "place" — that's habitat). Must include biotic + abiotic + interactions + energy flow.

### Types by Origin and Size

| Classification | Types | Example |
|----------------|-------|---------|
| **Natural** | Forest, grassland, desert, lake, ocean | Dudhwa forest ecosystem |
| **Anthropogenic** | Cropland, plantation, aquaculture pond | Sugarcane fields of western UP |
| **Micro** | Pond, rotting log, cow rumen | Garden pond in Lucknow |
| **Macro** | River basin, ocean, biome | Ganga basin ecosystem |
| **Open system** | Most natural ecosystems | River, forest |
| **Closed system** | Laboratory microcosms | Sealed bottle ecosystem (short-term) |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- **Tansley (1935)** coined "ecosystem"
- Ecosystem = biotic + abiotic + **energy flow** + **nutrient cycling**
- Energy flow = **unidirectional**; nutrients = **cyclic**
- Most ecosystems are **open systems**
- Artificial ecosystems: cropland, aquarium, **sewage treatment plant** (engineered wetland)
- Smallest to largest: micro (pond) → macro (biosphere)

## 2.3 Types of Ecosystems

### Definitions

| Basis | Categories |
|-------|------------|
| **Origin** | Natural vs Anthropogenic (man-made) |
| **Medium** | Terrestrial vs Aquatic |
| **Water flow** | Lentic (standing) vs Lotic (flowing) |
| **Salinity** | Freshwater vs Brackish vs Marine |

### Types of Ecosystems — How It Works

Ecosystems are classified by **where they occur** (land vs water), **how they were formed** (natural vs human-made), and **water characteristics** (standing, flowing, saline). This classification is not academic — it determines which species thrive, which nutrient cycles dominate, and which conservation strategies apply. **Terrestrial ecosystems** cover ~29% of Earth's surface; **aquatic** cover ~71% — yet terrestrial ecosystems are disproportionately tested because human settlements and agriculture are land-based.

**Natural ecosystems** self-regulate through evolved feedback loops (predator–prey balance, nutrient cycling). **Anthropogenic ecosystems** (croplands, cities, aquaculture) depend on human inputs — fertilisers, irrigation, waste removal — and are inherently less stable. Within aquatic systems, **lentic** (lakes, ponds, reservoirs) and **lotic** (rivers, streams) differ fundamentally: lotic systems have directional flow, higher oxygen mixing, and different food webs than still waters.

**Transitional ecosystems** — wetlands, estuaries, mangroves — bridge terrestrial and aquatic. Exams trap students who classify mangroves as "purely terrestrial" (UPPCS 2025 style from Topic 1). These are **coastal/brackish transitional** ecosystems.

> **Exam note:** Mangrove, wetland, estuary = **transitional** — NOT purely terrestrial or purely aquatic.

## 2.4 Terrestrial Ecosystem

### Definitions

| Ecosystem | Climate/Soil | Dominant Vegetation | India Location |
|-----------|--------------|---------------------|----------------|
| **Tropical Rainforest** | Hot, wet (>200 cm rain) | Broad-leaved evergreen trees | Western Ghats, NE India |
| **Tropical Deciduous** | Monsoon (wet-dry) | Seasonal leaf fall | Most of India (UP Gangetic plain fringes) |
| **Temperate Deciduous** | Moderate temp | Oak, maple | Himalaya foothills |
| **Taiga/Boreal** | Cold, long winters | Coniferous (pine, spruce) | Higher Himalaya |
| **Grassland** | Moderate rain | Grasses, few trees | Terai (Dudhwa), Deccan |
| **Savanna** | Seasonal rain | Grass + scattered trees | Gir Forest (Gujarat) |
| **Desert** | Arid (<25 cm rain) | Xerophytes, sparse shrubs | Thar Desert |
| **Tundra** | Extreme cold, permafrost | Moss, lichen, dwarf shrubs | Arctic (not India) |
| **Mountain/Alpine** | Altitude-driven zonation | Changes with elevation | Himalaya altitudinal belts |

### Terrestrial Ecosystem — How It Works

Terrestrial ecosystems are shaped primarily by **climate** (temperature, rainfall, seasonality) and **soil** (edaphic factors). The same latitude can support desert or forest depending on rain — Rajasthan's Thar vs Kerala's rainforest. In India, the **monsoon climate** creates the world's most widespread terrestrial type: **tropical deciduous forest** (teak, sal, bamboo), covering much of the peninsula and Himalayan foothills.

**Grasslands** occur where rainfall is insufficient for full forest but enough for grasses — India's Terai grasslands (Dudhwa, Kishanpur) support deer, rhino, and tiger. **Deserts** feature water conservation adaptations (CAM photosynthesis in cacti, deep roots in keekar). **Taiga/tundra** concepts appear in UPSC questions about **permafrost** — found in tundra and taiga, NOT savannah (UPSC 2013 trap).

In **Uttar Pradesh**, terrestrial ecosystems are dominated by **Gangetic plain agriculture** (anthropogenic), **Terai-Duar sal forests** (north), **Vindhyan scrub** (south), and **Bundelkhand dry deciduous** (drought-prone). Forest cover in UP is low (~6%); highest forest districts: **Bahraich, Chandauli, Shravasti** (>20% — ISFR 2023).

> **Exam note:** Permafrost found in **Tundra + Taiga** — NOT savannah. UP's main natural terrestrial ecosystem = Terai sal forest + grassland.

|--------|-----------|-------------|

| Central India | Tropical deciduous | Tiger, leopard, sal forest |

- **Permafrost** = tundra + taiga (UPSC 2013)

## 2.5 Aquatic Ecosystem

### Definitions

| Type | Feature | Examples |
|------|---------|----------|
| **Lentic** | Standing/still water | Lake, pond, reservoir, wetland |
| **Lotic** | Flowing water | River, stream, brook, spring |
| **Wetland** | Shallow, saturated soil, transitional | Marshes, swamps, bogs |
| **Estuary** | River meets sea; brackish water | Sundarbans mouth |
| **Littoral** | Shore zone (marine) | Intertidal pools, sandy beaches |
| **Neritic** | Shallow sea over continental shelf (0–200 m) | Coastal fisheries |
| **Pelagic** | Open ocean water column | Whales, tuna in open sea |
| **Benthic** | Bottom-dwelling zone | Crabs, sea cucumbers on sea floor |
| **Abyssal** | Deep ocean floor (>2000 m) | Deep-sea fish, bioluminescent organisms |

### Aquatic Ecosystem — How It Works

Water is the **dominant abiotic factor** in aquatic ecosystems — it determines light penetration (less in turbid Ganga than clear mountain stream), temperature stability (aquatic heats/cools slower than air), and dissolved oxygen (critical for fish survival). **Lotic** systems (rivers) have **high oxygen** from turbulence but organisms must adapt to **current** — flattened bodies (trout), attached organisms (mussels). **Lentic** systems stratify: warm epilimnion on top, cold hypolimnion below, with a thermocline in between.

**Dissolved oxygen (DO)** is the lifeline of aquatic ecosystems. Pollution (sewage, industrial effluent) raises BOD, depletes DO, and kills fish — a classic cause–effect chain tested in pollution topics but rooted in aquatic ecology. India's major **lotic** systems — Ganga, Yamuna, Godavari — carry enormous biodiversity but face severe degradation in urban stretches (Kanpur, Varanasi, Lucknow's Gomti).

**Marine ecosystems** cover 71% of Earth. The **continental shelf** (neritic zone) is the most productive marine zone — sunlight reaches the bottom, supporting fisheries. **Pelagic** open ocean has lower productivity per unit area but vast volume. **Benthic** organisms recycle nutrients from dead material sinking to the floor.

> **Exam note:** Lentic = **standing** (Lazy lake); Lotic = **flowing** (Lively river). Ganga at Varanasi = lotic. Sur Sarovar Agra = lentic wetland.

### Freshwater vs Marine Comparison

| Feature | Freshwater | Marine |
|---------|------------|--------|
| Salinity | <0.5 ppt | ~35 ppt average |
| Light penetration | Variable (turbidity) | Deeper in open ocean |
| Productivity hotspot | Wetlands, shallow lakes | Continental shelf (neritic) |
| India examples | Ganga, Loktak, Sur Sarovar | Arabian Sea, Bay of Bengal coral reefs |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- **71%** Earth surface = aquatic
- Lentic = standing; Lotic = flowing
- Marine zones: Littoral → Neritic → Pelagic → Benthic → Abyssal
- Wetland = transitional (shallow, saturated)
- Estuary = brackish (mixing zone) — high productivity
- DO critical for aquatic life; pollution reduces DO
- UP: Ganga, Yamuna (lotic); Sur Sarovar, Nawabganj Bird Sanctuary wetlands (lentic)

## 2.6 Structure and Functions of Ecosystem

### Definitions

| Component | Role |
|-----------|------|
| **Structure** | Species composition (biotic) + physical/chemical environment (abiotic) + spatial arrangement |
| **Function** | Energy flow, nutrient cycling, productivity, decomposition, regulation |

### Structure and Functions — How It Works

Every ecosystem has **structure** (what is there) and **function** (what it does). Structure is described by listing **biotic components** (producers, consumers, decomposers) and **abiotic factors** (light, temperature, water, soil, gases). Function is described by four interconnected processes: **(1) Productivity** — how much biomass energy is fixed; **(2) Decomposition** — how dead matter is broken down; **(3) Nutrient cycling** — how elements circulate between living and non-living pools; **(4) Energy flow** — how solar energy moves through trophic levels and exits as heat.

These functions are **interdependent**. High productivity without decomposition would lock nutrients in dead wood (why old-growth forests have slow nutrient cycling). Decomposition without nutrient cycling would waste minerals. Energy flow drives the entire system — without photosynthesis, no consumers or decomposers survive. The **10% rule** (Lindeman) and **1% sunlight capture** quantify energy flow and productivity — tested directly in UPPCS 2025 Q5.

**Stratification** (vertical layering) is a structural feature: forest has canopy, understory, shrub, herb, forest floor — each layer hosts different niches. **Pond stratification**: littoral (shore plants), limnetic (open water), profundal (deep bottom), benthic (sediment).

> **Exam note:** Structure = who + what environment; Function = productivity + decomposition + nutrient cycling + energy flow. All four functions must be named for Mains.

### Four Functional Processes

| Function | Process | Key Organisms | Output |
|----------|---------|---------------|--------|
| **Productivity** | Photosynthesis fixes solar energy into biomass | Green plants, algae | GPP, NPP |
| **Decomposition** | Dead organic matter broken to simple compounds | Bacteria, fungi (decomposers) | CO₂, minerals, humus |
| **Nutrient cycling** | Elements move between biotic and abiotic pools | Decomposers + plants + soil | N, P, C, S cycles |
| **Energy flow** | Solar → producers → consumers → heat | All trophic levels | Unidirectional heat loss |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Structure = biotic composition + abiotic factors + stratification
- Four functions: Productivity, Decomposition, Nutrient cycling, Energy flow
- Energy = unidirectional; Nutrients = cyclic
- Forest stratification: canopy → understory → shrub → herb → floor
- Pond zones: littoral, limnetic, profundal, benthic
- Decomposers critical for nutrient recycling

## 2.7 Biotic and Abiotic Components

### Definitions

| Component | Sub-types | Role |
|-----------|-----------|------|
| **Biotic — Producers** | Plants, algae, cyanobacteria, chemosynthetic bacteria | Autotrophs; fix energy |
| **Biotic — Consumers** | Herbivores (1°), Carnivores (2°/3°), Omnivores | Heterotrophs; transfer energy |
| **Biotic — Decomposers** | Bacteria, fungi | Break dead organic matter to minerals |
| **Biotic — Detritivores** | Earthworm, millipede, woodlice | Ingest dead matter; fragment for decomposers |
| **Abiotic — Climatic** | Light, temperature, rainfall, wind, humidity | Regulate photosynthesis, distribution |
| **Abiotic — Edaphic** | Soil texture, pH, minerals, moisture | Determine plant community |
| **Abiotic — Topographic** | Altitude, slope, aspect | Create microclimates |
| **Abiotic — Inorganic** | Water, gases (O₂, CO₂, N₂), minerals | Raw materials for life |

### Biotic and Abiotic Components — How It Works

The biotic–abiotic divide is ecology's foundational split, but the boundary is not always clean. **Humus** — decomposed organic matter in soil — is abiotic in *location* (it's in soil) but biotic in *origin* (formed from dead organisms). UPPCS 2018 trapped students calling humus "abiotic" — it is **organic matter of biotic origin**, not a mineral.

**Producers** (autotrophs) capture solar energy via photosynthesis (or chemosynthesis at deep-sea vents). **Consumers** are classified by trophic level: primary (herbivores), secondary (carnivores eating herbivores), tertiary (top carnivores). **Decomposers** close the nutrient loop — without them, nutrients remain locked in dead bodies. **Detritivores** (earthworms) fragment large dead material, speeding decomposition.

**Abiotic factors** limit distribution via **Liebig's Law** (minimum limiting factor) and **Shelford's Law** (tolerance range). A plant may have plenty of water and light but fail if soil pH is outside its tolerance. **Edaphic** = soil-related; **Climatic** = weather-related; **Topographic** = landform-related.

> **Exam note:** Humus = **NOT abiotic** (organic, biotic-origin). Producers = autotrophs; Consumers = heterotrophs. Decomposers ≠ detritivores (decomposers chemically break down; detritivores physically ingest).

### Biotic Classification Table

| Group | Type | Examples | Trophic Role |
|-------|------|----------|--------------|
| **Producers** | Autotrophs | Wheat, phytoplankton, grass | Base of energy pyramid |
| **Primary consumers** | Herbivores | Deer, rabbit, zooplankton | Eat producers |
| **Secondary consumers** | Carnivores | Frog, small fish | Eat herbivores |
| **Tertiary consumers** | Top carnivores | Tiger, eagle | Eat carnivores |
| **Omnivores** | Mixed diet | Bear, human, crow | Multiple trophic levels |
| **Decomposers** | Saprotrophs | Bacteria, fungi | Recycle nutrients |
| **Detritivores** | Scavengers/fragmenters | Earthworm, vulture | Break up dead matter |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Producers = autotrophs (photosynthesis/chemosynthesis)
- Consumers = heterotrophs (herbivore → carnivore → top carnivore)
- Decomposers recycle nutrients; detritivores fragment dead matter
- **Humus = organic (biotic-origin)** — NOT abiotic
- Edaphic = soil; Climatic = weather; Topographic = landform
- 99% ecosystem energy from **sun**; chemosynthesis at hydrothermal vents exception
- Rhizobium = biotic N-fixation in legume root nodules

## 2.8 Ecological Niche

### Definitions

| Concept | Definition |
|---------|------------|
| **Ecological Niche** | Role/function of a species in ecosystem — what it eats, where it lives, when it's active, how it reproduces |
| **Fundamental Niche** | Full range of conditions/resources species *could* occupy without competition |
| **Realized Niche** | Actual occupied niche after competition, predation, and other limits |
| **Hutchinson (1957)** | Niche as **n-dimensional hypervolume** in environmental space |
| **Gause's Law** | Two species competing for same niche cannot coexist indefinitely — competitive exclusion |

### Ecological Niche — How It Works

**Habitat** tells you *where* an organism lives (address); **niche** tells you *what it does* there (job). Two species can share a habitat but occupy different niches — warblers feeding at different heights on the same tree. When niches **overlap too much**, **competitive exclusion** (Gause) operates: the superior competitor eliminates or displaces the weaker one. This is why invasive species devastate ecosystems — they occupy vacant or overlapping niches with no natural predators.

**Fundamental niche** is the theoretical maximum — a species' potential role if no competitors existed. **Realized niche** is smaller — what the species actually does after competition. On UPPCS 2024 Q9, the false statement was: "Number of species becomes **small** in ecological niche, if resources are **sufficient**." This is backwards — **sufficient resources support MORE species** (higher species richness). Equitable resource distribution reduces dominance of any single species (statement D is true).

**Dominant species** occupy broader niches (statement A true). Niche differentiation (resource partitioning) allows coexistence — similar species evolve to use slightly different resources or times.

> **Exam note:** UPPCS 2024 Q9 — FALSE statement = **C** ("species become small when resources sufficient"). Sufficient resources → **large** species number.

### Niche vs Habitat vs Ecosystem

| Term | Question answered | Example (Tiger) |
|------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Habitat** | Where? | Terai forest of Dudhwa |
| **Niche** | What role? | Apex predator controlling herbivore population |
| **Ecosystem** | Whole system? | Dudhwa forest + rivers + soil + all species |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Niche = **role** (not place — that's habitat)
- Fundamental vs Realized niche
- Gause's competitive exclusion principle
- Sufficient resources → **more** species (not fewer)
- Dominant species = broader niche
- Equitable resources → less single-species dominance
- Hutchinson: niche = multidimensional hypervolume

## 2.9 Ecological Principles

### Definitions

| Principle | Scientist | Statement |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Law of Minimum (Liebig)** | Justus von Liebig | Growth limited by scarcest essential resource (limiting factor) |
| **Law of Tolerance (Shelford)** | Victor Shelford | Organism survives only between min–max tolerance for each factor |
| **Competitive Exclusion (Gause)** | G.F. Gause | Two species with identical niche cannot coexist indefinitely |
| **Limiting Similarity** | MacArthur & Levins | Coexisting species must differ sufficiently in niche |
| **10% Energy Transfer (Lindeman)** | Raymond Lindeman (1942) | ~10% energy passes to next trophic level |
| **Allen's Rule** | Joel Allen | Extremities (ears, limbs) smaller in colder climates |
| **Bergmann's Rule** | Carl Bergmann | Body size larger in colder climates |
| **Gloger's Rule** | Constantin Gloger | Darker pigmentation in warmer, humid climates |
| **Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis** | Connell (1978) | Moderate disturbance maximises species diversity |

### Ecological Principles — How It Works

Ecological principles are the **rules of the game** — they predict how organisms respond to resources, competitors, and climate. **Liebig's Law** explains why fertilising a water-logged crop fails (water is the limiting factor, not nitrogen). **Shelford's Law** adds that every factor has a tolerance range — too much of a good thing (even water for a cactus) kills. Together, they explain species distribution: a fish needs oxygen (Liebig minimum) within a temperature range (Shelford tolerance).

**Lindeman's 10% rule** (1942) quantifies energy transfer: if producers fix 1000 kcal, primary consumers get ~100 kcal, secondary ~10 kcal. The 90% "loss" at each level is respiration, movement, heat — not "destruction." This is why food chains are short (rarely >4–5 levels) and why top predators are few. UPPCS 2025 Q5 Statement 2 tests this directly.

**Biogeographic rules** (Allen, Bergmann, Gloger) link **morphology to climate** — useful for evolutionary ecology questions. **Connell's intermediate disturbance hypothesis** explains why moderate forest fires or grazing can increase diversity (prevents competitive exclusion by one dominant species).

> **Exam note:** Liebig = **minimum/scarcest** factor; Shelford = **tolerance range**. Don't confuse. 10% rule = **energy** transfer (Lindeman 1942), not biomass of all organic matter in every context — but UPPCS 2025 accepted "10% organic matter to next consumer level."

### Principle Comparison Table

| Principle | What it limits | Exam trap |
|-----------|----------------|-----------|
| Liebig | Scarcest resource | Confusing with Shelford |
| Shelford | Min–max tolerance | "More is always better" = false |
| Gause | Niche overlap | Two identical niches → one survives |
| Lindeman 10% | Energy transfer | Thinking 50% or 90% transfers |
| Connell | Disturbance level | Zero disturbance ≠ max diversity |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Liebig = limiting factor (minimum); Shelford = tolerance range (min–max)
- Lindeman 1942: **10% energy** to next trophic level
- Gause: competitive exclusion — identical niches cannot coexist
- Allen: smaller extremities in cold; Bergmann: larger body in cold
- Connell 1978: intermediate disturbance → maximum diversity
- Liebig + Shelford taught together — paired concepts (Topic 1 §1.2 overlap)

**UPSC Prelims**

   → Lindeman's ecological efficiency principle.

3. **(UPSC 2014 — Topic 1 bank)** Legumes + urea add nitrogen — Liebig's limiting factor shifted by human input.

## 2.10 Ecological Balance

### Definitions

| Concept | Definition |
|---------|------------|
| **Ecological Balance** | Dynamic equilibrium among biotic and abiotic components of an ecosystem |
| **Homeostasis** | Self-regulating tendency of ecosystems to maintain stability |
| **Resilience** | Ability to recover after disturbance |
| **Resistance** | Ability to withstand disturbance without change |
| **Feedback (negative)** | Output reduces input — stabilises system (predator–prey cycles) |
| **Feedback (positive)** | Output amplifies change — destabilises (algal bloom → more decay → less O₂ → more death) |

### Ecological Balance — How It Works

Ecological balance is **not static** — it is a **dynamic equilibrium** where populations rise and fall in cycles but the overall system persists. Classic example: when rabbit population increases, fox population rises (more food), which then reduces rabbits, which eventually reduces foxes — a **negative feedback loop** restoring balance. When humans remove top predators (wolves, tigers), herbivore populations explode, vegetation collapses, and the system loses balance.

**Disturbances** — fire, flood, drought, disease — are natural. Ecosystems with high **resilience** (grasslands, tropical forests) recover quickly; low-resilience systems (coral reefs, alpine meadows) take decades. **Keystone species** (starfish in intertidal, beavers in streams, tigers in forests) disproportionately maintain balance — removing them causes cascade effects far beyond their biomass contribution.

Human activities disrupt balance through **pollution** (nutrient overload → eutrophication), **habitat fragmentation**, **invasive species**, and **overexploitation**. The **IPAT equation** (Impact = Population × Affluence × Technology) quantifies human pressure on ecological balance.

> **Exam note:** Balance = **dynamic** equilibrium (not frozen). Removing apex predator → herbivore explosion → vegetation loss = imbalance cascade.

### Factors Maintaining vs Disrupting Balance

| Maintaining | Disrupting |
|-------------|------------|
| Predator–prey cycles | Overhunting/poaching |
| Decomposer nutrient recycling | Pesticide killing decomposers |
| Keystone species presence | Invasive species (water hyacinth) |
| Negative feedback loops | Positive feedback (eutrophication death spiral) |
| Biodiversity (insurance effect) | Monoculture plantations |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Ecological balance = **dynamic** equilibrium (not static)
- Negative feedback = stabilising; Positive feedback = destabilising
- Resilience = recovery capacity; Resistance = withstand capacity
- Keystone species maintain balance disproportionately
- Removing top predator causes trophic cascade
- Human disruption: pollution, invasion, overexploitation, fragmentation
- Biodiversity increases ecosystem stability (insurance hypothesis)

**UPPCS Prelims**

   → Balance maintained when no one species monopolises resources.

**UPSC Prelims**

   → Shared resource overuse → degradation.

   → Integrated conservation preserves balance.

## 2.11 Ecological Succession

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Ecological Succession** | Predictable, directional change in species composition of a community over time |
| **Primary Succession** | Starts on bare, lifeless substrate (bare rock, cooled lava, sand dune) |
| **Secondary Succession** | Starts where soil/organisms already exist after disturbance (fire, abandoned farm) |
| **Pioneer Species** | First colonisers — hardy, fast-growing (lichen, moss, phytoplankton) |
| **Seral Stages** | Intermediate communities during succession (seral community) |
| **Climax Community** | Final, relatively stable community in equilibrium with climate |
| **Sere** | Entire sequence of successional communities |

### Ecological Succession — How It Works

Succession is ecological **community ageing** — from bare substrate to complex, stable community. **Primary succession** begins where no soil exists: lichens secrete acids, weather rock into soil (pedogenesis), mosses follow, then grasses, shrubs, and finally trees. A **cooled volcanic island** or **glacier retreat zone** shows primary succession. **Secondary succession** is faster because soil and seed banks survive — an abandoned UP farmland reverts to grassland then scrub forest within decades.

**Hydrosere** (aquatic succession): pond → submerged plants → floating plants → reed swamp → marsh → meadow → woodland. **Xerosere** (dry succession): rock → lichen → moss → grass → shrub → forest. Each stage **modifies the environment** for the next (pioneers add organic matter, improve soil, increase shade) — this is the **reaction phase**.

**Succession phases** (UPPCS 2024 Q26): **Migration** (seeds/spores arrive) → **Ecesis** (germination, establishment) → **Reaction** (species modify environment) → **Stabilisation** (climax community). Earlier phases also include **Nudation** (creation of bare area) and **Aggregation** (multiplication of established species).

**Climax theory**: **Clements** argued one predictable climax per climate (monoclimax). **Gleason** argued individualistic, unpredictable species assembly. Modern view: **climax as mosaic** — multiple stable states possible.

**Lichens** are classic pioneer species on bare rock — also **pollution indicators** (sensitive to SO₂). They grow poorly in polluted areas (UPPCS 2025 Q61: Statement 1 true — good indicators; Statement 2 false — they do NOT grow well in pollution).

> **Exam note:** UPPCS 2024 Q26 — correct sequence = **D: Migration → Ecesis → Reaction → Stabilisation**. Lichens = pioneer + pollution indicator (NOT pollution-tolerant).

### Primary vs Secondary Succession

| Feature | Primary | Secondary |
|---------|---------|-----------|
| Starting point | Bare rock, lava, sand | Burned forest, abandoned field |
| Soil present? | No — must form | Yes — already exists |
| Speed | Very slow (centuries) | Faster (decades) |
| Pioneer | Lichen on rock | Grasses, weeds |
| India example | New volcanic barren land | Abandoned farmland in Bundelkhand |

### Succession Phases (complete)

| Phase | Process |
|-------|---------|
| **1. Nudation** | Bare area created (volcano, fire, flood, human clearing) |
| **2. Migration** | Seeds, spores, propagules reach the area |
| **3. Ecesis** | Germination, establishment, initial growth |
| **4. Aggregation** | Multiplication and increase in number |
| **5. Competition** | Species compete for resources; some eliminated |
| **6. Reaction** | Community modifies environment (soil, light, moisture) |
| **7. Stabilisation** | Climax community reached; equilibrium with climate |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Primary = no soil (lichen on rock); Secondary = soil exists (faster)
- Phases: Migration → Ecesis → Reaction → Stabilisation (UPPCS 2024)
- Hydrosere = water to land; Xerosere = dry rock to forest
- Pioneer species: lichen, moss, phytoplankton, grasses
- Climax = final stable community
- Clements (monoclimax) vs Gleason (individualistic)
- Lichens = pioneer + **pollution indicator** (die in polluted air)

## 2.12 Ecosystem Services

### Definitions

| Category (MEA 2005) | Definition | Examples |
|---------------------|------------|----------|
| **Provisioning** | Products obtained from ecosystems | Food, water, timber, fibre, fuel, genetic resources |
| **Regulating** | Benefits from ecosystem regulation | Climate regulation, flood control, pollination, disease control, water purification |
| **Supporting** | Services necessary for other categories | Nutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production, oxygen production |
| **Cultural** | Non-material benefits | Recreation, aesthetic, spiritual, educational, tourism |

### Ecosystem Services — How It Works

Ecosystem services are the **benefits humans derive from functioning ecosystems** — without paying for them directly. The **Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (MEA, 2005)** classified them into four categories (memorise as **PRCS**: Provisioning, Regulating, Cultural, Supporting). **TEEB** (The Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity, 2010) valued these in monetary terms — e.g., pollinators contribute billions to agriculture; wetlands save flood damage costs.

**Provisioning** is the most visible — crops, fish, timber. **Regulating** is the most undervalued — forests regulate rainfall, mangroves buffer cyclones, wetlands filter sewage. **Supporting** underpins everything — without nutrient cycling and soil formation, no agriculture exists. **Cultural** includes pilgrimage sites in forests (Himalaya), bird-watching at **Nawabganj Bird Sanctuary (UP)**, and Ganga's spiritual significance.

When ecosystems degrade, services decline: deforestation → lost flood regulation (UP floods worsen); wetland drainage → lost water purification; pollinator decline → reduced crop yield. **IPBES** (Intergovernmental Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services, 2012) continues MEA's work with policy-focused assessments.

> **Exam note:** Four categories = **Provisioning, Regulating, Supporting, Cultural** (MEA 2005). Pollination = **Regulating** (not Provisioning). Nutrient cycling = **Supporting**.

## 2.13 Ecosystem Productivity

### Definitions

| Term | Formula/Definition |
|------|-------------------|
| **GPP (Gross Primary Productivity)** | Total energy fixed by producers through photosynthesis |
| **NPP (Net Primary Productivity)** | GPP − Respiration (R) of producers — biomass available for consumers |
| **NSP (Net Secondary Productivity)** | Energy left after heterotroph respiration — available to next trophic level |
| **Standing Crop/Biomass** | Total mass of living organisms at a given time (g/m²) |
| **Productivity** | Rate of biomass production per unit area per unit time (g/m²/yr or kcal/m²/yr) |

### Ecosystem Productivity — How It Works

Productivity measures **how much life an ecosystem makes** — the engine of all food webs. **GPP** is total photosynthetic capture. Plants use much of it for their own respiration (R), leaving **NPP = GPP − R** as the energy available to herbivores. This is the most important productivity metric in exams.

In **terrestrial ecosystems**, plants capture only about **1% of incident solar energy** falling on their leaves (NCERT; UPPCS 2025 Q5 Statement 1). The rest is reflected, transmitted, or not usable for photosynthesis. Despite this tiny fraction, tropical rainforests have the **highest NPP** globally (~2000 g/m²/yr) due to year-round warmth and rainfall. Deserts have the lowest. **Oceans** cover 71% of Earth but have lower per-area productivity except in coastal/neritic zones (upwelling nutrients).

**Standing crop** is a snapshot (how much biomass exists now); **productivity** is a rate (how fast biomass accumulates). A forest has high standing crop but slow annual productivity; a grassland has lower standing crop but can have comparable productivity due to fast turnover.

> **Exam note:** UPPCS 2025 Q5 — plants capture **~1% of sunlight** on leaves = **TRUE**. GPP − R = NPP. Units: **g/m²/yr**.

### Productivity Comparison

| Ecosystem | NPP (approx. g/m²/yr) | Rank |
|-----------|----------------------|------|
| Tropical rainforest | 2000 | Highest |
| Wetlands/marshes | 1500 | Very high |
| Tropical deciduous forest | 1200 | High |
| Temperate forest | 800 | Moderate |
| Grassland | 600 | Moderate |
| Desert | 200 | Low |
| Open ocean (pelagic) | 200 | Low per area |
| Continental shelf (neritic) | 1000 | High (marine) |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- GPP − R = **NPP** (most important metric)
- Terrestrial plants capture **~1% of sunlight** on leaves (NCERT; UPPCS 2025)
- Highest NPP: tropical rainforest; Lowest: desert, open ocean
- Productivity units: g/m²/yr or kcal/m²/yr
- Standing crop ≠ productivity (forest: high crop, grassland: fast turnover)
- Coastal oceans more productive than open ocean (nutrient upwelling)

## 2.14 Ecological Efficiency

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Ecological Efficiency** | Percentage of energy/biomass transferred from one trophic level to the next |
| **Lindeman's 10% Rule (1942)** | ~10% of energy at one trophic level passes to the next |
| **Assimilation Efficiency** | % of consumed food assimilated into body (not egested) |
| **Net Production Efficiency** | % of assimilated energy converted to biomass (rest = respiration) |
| **Ecological Pyramids** | Graphic representation of energy/biomass/numbers at trophic levels |

### Ecological Efficiency — How It Works

Energy transfer between trophic levels is **highly inefficient** — approximately **90% is lost** at each step as respiration, heat, movement, and waste. **Lindeman (1942)** established the **10% rule**: if producers have 10,000 kcal, primary consumers get ~1,000 kcal, secondary ~100 kcal, tertiary ~10 kcal. UPPCS 2025 Q5 Statement 2 — "Around 10% of organic matter reaches the next higher level of consumers" — is **correct** per NCERT.

This inefficiency explains three universal patterns: **(1)** food chains are short (rarely >4–5 levels); **(2)** top predators are few (tiger, eagle); **(3)** more land is needed to feed carnivores than herbivores. It takes ~10 kg of grain to produce 1 kg of beef (rough trophic transfer analogy).

**Three types of ecological efficiency**: **Assimilation efficiency** (how much eaten is absorbed — varies 20–50% for herbivores); **Net production efficiency** (how much absorbed becomes new biomass — ~20–30%); **Ecological efficiency** (combined effect — the famous ~10%). **Ecological pyramids** (energy, biomass, number) visualise this — energy pyramid is always upright; biomass/number pyramids can be inverted in some aquatic systems (Topic 3 covers pyramid detail).

> **Exam note:** 10% rule = **Lindeman 1942**. UPPCS 2025: both 1% sunlight AND 10% transfer are correct → **Answer C**. Full pyramid types taught in Topic 3.

### Energy Loss at Each Trophic Level

| Fate of energy | Approximate % lost |
|----------------|-------------------|
| Respiration (metabolic heat) | ~60–70% |
| Movement, digestion, excretion | ~20–30% |
| Transferred to next level | **~10%** |
| Not consumed (dies, decomposed) | Variable |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Lindeman **1942**: **10% energy** transfer between trophic levels
- ~90% lost as heat/respiration at each level
- Short food chains (3–5 levels max) due to energy loss
- Few top predators because energy dwindles up the chain
- Assimilation efficiency ≠ ecological efficiency
- UPPCS 2025 Q5: 10% organic matter to next consumer = **TRUE**

## 2.15 Genetic Level Ecology

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Gene Pool** | Total collection of genes in all individuals of a population |
| **Genetic Diversity** | Variety of genes/alleles within a species or population |
| **Allele Frequency** | Proportion of specific allele in gene pool |
| **Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium** | Allele frequencies remain constant in ideal population (no evolution) |
| **Microevolution** | Change in allele frequencies over generations |

### Genetic Level Ecology — How It Works

At the **genetic level**, ecology studies how environmental pressures shape **gene pools** of populations. A population (e.g., tiger in Dudhwa) carries a shared **gene pool** — all alleles for coat colour, disease resistance, size. **Genetic diversity** within this pool is the raw material for adaptation: a population with low genetic diversity (cheetah, Asiatic lion) is vulnerable to disease and environmental change.

**Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium** (p² + 2pq + q² = 1) describes an **ideal population** where allele frequencies don't change — requiring: no mutation, no migration, large population, random mating, no natural selection. Real populations violate these conditions, so **microevolution** (allele frequency change) occurs. Environmental factors drive selection: pesticide resistance in mosquitoes, drought-tolerant alleles in plants.

**Genetic level ecology** connects to conservation: small isolated populations (UP's fragmented tiger corridors) lose genetic diversity through **inbreeding** and **genetic drift**. Wildlife corridors and translocation programmes aim to maintain gene flow between populations.

> **Exam note:** Hardy-Weinberg = **no evolution** conditions (ideal). Genetic diversity = adaptation insurance. Small population = genetic drift risk.

### Hardy-Weinberg Conditions (all must be met)

| Condition | Violation causes |
|-----------|------------------|
| No mutation | Mutation introduces new alleles |
| No gene flow (migration) | Immigration/emigration changes frequencies |
| Large population | Genetic drift in small populations |
| Random mating | Assortative mating changes genotype ratios |
| No natural selection | Selection favours certain alleles |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Gene pool = all genes in a population
- Genetic diversity = raw material for adaptation
- Hardy-Weinberg: p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (no evolution in ideal conditions)
- Five violations: mutation, migration, small size, non-random mating, selection
- Small isolated populations → inbreeding + genetic drift
- Cheetah, Asiatic lion = low genetic diversity examples

## 2.16 Species Level Ecology

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Population Ecology** | Study of population size, density, distribution, age structure, growth |
| **Population Density** | Number of individuals per unit area/volume |
| **Growth Curve (J-shaped)** | Exponential growth — no limiting resources |
| **Growth Curve (S-shaped/Logistic)** | Growth slows as carrying capacity approached |
| **r-strategists** | Many offspring, fast reproduction, short life, little parental care |
| **K-strategists** | Few offspring, slow reproduction, long life, high parental care |
| **Carrying Capacity (K)** | Maximum population size environment can sustain indefinitely |

### Species Level Ecology — How It Works

**Population ecology** tracks how single-species populations change over time. Key parameters: **birth rate, death rate, immigration, emigration, age structure, sex ratio**. Populations grow exponentially (J-curve) when resources are unlimited — rare in nature. Real populations follow **logistic growth** (S-curve), levelling off at **carrying capacity (K)** where birth rate equals death rate.

**r-strategists** (mosquitoes, weeds, mice) produce many offspring quickly, invest little in each, and colonise disturbed habitats — ideal pioneer species. **K-strategists** (elephants, tigers, whales) produce few offspring, invest heavily in parental care, and thrive in stable climax communities. This r/K framework explains life-history strategies and is tested in comparative questions.

**Density-dependent** factors (competition, disease, predation) intensify as population grows — regulate around carrying capacity. **Density-independent** factors (fire, flood, drought) affect population regardless of size. Tiger population in UP Terai is regulated by prey availability (density-dependent) and monsoon flooding (density-independent).

> **Exam note:** J-curve = exponential (unlimited resources); S-curve = logistic (carrying capacity). r-strategist = many offspring; K-strategist = few, cared-for offspring.

### r vs K Strategists — Complete Comparison

| Feature | r-strategist | K-strategist |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Offspring number | Many | Few |
| Body size | Small | Large |
| Lifespan | Short | Long |
| Parental care | Minimal | Extensive |
| Reproduction age | Early | Late |
| Population stability | Fluctuating | Stable near K |
| Examples | Mosquito, rat, weed, phytoplankton | Elephant, tiger, whale, human |
| Succession role | Pioneer/coloniser | Climax community species |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Population ecology = single-species dynamics
- J-curve = exponential; S-curve = logistic (carrying capacity K)
- r-strategists: many offspring, fast, short life
- K-strategists: few offspring, slow, long life, parental care
- Density-dependent: competition, predation, disease
- Density-independent: fire, flood, drought
- Carrying capacity = max sustainable population

## 2.17 Community Level Ecology

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Community** | All populations of different species living and interacting in an area |
| **Species Richness** | Number of different species in a community |
| **Species Evenness** | Relative abundance of each species |
| **Species Diversity** | Richness + Evenness combined (Shannon index) |
| **Competition** | Species compete for same limited resource |
| **Predation** | One species kills and eats another |
| **Mutualism** | Both species benefit (+/+) |
| **Commensalism** | One benefits, other unaffected (+/0) |
| **Parasitism** | One benefits, other harmed (+/−) |

### Community Level Ecology — How It Works

A **community** is the biotic heart of an ecosystem — all species interacting simultaneously. **Species richness** (how many species) and **evenness** (how equally abundant) together define **diversity**. High diversity communities (tropical rainforest) are generally more **stable and resilient** — the "insurance hypothesis" — because if one species fails, others compensate.

**Species interactions** shape community structure:

- **Competition** (–/–): two species fight for same resource; resolved by niche partitioning or competitive exclusion (Gause).
- **Predation** (+/–): predator controls prey population; keystone predators (sea otter, tiger) maintain community structure.
- **Mutualism** (+/+): both benefit — mycorrhizal fungi + plant roots (fungi get sugar, plant gets phosphorus); Rhizobium + legume.
- **Commensalism** (+/0): epiphytes on trees (get height/light, tree unaffected); cattle egret with grazing cattle.
- **Parasitism** (+/–): tick on deer, mistletoe on tree — parasite benefits, host harmed.

**Connell's Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis**: moderate disturbance (fire, grazing) prevents one species from dominating, maintaining high diversity. **Edge effect**: community at habitat boundary (forest–field edge) often has higher diversity but also more invasive species.

> **Exam note:** Mutualism = both benefit (+/+). Commensalism = one benefits, other neutral (+/0). Parasitism = one benefits, other harmed (+/−). Don't confuse commensalism with mutualism.

### Species Interactions — Complete Table

| Interaction | Species A | Species B | Example |
|-------------|-----------|-----------|---------|
| **Competition** | − | − | Two deer species eating same grass |
| **Predation** | + | − | Tiger kills deer |
| **Mutualism** | + | + | Rhizobium fixes N for legume |
| **Commensalism** | + | 0 | Epiphyte orchid on sal tree |
| **Parasitism** | + | − | Tapeworm in human intestine |
| **Amensalism** | − | 0 | Penicillium secretes antibiotic killing bacteria |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Community = all species populations in an area
- Species diversity = richness + evenness
- Competition (−/−), Predation (+/−), Mutualism (+/+), Commensalism (+/0), Parasitism (+/−)
- High diversity → more stable community (insurance hypothesis)
- Connell: intermediate disturbance → max diversity
- Keystone predator maintains community structure
- Edge effect at habitat boundaries

## Mains Answer Framework (Topic 2 — Optional)

### Q: Explain Ecosystem Structure and Functions with Indian Examples

**Structure (200 words):**
1. **Define ecosystem** (Tansley 1935) — functional unit
2. **Structure:** Biotic (producers, consumers, decomposers) + Abiotic (climatic, edaphic, topographic)
3. **Functions:** Productivity (GPP/NPP), decomposition, nutrient cycling, energy flow
4. **Indian example:** Dudhwa Terai — sal forest producers, deer consumers, tiger apex predator, soil decomposers
5. **Energy:** 1% sunlight capture, 10% trophic transfer (Lindeman)
6. **Conclusion:** Ecosystem health = all four functions working in balance

### Q: Ecological Succession and Its Role in Ecosystem Restoration

**Structure (200 words):**
1. **Define succession** — directional community change
2. **Primary vs Secondary** with examples (volcanic island vs abandoned UP farmland)
3. **Phases:** Migration → Ecesis → Reaction → Stabilisation
4. **Hydrosere/Xerosere** examples
5. **Restoration ecology** application: wetland restoration (Sur Sarovar), reforestation
6. **Conclusion:** Succession shows ecosystems self-repair if given time and reduced human disturbance
