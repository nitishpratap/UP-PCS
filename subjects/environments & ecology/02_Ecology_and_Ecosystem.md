# Ecology – Meaning

## Definition

**Ecology** (Greek: *Oikos* = house/home + *Logos* = study) is the branch of biology that studies the **interactions among organisms and between organisms and their physical (abiotic) environment**.

**Ernst Haeckel (1866)** coined the term "Ecology."

**NCERT definition:** *"Ecology is the study of the relationships of living organisms with each other and with their environment."*

**Levels of ecological organisation:**
1. Organism → 2. Population → 3. Community → 4. Ecosystem → 5. Biome → 6. Biosphere

## Explanation

Ecology is both a **natural science** and an **interdisciplinary field** connecting biology, geography, chemistry, climatology, and social sciences.

**Branches of Ecology:**

| Branch | Study Focus |
|--------|-------------|
| **Autoecology** | Individual species and its environment |
| **Synecology** | Communities and ecosystems |
| **Population Ecology** | Population dynamics, growth, regulation |
| **Community Ecology** | Species interactions (competition, predation, symbiosis) |
| **Ecosystem Ecology** | Energy flow, nutrient cycling |
| **Landscape Ecology** | Spatial patterns across landscapes |
| **Global Ecology** | Biosphere-level processes (biogeochemical cycles) |
| **Applied Ecology** | Conservation, restoration, environmental management |

**Ecology vs Environment Studies:**
- Ecology = scientific study of interactions (biology-based)
- Environment Studies = broader interdisciplinary study including policy, economics, law

**Father of Ecology in India:** **Ramdeo Misra** (considered father of Indian ecology).

**Father of Modern Ecology:** **Eugene P. Odum** (*Fundamentals of Ecology*, 1953).

## Important Facts

- Ernst Haeckel coined "Ecology" in **1866**.
- **E.P. Odum** — father of modern ecosystem ecology.
- **Ramdeo Misra** — father of ecology in India.
- Ecology operates at **6 hierarchical levels** (organism to biosphere).
- **Deep Ecology** (Arne Næss, 1973) — philosophical movement valuing all life intrinsically.
- **Human Ecology** — study of human societies as ecological systems.
- **Restoration Ecology** — science of restoring degraded ecosystems.

# Ecosystem

## Definition

An **ecosystem** is a **functional unit of nature** where living organisms (biotic community) interact among themselves and with the surrounding physical environment (abiotic factors) as a system, resulting in energy flow and nutrient cycling.

**A.G. Tansley (1935)** coined the term "ecosystem."

**NCERT definition:** *"An ecosystem is a functional unit of nature where living organisms interact among themselves and with the surrounding physical environment."*

**Key characteristics:**
- Open system (energy flows in and out)
- Dynamic equilibrium
- Self-regulating (homeostasis)
- Functional (not just structural)

## Explanation

### Components of Ecosystem

```
ECOSYSTEM
├── Abiotic (Climate, soil, water, minerals, sunlight)
└── Biotic
    ├── Producers (Autotrophs)
    ├── Consumers (Heterotrophs)
    │   ├── Primary (Herbivores)
    │   ├── Secondary (Carnivores)
    │   └── Tertiary (Top carnivores)
    └── Decomposers (Bacteria, fungi)
```

### Types (by origin)
- **Natural:** Forest, lake, desert, ocean
- **Artificial/Man-made:** Crop field, aquarium, garden

### Types (by size)
- **Micro:** Pond water drop, rotting log
- **Macro:** Ocean, forest, grassland

### Functions
1. **Energy flow** — Unidirectional (sun → producers → consumers → decomposers)
2. **Nutrient cycling** — Cyclic (biogeochemical cycles)
3. **Primary production** — Conversion of solar energy to biomass
4. **Ecological regulation** — Climate moderation, flood control, pollination

### Energy Flow vs Nutrient Cycling

| Feature | Energy Flow | Nutrient Cycling |
|---------|-------------|------------------|
| Direction | Unidirectional | Cyclic |
| Loss | Lost as heat at each trophic level | Recycled within ecosystem |
| Source | Sun | Earth/atmosphere (finite pool) |

## Important Facts

- **Tansley (1935)** introduced "ecosystem" concept.
- Ecosystem is an **open system** — exchanges energy and matter with surroundings.
- **10% Law (Lindeman, 1942):** Only ~10% energy transferred between trophic levels.
- **Gross Primary Productivity (GPP)** > **Net Primary Productivity (NPP)**.
- **NPP = GPP − Respiration (R)**.
- Largest ecosystem on Earth: **Ocean/Marine ecosystem**.
- **Estuary** = among most productive ecosystems (high nutrient mixing).

# Types of Ecosystems

## Definition

**Types of ecosystems** refer to the classification of ecosystems based on various criteria — primarily **habitat medium** (terrestrial vs aquatic), **origin** (natural vs artificial), **size** (micro vs macro), **climate** (biome-based), and **salinity** (freshwater vs marine vs brackish).

## Explanation

### Major Classification Framework

```
ECOSYSTEMS
├── TERRESTRIAL
│   ├── Forests (Tropical, Temperate, Boreal/Taiga)
│   ├── Grasslands (Savanna, Prairie, Steppe)
│   ├── Deserts (Hot, Cold)
│   ├── Tundra (Arctic, Alpine)
│   └── Mountain
├── AQUATIC
│   ├── Freshwater
│   │   ├── Lentic (standing): Ponds, lakes, wetlands
│   │   └── Lotic (flowing): Rivers, streams
│   ├── Marine (oceans, seas, coral reefs, estuaries)
│   └── Brackish (estuaries, mangroves, lagoons)
└── ARTIFICIAL
    ├── Croplands / Agroecosystems
    ├── Urban ecosystems
    └── Aquaculture systems
```

### Classification by Productivity

| Category | Ecosystems | NPP Range |
|----------|------------|-----------|
| **Most productive** | Estuaries, swamps, marshes, tropical rainforest, mangroves, coral reefs | 1500–3000 g/m²/yr |
| **Moderately productive** | Temperate forest, savanna | 600–1200 g/m²/yr |
| **Least productive** | Open ocean, desert, tundra | <200 g/m²/yr |

### Classification by Stability

| Type | Characteristics | Examples |
|------|-----------------|----------|
| **Pioneer/Unstable** | Early succession, low species diversity | Bare rock, new volcanic island |
| **Stable/Climax** | High diversity, complex food webs | Old-growth tropical rainforest |
| **Disturbed** | Periodic disruption, intermediate | Fire-prone savanna |

### India's Major Ecosystem Types

| Ecosystem | Area/Extent |
|-----------|-------------|
| Forest | ~24% of geographical area (forest cover) |
| Grassland | Deccan Plateau, Terai |
| Desert | Thar Desert |
| Wetlands | ~1,57,000 sq km (4.7% of area) |
| Coastal/Marine | 7517 km coastline |
| Himalayan/Alpine | Northern mountain ecosystems |

## Important Facts

- **14 terrestrial biomes** globally.
- **71% of Earth** covered by aquatic ecosystems.
- **Estuaries** are called **"nurseries of the sea"** — highest productivity.
- **Tropical rainforests** cover ~6% of land but contain >50% of species.
- India has **10 biogeographic zones** hosting diverse ecosystem types.
- **Wetlands** = Ramsar Convention protection category.
- **Agroecosystem** = largest human-modified ecosystem by area globally.

# Terrestrial Ecosystem

## Definition

A **terrestrial ecosystem** is an ecosystem that exists on **land** (lithosphere), characterized by organisms adapted to life in soil and air, influenced primarily by **climatic factors** (temperature, rainfall, humidity) and **edaphic factors** (soil type, pH, minerals).

Land covers approximately **29%** of Earth's surface but supports the majority of human population and terrestrial biodiversity.

## Explanation

### Major Terrestrial Ecosystems (Biomes)

| Biome | Climate | Vegetation | Key Fauna |
|-------|---------|------------|-----------|
| **Tropical Rainforest** | Hot, wet (>200 cm rain) | Dense evergreen, multi-layered canopy | Tigers, monkeys, hornbills |
| **Tropical Deciduous** | Monsoon, seasonal rain | Teak, sal, deciduous trees | Deer, elephants, peacocks |
| **Temperate Deciduous** | Moderate, 4 seasons | Oak, maple, birch | Bears, wolves, deer |
| **Taiga/Boreal** | Cold, long winters | Coniferous (pine, spruce, fir) | Moose, lynx, wolves |
| **Tundra** | Very cold, short summer | Mosses, lichens, dwarf shrubs | Caribou, Arctic fox, lemmings |
| **Grassland/Savanna** | Moderate rain | Grasses, scattered trees | Lions, zebras, antelopes |
| **Desert** | Arid (<25 cm rain) | Xerophytes, cacti, shrubs | Camels, reptiles, rodents |
| **Mediterranean** | Mild, wet winter, dry summer | Sclerophyllous shrubs | Deer, wild goats |
| **Mountain/Alpine** | Altitude-dependent | Changes with elevation | Snow leopard, yak |

### Indian Terrestrial Ecosystems

| Ecosystem | Region |
|-----------|--------|
| Tropical Wet Evergreen | Western Ghats, NE India |
| Tropical Moist Deciduous | Central India, Himalayan foothills |
| Tropical Dry Deciduous | Peninsular India |
| Tropical Thorn Forest | Rajasthan, Gujarat |
| Montane/Wet Temperate | Himalayas |
| Alpine | High Himalayas (>3500 m) |
| Desert | Thar Desert |

### Adaptations in Terrestrial Ecosystems

| Adaptation | Example |
|------------|---------|
| **Xerophytic** (water conservation) | Cactus — thick cuticle, sunken stomata |
| **Mesophytic** (moderate water) | Most crop plants |
| **Hygrophytic** (water-loving) | Plants in wet habitats |
| **Cryptozoic** (burrowing) | Desert rodents, reptiles |
| **Hibernation** | Bears in temperate regions |
| **Aestivation** | Frogs, snails in summer drought |

### Structure of Forest Ecosystem (Vertical Stratification)

1. **Emergent layer** — Tallest trees (40–60 m)
2. **Canopy** — Main tree crowns
3. **Understorey** — Shrubs, young trees
4. **Herb/Field layer** — Grasses, herbs
5. **Forest floor** — Decomposers, detritus

## Important Facts

- Terrestrial ecosystems occupy **29%** of Earth's surface.
- **Tropical rainforests** = greatest terrestrial biodiversity (50%+ of species on 6% land).
- **Taiga** = largest terrestrial biome by area.
- **Permafrost** = permanently frozen subsoil in tundra and taiga.
- India's forest cover: **~24.6%** of geographical area (ISFR 2023).
- **Endemic species** concentrated in Western Ghats and NE India terrestrial ecosystems.
- **Desertification** affects ~30% of India's land area.
- **Social forestry** and **agroforestry** = human-managed terrestrial ecosystems.

# Aquatic Ecosystem

## Definition

An **aquatic ecosystem** is an ecosystem located in **water bodies** (hydrosphere), encompassing **marine** (oceans, seas), **freshwater** (lakes, rivers, ponds), and **brackish water** (estuaries, mangroves) environments where organisms are adapted to life in or near water.

Water covers approximately **71%** of Earth's surface, making aquatic ecosystems the geographically dominant ecosystem type.

## Explanation

### Classification of Aquatic Ecosystems

```
AQUATIC ECOSYSTEMS
├── MARINE (Saltwater, >3.5% salinity)
│   ├── Open Ocean (pelagic)
│   ├── Deep Sea (benthic)
│   ├── Coral Reefs
│   ├── Estuaries
│   └── Mangroves
├── FRESHWATER (<0.5% salinity)
│   ├── Lentic (standing water)
│   │   ├── Ponds
│   │   ├── Lakes
│   │   └── Wetlands (marshes, swamps, bogs)
│   └── Lotic (flowing water)
│       ├── Rivers
│       └── Streams
└── BRACKISH (0.5–3.5% salinity)
    ├── Estuaries
    ├── Mangrove swamps
    └── Lagoons
```

### Zones of Lake Ecosystem (Lentic)

| Zone | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Littoral** | Shallow, near shore; rooted plants, high biodiversity |
| **Limnetic** | Open water, light penetrates; plankton, free-swimming fish |
| **Profundal** | Deep water, no light; decomposers, low DO |
| **Benthic** | Bottom sediment; bottom dwellers, decomposers |

### Zones of Ocean Ecosystem (Marine)

| Zone | Depth | Characteristics |
|------|-------|-----------------|
| **Intertidal** | 0 | Alternately submerged/exposed |
| **Neritic** | 0–200 m | Continental shelf, most productive |
| **Oceanic (Pelagic)** | >200 m | Open ocean |
| **Benthic** | Sea floor | Bottom-dwelling organisms |
| **Abyssal** | >4000 m | Extreme pressure, darkness |

### Key Characteristics

| Feature | Aquatic Adaptation |
|---------|-------------------|
| **Buoyancy** | Reduced skeletal structure in marine animals |
| **Streamlined body** | Fish, aquatic mammals |
| **Gills** | Dissolved oxygen extraction |
| **Salinity tolerance** | Euryhaline (wide range) vs Stenohaline (narrow) |
| **Plankton** | Drifting organisms (phytoplankton = producers) |

### Productivity Comparison

| Ecosystem | Productivity | Reason |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| **Estuary** | Highest | Nutrient mixing of river + sea |
| **Coral reef** | Very high | Symbiotic algae, nutrient-rich |
| **Mangrove** | High | Nutrient trapping, tidal flushing |
| **Open ocean** | Lowest | Nutrient-poor, sunlight only in surface |

### Indian Aquatic Ecosystems

| Type | Examples |
|------|----------|
| Rivers | Ganga, Brahmaputra, Godavari, Krishna, Narmada |
| Lakes | Wular (J&K), Chilika (Odisha), Loktak (Manipur), Dal Lake |
| Wetlands (Ramsar) | Bhitarkanika, Sundarbans, Keoladeo |
| Mangroves | Sundarbans (largest), Bhitarkanika, Pichavaram |
| Coral reefs | Gulf of Mannar, Gulf of Kutch, Lakshadweep, Andaman |
| Estuaries | Hooghly, Cochin backwaters |

## Important Facts

- **71%** of Earth's surface is aquatic.
- **Phytoplankton** produce ~50% of Earth's oxygen (more than all forests combined).
- **Coral reefs** = "rainforests of the sea" — 25% of marine species on 0.1% of ocean area.
- **Sundarbans** = largest mangrove ecosystem in the world.
- India has **7517 km coastline** and **85+ Ramsar wetland sites**.
- **Eutrophication** = excessive nutrient enrichment → algal blooms → oxygen depletion.
- **Dissolved Oxygen (DO)** = critical abiotic factor; fish need >5 mg/L.
- **Wetlands** store **30% of global soil carbon** despite covering only 5–8% of land surface.

# Structure and Functions of Ecosystem

## Definition

**Structure of an ecosystem** refers to its **biotic composition** (species present, trophic levels, stratification) and **abiotic framework** (climate, soil, water, nutrients). **Functions** refer to the **dynamic processes** — energy flow, nutrient cycling, primary production, and ecological regulation — that maintain the ecosystem.

## Explanation

### Structural Components

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Species composition** | Types and number of species |
| **Trophic structure** | Arrangement of organisms by feeding level |
| **Stratification** | Vertical/layered arrangement (forest canopy layers) |
| **Standing crop/biomass** | Total mass of living organisms at a given time |
| **Abiotic framework** | Climate, topography, soil, water chemistry |

### Functional Processes

| Function | Process |
|----------|---------|
| **Primary production** | Autotrophs convert solar energy to biomass |
| **Secondary production** | Heterotrophs convert consumed biomass to own tissue |
| **Energy flow** | Unidirectional transfer through trophic levels |
| **Nutrient cycling** | Circulation of C, N, P, S, H₂O through biotic-abiotic reservoirs |
| **Decomposition** | Breakdown of organic matter; nutrient release |
| **Ecological regulation** | Climate moderation, flood control, pollination |

### Major Biogeochemical Cycles

| Cycle | Key Reservoir | Human Impact |
|-------|---------------|--------------|
| **Carbon (C)** | Atmosphere (CO₂), oceans, fossil fuels | Fossil fuel burning → global warming |
| **Nitrogen (N)** | Atmosphere (N₂) | Fertilizer runoff → eutrophication |
| **Phosphorus (P)** | Rocks, soil (no atmospheric phase) | Mining, agricultural runoff |
| **Water (H₂O)** | Oceans, ice caps, groundwater | Over-extraction, pollution |
| **Sulphur (S)** | Rocks, ocean sediments | Acid rain (SO₂ emissions) |

### Food Chain Structure

```
Sun → Producers → Primary Consumers → Secondary Consumers → Tertiary Consumers → Decomposers
         ↓              ↓                    ↓                     ↓                  ↓
      (Plants)      (Herbivores)          (Carnivores)         (Top carnivores)    (Bacteria/Fungi)
```

## Important Facts

- **Energy flow** is unidirectional; **nutrients** cycle repeatedly.
- **10% energy transfer** between trophic levels (Lindeman's Law).
- **Detritus food chain** begins with dead organic matter (vs grazing food chain from living plants).
- **Grazing food chain** = dominant in most ecosystems.
- **Phosphorus cycle** is **sedimentary** (slow, no gaseous phase).
- **Nitrogen cycle** involves fixation, nitrification, denitrification, ammonification.

# Biotic and Abiotic Components

## Definition

**Biotic components** are all **living organisms** in an ecosystem — producers, consumers, and decomposers. **Abiotic components** are all **non-living physical and chemical factors** — sunlight, temperature, water, air, soil, minerals, pH, salinity — that influence living organisms.

Together they constitute the total environment of an ecosystem.

## Explanation

### Biotic Components (3 Groups)

| Group | Type | Examples | Role |
|-------|------|----------|------|
| **Producers** | Autotrophs | Green plants, algae, cyanobacteria, chemosynthetic bacteria | Photosynthesis/chemosynthesis → food |
| **Consumers** | Heterotrophs | Herbivores, carnivores, omnivores | Feed on other organisms |
| **Decomposers** | Saprotrophs | Bacteria, fungi | Break down dead organic matter |

**Consumer sub-types:**
- **Primary consumers** — Herbivores (deer, rabbit, zooplankton)
- **Secondary consumers** — Carnivores eating herbivores (snake, frog)
- **Tertiary consumers** — Top carnivores (tiger, hawk, eagle)
- **Detritivores** — Feed on detritus (earthworm, termite, vulture)

### Abiotic Components

| Factor | Influence |
|--------|-----------|
| **Solar radiation** | Energy source for photosynthesis |
| **Temperature** | Metabolic rates, species distribution |
| **Water** | Universal medium for biochemical reactions |
| **Atmospheric gases** | O₂, CO₂, N₂ — respiration, photosynthesis, N-fixation |
| **Soil** | Nutrients, anchorage, water retention |
| **pH** | Affects enzyme activity, species tolerance |
| **Salinity** | Critical in aquatic ecosystems |
| **Wind** | Seed dispersal, evaporation, pollination |
| **Topography** | Altitude, slope — microclimate creation |

### Interaction Between Biotic and Abiotic

- Plants (biotic) modify CO₂ levels (abiotic atmosphere).
- Decomposers release minerals into soil (abiotic).
- Temperature (abiotic) limits species distribution (biotic).
- Beavers (biotic) create wetlands (abiotic water bodies).

## Important Facts

- **99% of ecosystem energy** comes from the sun via producers.
- **Chemosynthetic bacteria** (e.g., near hydrothermal vents) are producers without sunlight.
- **Rhizobium** bacteria fix nitrogen — critical biotic-abiotic interaction.
- **Mycorrhizal fungi** help plants absorb phosphorus from soil.
- **Keystone species** disproportionately affect ecosystem structure.
- **pH 6.5–7.5** optimal for most crops; **acid rain** lowers soil pH.

# Ecological Niche

## Definition

An **ecological niche** is the **functional role and position** of a species in its ecosystem — encompassing its **habitat**, **food habits**, **breeding behaviour**, **activity period**, and **interactions** with other species. It is often described as the species' **"profession"** in the ecosystem (as opposed to habitat = "address").

**Grinnell (1917)** — niche as habitat concept.  
**Elton (1927)** — niche as ecological role.  
**Hutchinson (1957)** — **n-dimensional hypervolume** niche model (most accepted).

**Habitat vs Niche:**
| Term | Analogy | Meaning |
|------|---------|---------|
| **Habitat** | Address | Physical place where organism lives |
| **Niche** | Profession | Functional role in ecosystem |

## Explanation

### Types of Niches

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Fundamental niche** | Full range of conditions a species *could* occupy (without competition) |
| **Realised niche** | Actual range occupied after competition and predation |
| **Spatial niche** | Physical space used |
| **Functional niche** | Role in energy flow and nutrient cycling |

### Niche Parameters (Hutchinson's n-dimensional model)

- Temperature tolerance range
- Humidity requirements
- Food type and size
- Breeding season
- Predators and prey
- Activity time (diurnal/nocturnal)
- pH tolerance

### Competitive Exclusion Principle (Gause's Law, 1934)

> *"Two species competing for the same niche cannot coexist indefinitely; the inferior competitor will be eliminated or forced to shift its niche."*

- Explains **resource partitioning** — similar species coexist by using slightly different resources.
- Example: Different warbler species feed at different heights in the same tree.

### Niche Overlap and Resource Partitioning

```
Same Habitat, Different Niches:
  Tree canopy → Insectivorous birds at different heights
  River → Fish species feeding at different depths/times
  Forest → Lions (day hunters) vs Leopards (night hunters)
```

## Important Facts

- **Realised niche ≤ Fundamental niche** always.
- **Competitive Exclusion Principle** = Gause's Law (1934).
- **Resource partitioning** allows species coexistence.
- **Empty niche** concept — unfilled functional roles in ecosystems.
- **Invasive species** often succeed by occupying vacant niches.
- **Keystone species** have niches disproportionately affecting ecosystem.

# Ecological Principles

## Definition

**Ecological principles** are the fundamental **laws and rules** governing the organisation, function, and dynamics of ecosystems. They form the scientific foundation of ecology and environmental management.

## Explanation

### Core Ecological Principles

| Principle | Description | Proponent/Year |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| **Everything is connected** | All organisms and factors are interlinked in ecosystems | Holism |
| **Energy flows unidirectionally** | Energy enters as sunlight, exits as heat; not recycled | Lindeman (1942) |
| **Nutrients cycle** | Chemical elements circulate between biotic and abiotic compartments | Biogeochemical cycles |
| **10% Energy Transfer Law** | Only ~10% energy passes to next trophic level | Lindeman (1942) |
| **Competitive Exclusion** | Two species cannot share identical niche indefinitely | Gause (1934) |
| **Liebig's Law of Minimum** | Growth limited by scarcest essential resource | Liebig (1840) |
| **Shelford's Law of Tolerance** | Each factor has minimum, maximum, and optimum tolerance range | Shelford (1913) |
| **Ecological Succession** | Ecosystems develop predictably over time | Clements (1916) |
| **Carrying Capacity** | Maximum population sustainable by environment | Population ecology |
| **Precautionary Principle** | Uncertainty should not delay preventive environmental action | Rio Summit (1992) |
| **Polluter Pays Principle** | Polluter bears cost of environmental damage | OECD (1972) |
| **Tragedy of the Commons** | Shared resources overexploited for individual gain | Hardin (1968) |

### Shelford's Law of Tolerance

```
Population
    ↑
    |     ___Optimum Range___
    |    /                   \
    |   /                     \
    |  /  Zone of              \  Zone of
    | /   Physiological         \ Physiological
    |/    Stress                \ Stress
    +──────────────────────────────→ Factor intensity
   Min    Zone of          Zone of    Max
         Intolerance     Intolerance
```

### Liebig's Law of Minimum

- Growth is controlled NOT by total resources but by the **scarcest** essential factor.
- Example: In desert, water is the limiting factor regardless of abundant sunlight.

### Ecosystem Stability Principles

| Principle | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| **Biodiversity-stability hypothesis** | More diverse ecosystems are more stable/resilient |
| **Homeostasis** | Ecosystems self-regulate to maintain equilibrium |
| **Feedback mechanisms** | Negative feedback maintains balance; positive feedback amplifies change |
| **Resistance** | Ability to withstand disturbance |
| **Resilience** | Ability to recover after disturbance |

## Important Facts

- **10% Law** → 90% energy lost as heat at each trophic level.
- **Liebig's Barrel** — staves of different heights; water fills only to shortest stave (limiting factor).
- **Rapoport's Rule** — species geographic range increases with latitude.
- **Bergmann's Rule** — species size increases with latitude (colder climates).
- **Allen's Rule** — appendages smaller in colder climates.
- **Precautionary & Polluter Pays** — now part of Indian environmental jurisprudence.

# Ecological Balance

## Definition

**Ecological balance** (ecological equilibrium/homeostasis) is the **stable state** of an ecosystem maintained through dynamic interactions among biotic and abiotic components, where **energy flow** and **nutrient cycling** operate in harmony, and **population sizes** of species remain relatively constant over time through natural regulatory mechanisms.

## Explanation

### Mechanisms Maintaining Ecological Balance

| Mechanism | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Negative feedback** | Predation controls prey population; disease limits overcrowding |
| **Food web complexity** | Multiple food chains provide stability (if one link breaks, others compensate) |
| **Nutrient cycling** | Decomposers return nutrients; prevents nutrient lock-up |
| **Predator-prey cycles** | Oscillating populations maintain balance (Lotka-Volterra model) |
| **Competition** | Prevents any single species from dominating |
| **Carrying capacity** | Environment limits maximum population |

### Predator-Prey Relationship (Balance Example)

```
High prey → More predators → Prey decreases → Fewer predators → Prey increases → Cycle repeats
```

### Factors Disturbing Ecological Balance

| Factor | Impact |
|--------|--------|
| **Habitat destruction** | Species loss, food web disruption |
| **Pollution** | Biomagnification, species mortality |
| **Invasive species** | Niche displacement, predation on natives |
| **Overexploitation** | Trophic cascade (e.g., overfishing) |
| **Climate change** | Range shifts, phenological mismatch |
| **Deforestation** | Soil erosion, microclimate change |

### Trophic Cascade

- Removal of top predator → herbivore population explosion → vegetation destruction → ecosystem collapse.
- Example: Wolf removal in Yellowstone → elk overgrazing → riverbank erosion.

### Restoration of Balance

- **Reintroduction of predators** (wolves in Yellowstone)
- **Invasive species removal** (Lantana clearing in Indian forests)
- **Afforestation** and habitat corridors
- **Pollution control** and waste management

## Important Facts

- Ecological balance is **dynamic**, not static — populations fluctuate within limits.
- **Biodiversity** enhances ecological balance (stability-resilience hypothesis).
- **Keystone species** removal can collapse ecological balance.
- **Biomagnification** of toxins (DDT, mercury) disrupts balance at top trophic levels.
- **Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962)** — documented pesticide disruption of ecological balance.
- **Eutrophication** disrupts aquatic ecological balance.

# Ecological Succession

## Definition

**Ecological succession** is the **predictable, orderly, and sequential change** in the species composition and community structure of an ecosystem over time, leading from a **pioneer community** to a **climax community** in equilibrium with the regional climate.

**Clements (1916)** — **Climax theory** (succession ends at stable climax community).  
**Gleason (1926)** — **Individualistic concept** (species respond independently, no fixed climax).

## Explanation

### Types of Succession

| Type | Basis | Description |
|------|-------|-------------|
| **Primary succession** | On bare substrate with no prior life | Volcanic lava, glacial retreat, sand dunes |
| **Secondary succession** | On previously colonised but disturbed site | After fire, deforestation, abandoned farmland |
| **Autogenic succession** | Driven by organisms themselves | Plant roots break rock; organic matter builds soil |
| **Allogenic succession** | Driven by external forces | Climate change, flooding, volcanic eruption |
| **Hydrarch succession** | In water/wet habitats | Pond → marsh → meadow → forest |
| **Xerarch succession** | On dry/bare rock | Lichens → mosses → herbs → shrubs → forest |

### Stages of Succession

```
Pioneer Species (r-selected: fast growth, short life)
        ↓
Sub-seral / Intermediate Communities
        ↓
Climax Community (K-selected: stable, diverse, slow-changing)
```

| Stage | Species Characteristics |
|-------|------------------------|
| **Pioneer** | Lichens, mosses, grasses — tolerate harsh conditions |
| **Early seral** | Ferns, herbs, shrubs — soil formation begins |
| **Mid seral** | Small trees, more complex community |
| **Late seral** | Large trees, high biodiversity |
| **Climax** | Stable, self-perpetuating community (e.g., tropical rainforest) |

### Primary vs Secondary Succession

| Feature | Primary | Secondary |
|---------|---------|-----------|
| **Substrate** | Bare rock, sand, lava | Soil already present |
| **Speed** | Very slow (hundreds to thousands of years) | Faster (decades to centuries) |
| **Pioneer** | Lichens (on rock) | Grasses, weeds |
| **Example** | Volcanic island colonisation | Forest after wildfire |

### Hydrarch Succession (Pond to Forest)

```
Open Water (phytoplankton)
  → Reed-Swamp Stage (emergent vegetation)
    → Sedge-Meadow Stage
      → Woodland Stage
        → Climax Forest
```

### r-selected vs K-selected Species in Succession

| Trait | r-selected (Pioneer) | K-selected (Climax) |
|-------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Reproduction | Rapid, many offspring | Slow, few offspring |
| Size | Small | Large |
| Lifespan | Short | Long |
| Competition | Low tolerance | High tolerance |
| Examples | Lichens, weeds, insects | Trees, large mammals |

## Important Facts

- **Pioneer species** = **r-strategists** (opportunistic, colonising).
- **Climax species** = **K-strategists** (competitive, stable).
- **Sere** = entire sequence of successional stages.
- **Serule** = individual stage in succession.
- **Climax community** remains stable until major disturbance.
- **Secondary succession** is more common than primary in nature.
- **Lichens** are classic pioneers in primary succession (secrete acids, break rock → soil formation).

```
       Hydrarch (water→land) | Xerarch (dry rock→forest)

STAGES: Pioneer (r-selected) → Seral → Climax (K-selected, stable)

PIONEERS: Lichens, mosses, grasses
CLIMAX: Tropical rainforest, mature forest

Clements (1916) = Climax theory | Gleason (1926) = Individualistic
Sere = full sequence | Serule = one stage
```

## Memory Tricks

- **Primary = Primitive land** (no soil, bare rock)
- **Secondary = Soil ready** (faster recovery)
- **Hydrarch = Hydro (water)** succession
- **Xerarch = Zero water** (dry rock) succession
- **r-selected = Rapid reproducers** (pioneers) | **K-selected = Keen competitors** (climax)

# Ecosystem Services

## Definition

**Ecosystem services** are the **benefits that humans derive from ecosystems** — the goods and services provided by natural systems that sustain and fulfil human life. Formalised by the **Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005)**.

**Four Categories (MEA, 2005):**

| Category | Description |
|----------|-------------|

| **Regulating Services** | Benefits from ecosystem regulation (climate, floods, disease, water quality) |
| **Supporting Services** | Fundamental processes (nutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production) |
| **Cultural Services** | Non-material benefits (recreation, aesthetic, spiritual, educational) |

# Ecosystem Productivity

## Definition

**Ecosystem productivity** is the **rate of biomass production** (organic matter synthesis) in an ecosystem per unit area per unit time, driven primarily by **primary producers** converting solar energy (or chemical energy) into organic compounds through photosynthesis or chemosynthesis.

**Key terms:**
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **GPP (Gross Primary Productivity)** | Total organic matter produced by autotrophs |
| **NPP (Net Primary Productivity)** | GPP minus energy used in respiration (R); available for consumers |
| **NPP = GPP − R** | |
| **Secondary Productivity** | Biomass produced by heterotrophs (consumers) |
| **Standing Crop/Biomass** | Total biomass present at a given time (not a rate) |

**Units:** g/m²/year or kcal/m²/year

## Explanation

### Productivity Hierarchy

```
Solar Energy (100%)
    ↓
GPP (Gross Primary Productivity) — total photosynthesis
    ↓ − Respiration (R)
NPP (Net Primary Productivity) — available for ecosystem
    ↓
Secondary Productivity — consumer biomass production
    ↓
~90% lost as heat at each trophic level (10% Law)
```

### Factors Affecting Productivity

| Factor | Effect |
|--------|--------|
| **Sunlight** | Primary energy input; limits productivity at high latitudes |
| **Water availability** | Critical limiting factor in arid regions |
| **Temperature** | Affects photosynthetic rate and growing season length |
| **Nutrient availability** | N, P, K limit productivity (especially in oceans) |
| **CO₂ concentration** | Enhanced photosynthesis (fertilisation effect) |
| **Soil quality** | Determines terrestrial productivity |

### Productivity of Major Ecosystems

| Ecosystem | NPP (approx. g/m²/yr) | Rank |
|-----------|----------------------|------|
| **Estuaries/Swamps** | 1500–3000 | Highest |
| **Tropical Rainforest** | 1000–2500 | Very High |
| **Mangroves** | 1000–2000 | Very High |
| **Coral Reefs** | 1000–2000 | Very High |
| **Algal Beds** | 1500–2000 | Very High |
| **Temperate Forest** | 600–1500 | Moderate |
| **Savanna/Grassland** | 300–1000 | Moderate |
| **Desert** | <100 | Very Low |
| **Open Ocean** | 50–200 | Lowest |
| **Tundra** | 50–200 | Very Low |

### Why Open Ocean is Least Productive

- Nutrients sink to deep water (not recycled in photic zone).
- Only **photic zone** (upper 200 m) supports photosynthesis.
- **Upwelling zones** (off Peru, California) are exceptions — high productivity.

## Important Facts

- **NPP = GPP − R** — most important formula in productivity.
- **Tropical rainforests** have highest terrestrial NPP.
- **Open ocean** has lowest NPP despite covering 70% of Earth's surface.
- **Estuaries** are the most productive ecosystems overall.
- **Eutrophication** temporarily increases productivity but causes ecosystem collapse.
- **India's NPP** varies from high (Western Ghats) to low (Thar Desert).
- **10% of NPP** is consumed by herbivores globally; rest goes to decomposers.

# Ecological Efficiency

## Definition

**Ecological efficiency** is the **percentage of energy or biomass transferred** from one trophic level to the next higher trophic level in an ecosystem. It determines how many trophic levels an ecosystem can support and the biomass at each level.

**Lindeman's 10% Law (1942):** On average, only about **10% of energy** at one trophic level is transferred to the next; the remaining **90% is lost** as heat through respiration, metabolism, and waste.

## Explanation

### Types of Ecological Efficiency

| Type | Formula | Description |
|------|---------|-------------|
| **Assimilation Efficiency (AE)** | (Energy assimilated / Energy ingested) × 100 | How much consumed food is digested |
| **Net Production Efficiency (NPE)** | (Production / Assimilation) × 100 | How much assimilated energy goes to growth |
| **Ecological Efficiency (EE)** | AE × NPE (or Production/Ingested × 100) | Overall transfer between trophic levels |
| **Trophic Level Efficiency** | ~10% (Lindeman's Law) | Energy transfer between trophic levels |

### Energy Loss at Each Trophic Level

```
1000 kcal (Producers/Plants)
    ↓ 10% transferred
100 kcal (Primary Consumers/Herbivores)
    ↓ 10% transferred
10 kcal (Secondary Consumers/Carnivores)
    ↓ 10% transferred
1 kcal (Tertiary Consumers/Top Carnivores)
```

**90% lost at each level through:**
- **Respiration** (metabolic heat)
- **Excretion** (undigested waste)
- **Movement** (locomotion energy)
- **Heat loss** (second law of thermodynamics)

### Ecological Pyramids Reflect Efficiency

| Pyramid Type | Shape | Reason |
|-------------|-------|--------|
| **Pyramid of Energy** | Always upright | Energy decreases at each level (10% law) |
| **Pyramid of Biomass** | Usually upright | Biomass decreases at higher levels |
| **Pyramid of Numbers** | Upright or inverted | Depends on organism size |

### Why Food Chains are Short (3–5 trophic levels)

- With 10% efficiency, energy at 5th trophic level = 0.01% of producer energy.
- Insufficient energy to support more levels.
- Top predators (tiger, eagle) are few in number due to energy limitation.

### Exceptions to 10% Rule

| Ecosystem | Efficiency | Reason |
|-----------|-----------|--------|
| **Aquatic (phytoplankton-zooplankton)** | Up to 20% | Rapid turnover, small size |
| **Terrestrial (trees-insects)** | As low as 5% | Large biomass, less digestible |

## Important Facts

- **Lindeman (1942)** proposed 10% energy transfer law.
- **Energy pyramid** is **always upright** — never inverted.
- **Biomass pyramid** can be **inverted** (e.g., phytoplankton in ocean — rapid turnover).
- **Numbers pyramid** can be **inverted** (e.g., one tree supports thousands of insects).
- **Maximum 4–5 trophic levels** in most ecosystems due to energy loss.
- **Ecological efficiency** determines the **length of food chains**.

# Genetic Level Ecology

## Definition

**Genetic level ecology** (also called **molecular ecology** or **autecology at genetic level**) studies ecological processes and patterns at the level of **genes, genotypes, and genetic variation** within and between populations. It examines how **genetic diversity** influences adaptation, survival, and ecosystem function.

This is the **most fundamental level** of ecological organisation — the base of the hierarchy:
**Gene → Individual → Population → Community → Ecosystem → Biome → Biosphere**

## Explanation

### Key Concepts

| Concept | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Genetic diversity** | Variety of genes and alleles within a species/population |
| **Gene pool** | Total collection of genes in an interbreeding population |
| **Allele frequency** | Proportion of specific allele in gene pool |
| **Genetic drift** | Random change in allele frequency (especially in small populations) |
| **Gene flow** | Transfer of alleles between populations through migration |
| **Natural selection** | Differential survival of genotypes best adapted to environment |
| **Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium** | Allele frequencies remain constant without evolutionary forces |
| **Inbreeding depression** | Reduced fitness due to mating between related individuals |
| **Outbreeding enhancement** | Increased fitness from genetic diversity |

### Hardy-Weinberg Principle

**Conditions for equilibrium (no evolution):**
- Large population size
- Random mating
- No mutation
- No gene flow (migration)
- No natural selection

**Formula:** p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (where p and q are allele frequencies)

### Genetic Diversity and Ecosystem Health

| Factor | Impact |
|--------|--------|
| **High genetic diversity** | Greater adaptability to environmental change |
| **Low genetic diversity** | Vulnerability to disease, climate change, inbreeding |
| **Genetic bottleneck** | Sharp reduction in population → loss of genetic diversity |
| **Founder effect** | Small group colonises new area with limited gene pool |

### Molecular Ecology Tools

| Tool | Application |
|------|-------------|
| **DNA barcoding** | Species identification using genetic markers |
| **Microsatellite analysis** | Population genetic structure |
| **Whole genome sequencing** | Comprehensive genetic diversity assessment |
| **Environmental DNA (eDNA)** | Detect species from DNA in water/soil samples |

### Conservation Genetics

- **Minimum viable population (MVP)** — smallest population with 95% survival probability for 100 years.
- **50/500 rule** — Need 50 individuals to avoid inbreeding depression; 500 for long-term genetic viability.
- **Captive breeding programmes** use genetic management to maintain diversity (e.g., Project Tiger).

## Important Facts

- **Genetic diversity** is the **basis of all other biodiversity levels**.
- **Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium** is theoretical — rarely achieved in nature.
- **Cheetah** has extremely low genetic diversity — population bottleneck ~10,000 years ago.
- **Northern white rhino** — functionally extinct; only 2 females, no males (genetic dead end).
- **DNA barcoding** standard gene: **COI (Cytochrome Oxidase I)** for animals.
- **India's genetic resources** governed by **Biological Diversity Act, 2002** and **NBA**.
- **50/500 rule** widely used in conservation genetics.

# Species Level Ecology

## Definition

**Species level ecology** (also called **population ecology** or **autecology**) is the study of **individual species** in relation to their environment, focusing on **population dynamics** — birth rates, death rates, immigration, emigration, population growth, distribution, and interactions with other species.

A **species** is a group of organisms capable of interbreeding and producing fertile offspring in nature.

## Explanation

### Population Parameters

| Parameter | Symbol | Description |
|-----------|--------|-------------|
| **Birth rate (Natality)** | B | New individuals produced per unit time |
| **Death rate (Mortality)** | D | Deaths per unit time |
| **Immigration** | I | Individuals entering population |
| **Emigration** | E | Individuals leaving population |
| **Population growth** | ΔN/Δt | (B + I) − (D + E) |

### Population Growth Models

| Model | Formula/Pattern | Conditions |
|-------|----------------|------------|
| **Exponential (J-curve)** | Unlimited growth | Unlimited resources, no predators |
| **Logistic (S-curve)** | Growth slows at carrying capacity (K) | Limited resources, density-dependent |
| **r-strategists** | Rapid exponential growth | Unstable environments, many offspring |
| **K-strategists** | Logistic growth near K | Stable environments, few offspring |

### Logistic Growth Equation

**dN/dt = rN(K−N)/K**

- **r** = intrinsic rate of natural increase
- **K** = carrying capacity
- **N** = population size at time t

### Species Interactions

| Interaction | Effect on Species A | Effect on Species B | Example |
|-------------|--------------------|--------------------|---------|
| **Mutualism (+/+)** | Benefited | Benefited | Bee-flower pollination |
| **Commensalism (+/0)** | Benefited | Unaffected | Cattle egret and grazing cattle |
| **Parasitism (+/−)** | Benefited | Harmed | Tick on deer |
| **Predation (+/−)** | Benefited | Harmed | Tiger kills deer |
| **Competition (−/−)** | Harmed | Harmed | Lions vs cheetahs |
| **Amensalism (−/0)** | Harmed | Unaffected | Penicillium secretes antibiotic |
| **Neutralism (0/0)** | Unaffected | Unaffected | Rare in nature |

### Population Regulation

| Type | Factors |
|------|---------|
| **Density-dependent** | Competition, predation, disease, parasitism (act more strongly as population increases) |
| **Density-independent** | Natural disasters, climate extremes, habitat destruction (affect regardless of density) |

### Species Distribution

| Pattern | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Endemic** | Found only in a specific geographic area |
| **Cosmopolitan** | Found worldwide |
| **Disjunct distribution** | Separated geographic ranges |
| **Migratory** | Seasonal movement between habitats |

### Conservation Status (IUCN Red List)

| Category | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| **EX** | Extinct |
| **EW** | Extinct in Wild |
| **CR** | Critically Endangered |
| **EN** | Endangered |
| **VU** | Vulnerable |
| **NT** | Near Threatened |
| **LC** | Least Concern |
| **DD** | Data Deficient |

## Important Facts

- **Carrying capacity (K)** = maximum sustainable population.
- **r-strategists** (mosquitoes, weeds) vs **K-strategists** (elephants, whales).
- **J-curve** = exponential growth (population crash likely).
- **S-curve** = logistic growth (stable at K).
- **India has 7.6% of world's mammal species, 12.6% of bird species.**
- **Endemic species** concentrated in Western Ghats, Andaman & Nicobar, NE India.
- **IUCN Red List** assesses species extinction risk globally.
- **Population viability analysis (PVA)** predicts extinction probability.

# Community Level Ecology

## Definition

**Community level ecology** (synecology) is the study of **groups of different species (biotic communities)** living together in a defined area, focusing on **species diversity, species interactions, community structure, and community dynamics** including succession, invasion, and assembly.

A **community** is an assemblage of populations of different species living in the same habitat and interacting with each other.

## Explanation

### Community Characteristics

| Characteristic | Description |
|----------------|-------------|
| **Species richness** | Number of different species in community |
| **Species evenness** | Relative abundance of each species |
| **Species diversity** | Combination of richness and evenness (Shannon-Wiener Index) |
| **Dominant species** | Most abundant/high biomass species |
| **Keystone species** | Disproportionately large effect on community (may not be dominant) |
| **Foundation species** | Create/modify habitat for others (e.g., corals, trees) |
| **Indicator species** | Sensitive to environmental changes (e.g., lichens for air pollution) |
| **Invasive species** | Non-native species causing ecological/economic harm |

### Species Diversity Indices

| Index | Measures |
|-------|----------|
| **Shannon-Wiener Index (H')** | Both richness and evenness |
| **Simpson's Index (D)** | Probability two randomly chosen individuals are same species |
| **Margalef's Index** | Species richness relative to sample size |

**Higher H' value = Greater diversity**

### Community Structure

| Aspect | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Vertical stratification** | Layers in community (forest canopy, understory, floor) |
| **Horizontal zonation** | Zones across landscape (pond: littoral, limnetic, profundal) |
| **Trophic structure** | Feeding relationships (food web) |
| **Guild** | Group of species exploiting same resources similarly |

### Ecological Succession at Community Level

- **Pioneer community** → **Seral communities** → **Climax community**
- **Facilitation model** — early species modify environment, enabling later species.
- **Tolerance model** — species colonise based on tolerance, not facilitation.
- **Inhibition model** — early species prevent later colonisation.

### Community Stability and Resilience

| Concept | Definition |
|---------|------------|
| **Resistance** | Ability to remain unchanged despite disturbance |
| **Resilience** | Ability to recover after disturbance |
| **Constancy** | Ability to maintain population levels |
| **Inertia/Persistence** | Ability to avoid extinction |

**Diversity-stability hypothesis:** More diverse communities are more stable and resilient.

### Biomes as Major Communities

| Biome | Dominant Vegetation | Region |
|-------|---------------------|--------|
| Tropical Rainforest | Broad-leaved evergreen trees | Amazon, Congo, Western Ghats |
| Tropical Deciduous | Teak, sal, deciduous trees | Central India |
| Temperate Deciduous | Oak, maple | Europe, NE USA |
| Taiga/Boreal | Conifers (pine, spruce) | Canada, Russia, Scandinavia |
| Tundra | Mosses, lichens, dwarf shrubs | Arctic, Alpine |
| Savanna | Grasses + scattered trees | Africa, Deccan Plateau |
| Desert | Xerophytes, cacti | Sahara, Thar |
| Grassland | Grasses | Prairies, Steppes |

### India's Biogeographic Zones (10)

1. Trans-Himalaya  2. Himalaya  3. Desert  4. Semi-arid  5. Western Ghats  
6. Deccan Peninsula  7. Gangetic Plain  8. Coasts  9. Northeast  10. Islands

## Important Facts

- **Tropical rainforests** have highest **species richness** on Earth.
- **Keystone species** concept by **Robert Paine (1969)** — sea star removal collapsed intertidal community.
- **Edge effect** — boundary between two communities has higher species diversity but also higher vulnerability.
- **Ecotone** — transition zone between two communities; often higher diversity than either community.
- **Alpha diversity** = diversity within a community; **Beta diversity** = diversity between communities; **Gamma diversity** = regional diversity.
- **Rivet Popper Hypothesis** — species are like rivets in an aeroplane; losing too many causes crash.
- **India is one of 17 megadiverse countries** (70% of world's biodiversity in 17 countries).
