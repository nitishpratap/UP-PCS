# Stockholm Conference (1972)

## Definition

The **United Nations Conference on the Human Environment (Stockholm Conference, 1972)** was the **first major international conference on environmental issues** — held in **Stockholm, Sweden** from **5–16 June 1972** — marking the beginning of **global environmental diplomacy** and leading to the establishment of **UNEP (United Nations Environment Programme)**.

**Also called:** **Stockholm Conference 1972** or **UNCHE 1972**.

## Explanation

### Background and Significance

| Factor | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **Date** | 5–16 June 1972 |
| **Location** | Stockholm, Sweden |
| **Organiser** | United Nations |
| **Participants** | 122 countries (including India) |
| **Maurice Strong** | Secretary-General of the Conference |
| **Indira Gandhi** | India participated; delivered landmark speech |
| **First of its kind** | First UN conference solely on environment |

### Key Outcomes of Stockholm Conference

| Outcome | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Stockholm Declaration** | 26 principles on human environment |
| **Action Plan** | 109 recommendations for governments |
| **UNEP established** | United Nations Environment Programme (1972) |
| **World Environment Day** | 5 June declared (first WED 1973) |
| **Environmental diplomacy** | Beginning of international environmental law |
| **India's commitment** | India actively participated; later enacted EPA 1986 |

### 26 Principles of Stockholm Declaration — Key Principles

| Principle | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| **Principle 1** | Man has fundamental right to freedom, equality, adequate living conditions in dignity |
| **Principle 2** | Natural resources must be safeguarded |
| **Principle 4** | Man has special responsibility to safeguard and wisely manage wilderness |
| **Principle 7** | States shall take all possible steps to prevent pollution of seas |
| **Principle 11** | Environmental policies should enhance present and future generations |
| **Principle 21** | States have sovereign right to exploit own resources (with responsibility) |
| **Principle 22** | States shall cooperate to develop international law on liability |
| **Principle 26** | Man and his environment must be made the focus of international policies |

### Stockholm and India

| Impact on India | Detail |
|----------------|--------|
| **Indira Gandhi's speech** | "Poverty is the greatest polluter" — famous quote |
| **Art 48A, 51A(g)** | Added to Constitution (1976) — environmental provisions |

| **Water Act 1974** | Predates Stockholm but aligned with global movement |

| **World Environment Day** | India celebrates 5 June annually |

## Important Facts

- **5–16 June 1972** — Stockholm Conference dates.
- **First UN environment conference** — landmark in environmental history.
- **UNEP established** — as outcome of Stockholm Conference.
- **5 June** — World Environment Day (since 1973).
- **26 principles** — Stockholm Declaration.
- **Indira Gandhi** — represented India; famous "poverty is greatest polluter" quote.
- **122 countries** participated.
- **Maurice Strong** — Conference Secretary-General.
- **Led to** — Rio Earth Summit 1992 (20 years later).
- **India** — among active participants; shaped domestic environmental law.

# Brundtland Commission

## Definition

The **Brundtland Commission** (formally the **World Commission on Environment and Development, WCED**) was an independent commission established by the **United Nations in 1983** — chaired by **Norwegian Prime Minister Gro Harlem Brundtland** — which published the landmark report **"Our Common Future" (1987)** defining **Sustainable Development**.

**Report published:** 1987.  
**Key contribution:** Coined and defined **"Sustainable Development"**.

## Explanation

### Brundtland Commission — Key Details

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Established** | 1983 by UN General Assembly |
| **Chairperson** | Gro Harlem Brundtland (Norway) |
| **Report** | "Our Common Future" (1987) |
| **Duration** | 1983–1987 (4 years) |
| **Mandate** | Reconcile economic development with environmental protection |
| **Members** | 21 commissioners from 21 countries |

### Definition of Sustainable Development (Brundtland)

> **"Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."**

| Component | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| **Needs of present** | Essential needs of world's poor — priority |
| **Future generations** | Do not compromise their ability to meet needs |
| **Limits** | Technology and social organisation can manage environmental limits |
| **Participation** | All citizens involved in development decisions |

### Three Pillars of Sustainable Development

```
        SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT
               /    |    \
              /     |     \
             /      |      \
        ECONOMIC  SOCIAL  ENVIRONMENTAL
        (Growth) (Equity)  (Protection)
             \      |      /
              \     |     /
               \    |    /
            INTERGENERATIONAL EQUITY
         (Present needs + Future needs)
```

| Pillar | Focus |
|--------|-------|
| **Economic** | Growth, productivity, employment, income |
| **Social** | Equity, poverty reduction, education, health |
| **Environmental** | Resource conservation, pollution control, biodiversity |

### Key Recommendations of "Our Common Future"

| Recommendation | Detail |
|---------------|--------|
| **Sustainable Development** | New development paradigm |
| **Population policy** | Slow population growth |
| **Food security** | Sustainable agriculture |
| **Energy transition** | Renewable energy, efficiency |
| **Industrial reform** | Cleaner production |
| **Urban planning** | Sustainable cities |
| **International cooperation** | Global environmental governance |
| **Institutional reform** | Strengthen UNEP; new financial mechanisms |

## Important Facts

- **Established 1983** — by UN General Assembly.
- **Chairperson** — Gro Harlem Brundtland (Norway).

- **Defined Sustainable Development** — most cited definition globally.

# Rio Earth Summit (1992)

## Definition

The **United Nations Conference on Environment and Development (UNCED)**, commonly known as the **Rio Earth Summit (1992)**, was a landmark international conference held in **Rio de Janeiro, Brazil** from **3–14 June 1992** — establishing **Sustainable Development** as the global development paradigm and leading to the signing of **CBD, UNFCCC, and Agenda 21**.

**Also called:** **Rio Summit 1992**, **UNCED 1992**, or **Earth Summit**.

## Explanation

### Rio Earth Summit — Key Details

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Date** | 3–14 June 1992 |
| **Location** | Rio de Janeiro, Brazil |
| **Official name** | UN Conference on Environment and Development (UNCED) |
| **Participants** | 172 governments; 108 heads of state |
| **Secretary-General** | Maurice Strong |
| **Theme** | Sustainable Development (Brundtland concept) |
| **20 years after** | Stockholm Conference 1972 |

### Major Outcomes of Rio Earth Summit

| Outcome | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Rio Declaration** | 27 principles on environment and development |
| **Agenda 21** | Global action plan for sustainable development (40 chapters) |
| **CBD signed** | Convention on Biological Diversity opened for signature |
| **UNFCCC signed** | UN Framework Convention on Climate Change opened for signature |
| **Forest Principles** | Non-legally binding forest management principles |
| **Commission on SD** | UN Commission on Sustainable Development (CSD) established |
| **GEF** | Global Environment Facility operationalised |

### Rio Declaration — 27 Principles (Key)

| Principle | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| **Principle 1** | Human beings are at centre of sustainable development |
| **Principle 3** | Right to development must be fulfilled equitably |
| **Principle 4** | Environmental protection integral to development |
| **Principle 7** | Common but Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR) |
| **Principle 9** | Exchange of scientific and technological knowledge |
| **Principle 10** | Public participation in environmental decision-making |
| **Principle 15** | Precautionary approach |
| **Principle 17** | EIA as national instrument |
| **Principle 22** | Indigenous peoples and their communities |

### Treaties Signed at Rio 1992

| Treaty | Subject | India Status |
|--------|---------|-------------|
| **CBD** | Biodiversity conservation | Signed; ratified 1994; BDA 2002 |
| **UNFCCC** | Climate change | Signed; ratified 1993 |
| **Agenda 21** | SD action plan | India adopted National Agenda 21 |
| **Forest Principles** | Forest management | Non-binding; India adopted |

### Rio Institutional Outcomes

| Institution | Role |
|------------|------|
| **CSD** | UN Commission on Sustainable Development (1992–2013) |
| **GEF** | Global Environment Facility — funds environmental projects |
| **CBD Secretariat** | Montreal, Canada |
| **UNFCCC Secretariat** | Bonn, Germany |
| **HLPF** | High-Level Political Forum (replaced CSD in 2013) |

### Rio and India

| India's Rio Commitments | Detail |
|------------------------|--------|
| **CBD signed** | Ratified 1994; BDA 2002 enacted |
| **UNFCCC signed** | Ratified 1993; NAPCC 2008 |
| **Agenda 21** | India prepared National Report on Agenda 21 |
| **PM attended** | P.V. Narasimha Rao represented India |
| **NGT Act 2010** | Rio commitment to environmental tribunal |
| **EIA Notification 2006** | Rio Principle 17 (EIA) implemented |
| **NAPCC 2008** | 8 climate missions under UNFCCC |

### Rio to Paris — Climate Timeline

```
RIO 1992 → UNFCCC signed
        ↓
UNFCCC entered force 1994
        ↓
KYOTO PROTOCOL 1997 (binding targets)
        ↓
COPENHAGEN 2009 (failed binding agreement)
        ↓
DURBAN 2011 (Paris mandate)
        ↓
PARIS AGREEMENT 2015 (NDCs, 1.5°C target)
        ↓
COP28 2023 (Global Stocktake)
```

## Important Facts

- **3–14 June 1992** — Rio Earth Summit dates.
- **172 governments** participated; 108 heads of state.
- **CBD and UNFCCC** — opened for signature at Rio.
- **Agenda 21** — 40-chapter global SD action plan.
- **Rio Declaration** — 27 principles.
- **CBDR principle** — Common but Differentiated Responsibilities (Principle 7).
- **20 years after Stockholm** — continued environmental diplomacy.
- **Maurice Strong** — Secretary-General (also Stockholm 1972).
- **India signed both CBD and UNFCCC** at Rio.
- **CSD replaced by HLPF** in 2013.

# Agenda 21

## Definition

**Agenda 21** is a **comprehensive global action plan for Sustainable Development** adopted at the **Rio Earth Summit (1992)** — comprising **40 chapters** covering social, economic, and environmental dimensions of development for the **21st century**, to be implemented at **local, national, and global levels**.

**Adopted:** Rio Earth Summit, 1992.  
**Scope:** Local, national, regional, and global levels.

## Explanation

### Agenda 21 — Structure (40 Chapters in 4 Sections)

| Section | Chapters | Focus |
|---------|----------|-------|
| **Section I: Social & Economic** | Ch 2–8 | International cooperation, poverty, consumption, population, health, settlements |
| **Section II: Conservation & Management** | Ch 9–22 | Atmosphere, land, forests, biodiversity, water, oceans, waste |
| **Section III: Strengthening Role of Groups** | Ch 23–32 | Women, youth, indigenous peoples, NGOs, local authorities, workers, business, science |
| **Section IV: Means of Implementation** | Ch 33–40 | Finance, technology transfer, institutions, legal instruments, information |

### Key Chapters of Agenda 21

| Chapter | Subject |
|---------|---------|
| **Chapter 2** | International cooperation for accelerating SD |
| **Chapter 7** | Promoting sustainable human settlements |
| **Chapter 8** | Integrating environment and development in decision-making |
| **Chapter 11** | Combating deforestation |
| **Chapter 13** | Managing fragile ecosystems — combating desertification |
| **Chapter 15** | Conservation of biological diversity |
| **Chapter 17** | Protection of oceans and coastal areas |
| **Chapter 18** | Freshwater resources — protection and management |
| **Chapter 19** | Toxic chemicals — environmentally sound management |
| **Chapter 21** | Environmentally sound management of solid waste |
| **Chapter 28** | Local authorities' initiatives in support of Agenda 21 |
| **Chapter 33** | Financial resources and mechanisms |
| **Chapter 37** | National mechanisms and international cooperation for capacity-building |

### Three Levels of Agenda 21 Implementation

```
GLOBAL LEVEL
  → UN agencies, GEF, international treaties
        ↓
NATIONAL LEVEL
  → National Agenda 21, government policies, laws
        ↓
LOCAL LEVEL
  → Local Agenda 21, municipalities, panchayats, communities
```

| Level | Implementation |
|-------|---------------|
| **Global** | UN programmes, GEF funding, international treaties |
| **National** | National policies, legislation, action plans |
| **Local** | Local Agenda 21 — municipalities, gram panchayats |

### Agenda 21 → SDGs Evolution

```

        ↓

        ↓
RIO+20 (2012) — SDG process initiated
        ↓
SDGs 2030 (2015) — 17 goals, 169 targets
        ↓
Agenda 21 vision operationalised through SDGs
```

## Important Facts

- **21st century action plan** — hence "Agenda 21".
- **Four sections** — Social/Economic, Conservation, Stakeholders, Implementation.
- **Local Agenda 21** — Chapter 28 emphasises local authorities.
- **Non-binding** — but politically committed framework.
- **India submitted National Report** — on Agenda 21 implementation.
- **SDGs 2015** — successor framework to Agenda 21.
- **GEF** — financial mechanism for Agenda 21 implementation.
- **Chapter 15** — biodiversity (led to CBD implementation).

# Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD)

## Definition

The **Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD)** is an **international legally binding treaty** adopted at the **Rio Earth Summit (1992)** — aimed at **conservation of biological diversity, sustainable use of its components, and fair and equitable sharing of benefits** arising from genetic resources (the **three objectives of CBD**).

**Opened for signature:** Rio 1992.  
**Entered into force:** 29 December 1993.  
**India:** Signed 1992; ratified **1994**; domestic law — **BDA 2002**.

## Explanation

### Three Objectives of CBD

| Objective | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **1. Conservation** | Conserve biological diversity |
| **2. Sustainable use** | Use components sustainably |
| **3. Benefit sharing** | Fair and equitable sharing of benefits from genetic resources |

### CBD — Key Details

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Adopted** | 22 May 1992 (Rio Earth Summit) |
| **Entered into force** | 29 December 1993 |
| **Parties** | 196 countries (near universal) |
| **Secretariat** | Montreal, Canada |
| **COP** | Conference of Parties — biennial meetings |
| **India ratified** | 1994 |
| **Domestic law** | Biological Diversity Act, 2002 |

| **Nagoya Protocol on ABS** | 2010 | Access and Benefit Sharing of genetic resources |

| **Nagoya Protocol** | Entered force 2014 | Fair benefit sharing with communities |

# Kyoto Protocol

## Definition

The **Kyoto Protocol** is an **international treaty** adopted at **COP3 (1997) in Kyoto, Japan** — under the **UNFCCC framework** — establishing **legally binding emission reduction targets** for **developed countries (Annex I)** for the period **2008–2012** (first commitment period).

**Adopted:** 11 December 1997.  
**Entered into force:** 16 February 2005.  
**First commitment period:** 2008–2012.

## Explanation

### Kyoto Protocol — Key Details

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Adopted** | 11 December 1997 (COP3, Kyoto, Japan) |
| **Entered into force** | 16 February 2005 (after Russia ratified) |
| **Binding targets** | Annex I (developed) countries only |
| **First commitment period** | 2008–2012 |
| **Second commitment period** | 2013–2020 (Doha Amendment) |
| **India** | Non-Annex I — no binding targets |
| **USA** | Signed but never ratified; withdrew |

### Kyoto Mechanisms (Flexible Mechanisms)

| Mechanism | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **International Emissions Trading (IET)** | Trade emission allowances between Annex I countries |
| **Clean Development Mechanism (CDM)** | Developed countries fund emission reduction projects in developing countries |
| **Joint Implementation (JI)** | Annex I countries invest in emission reduction in other Annex I countries |

### Kyoto GHGs Covered (6 Gases)

| Gas | Source |
|-----|--------|
| **CO₂** | Fossil fuel combustion, deforestation |
| **CH₄ (Methane)** | Agriculture, landfills, natural gas |
| **N₂O (Nitrous Oxide)** | Agriculture, industrial processes |
| **HFCs** | Refrigerants, air conditioning |
| **PFCs** | Aluminium production |
| **SF₆** | Electrical equipment |

### Kyoto vs Paris — Comparison

| Feature | Kyoto Protocol | Paris Agreement |
|---------|---------------|----------------|
| **Year** | 1997 | 2015 |
| **Binding targets** | Annex I only (developed) | All countries (NDCs — voluntary) |
| **Developing countries** | No binding targets | NDCs (self-determined) |
| **Compliance** | Legally binding penalties | No penalties; global stocktake |
| **Mechanisms** | CDM, IET, JI | Carbon markets (Article 6) |
| **Status** | First commitment ended 2012 | Active (2016–) |
| **India** | Non-Annex I (no targets) | NDC submitted |

### Kyoto and India

| Feature | India Detail |
|---------|-------------|
| **Non-Annex I** | No binding emission reduction targets |
| **CDM beneficiary** | Major CDM project host country |
| **CDM projects** | Renewable energy, energy efficiency projects |
| **HFC phase-down** | Kigali Amendment (2016) — related but separate |
| **BASIC group** | Opposed binding targets for developing countries |
| **Kyoto principles** | CBDR upheld — differentiated responsibilities |

### Kyoto Protocol Timeline

```
COP3 KYOTO 1997 → Protocol adopted
        ↓
2005 → Entered force (Russia ratification)
        ↓
2008-2012 → First commitment period
        ↓
2012 Doha → Second commitment period (2013-2020)
        ↓
2015 PARIS → Replaced Kyoto as primary climate agreement
        ↓
2020 → Kyoto first commitment long over; Paris takes over
```

## Important Facts

- **Adopted 11 December 1997** — COP3 Kyoto, Japan.
- **Legally binding** — for Annex I (developed) countries only.
- **India Non-Annex I** — no binding targets.
- **Three mechanisms** — CDM, IET, JI.
- **6 GHGs covered** — CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, HFCs, PFCs, SF₆.
- **First commitment period** — 2008–2012.
- **USA never ratified** — major limitation.
- **CDM** — India major beneficiary of clean development projects.
- **Superseded by Paris 2015** — for future climate action.
- **Doha Amendment 2012** — second commitment period.

# Paris Agreement

## Definition

The **Paris Agreement** is a **legally binding international treaty on climate change** adopted at **COP21 (2015) in Paris, France** — under the **UNFCCC framework** — aiming to limit global warming to **well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels**, pursuing efforts to limit to **1.5°C**, through **Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs)** submitted by all parties.

**Adopted:** 12 December 2015.  
**Entered into force:** 4 November 2016.  
**India's NDC:** 45% emission intensity reduction by 2030; Net Zero by 2070.

## Explanation

### Paris Agreement — Key Details

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Adopted** | 12 December 2015 (COP21, Paris) |
| **Entered into force** | 4 November 2016 |
| **Parties** | 195 countries (near universal) |
| **Temperature target** | Well below 2°C; pursue 1.5°C |
| **Mechanism** | Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) |
| **Review** | Global Stocktake every 5 years |
| **India ratified** | 2 October 2016 (Gandhi Jayanti) |

### Key Features of Paris Agreement

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **NDCs** | Each country sets own emission reduction targets |
| **All countries participate** | Unlike Kyoto (Annex I only) |
| **5-year review cycles** | NDCs updated every 5 years (ratchet mechanism) |
| **Global Stocktake** | Assess collective progress (first in 2023) |
| **Climate finance** | $100 billion/year for developing countries |
| **Loss and Damage** | Address irreversible climate impacts (COP27 fund) |
| **Carbon markets** | Article 6 — international carbon trading |
| **No penalties** | Voluntary compliance; peer pressure |

### India's Paris Agreement Commitments

| Commitment | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **NDC 2015** | 33-35% emission intensity reduction by 2030 (vs 2005) |
| **NDC 2022 update** | 45% emission intensity reduction by 2030 |
| **Non-fossil fuel capacity** | 50% electric installed capacity by 2030 |
| **Carbon sink** | Additional 2.5-3 billion tonnes CO₂eq forest cover |
| **Net Zero** | Net Zero emissions by 2070 (COP26 2021) |
| **Ratified** | 2 October 2016 (Gandhi Jayanti) |
| **ISA** | International Solar Alliance (India-led, 2015) |

### Paris Agreement Structure

```
PARIS AGREEMENT (2015)
        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓
ARTICLE 7: Adaptation to climate impacts
        ↓
ARTICLE 9: Climate finance ($100 billion/year)
        ↓
ARTICLE 13: Transparency framework
        ↓
ARTICLE 14: Global Stocktake (every 5 years)
```

### Paris vs Kyoto — Key Differences

| Feature | Kyoto 1997 | Paris 2015 |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| **Participants** | Annex I only | All 195 countries |
| **Targets** | Legally binding (developed) | Voluntary NDCs (all) |
| **Developing countries** | No targets | NDCs submitted |
| **Compliance** | Penalties | No penalties; stocktake |
| **Flexibility** | CDM, IET, JI | Article 6 carbon markets |
| **Temperature goal** | Not specified | 2°C / 1.5°C |
| **India** | No binding targets | NDC: 45% intensity reduction |

| **Bundelkhand** | Drought adaptation under Paris adaptation goals |

# Montreal Protocol

## Definition

The **Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer** is an **international treaty designed to protect the ozone layer** by **phasing out the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances (ODS)** — considered the **most successful environmental treaty** in history.

**Adopted:** 16 September 1987.  
**Entered into force:** 1 January 1989.  
**India ratified:** 1992.  
**World Ozone Day:** 16 September.

## Explanation

### Montreal Protocol — Key Details

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Adopted** | 16 September 1987 (Montreal, Canada) |
| **Entered into force** | 1 January 1989 |
| **Parties** | 198 countries (universal ratification) |
| **Objective** | Phase out ozone-depleting substances |
| **Secretariat** | UNEP (Nairobi) |
| **India ratified** | 1992 |
| **World Ozone Day** | 16 September |

### Ozone-Depleting Substances (ODS) Controlled

| ODS | Use | Phase-out Status |
|-----|-----|-----------------|
| **CFCs** (Chlorofluorocarbons) | Refrigerants, aerosols | Phased out (1996 developed; 2010 developing) |
| **Halons** | Fire extinguishers | Phased out |
| **Carbon Tetrachloride** | Solvents | Phased out |
| **Methyl Chloroform** | Solvents | Phased out |
| **HCFCs** | Refrigerant substitute | Phase-out by 2040 |
| **Methyl Bromide** | Pesticide | Phase-out ongoing |
| **HFCs** | Refrigerant substitute | Phase-down under Kigali Amendment |

### Montreal Protocol Amendments

| Amendment | Year | Subject |
|-----------|------|---------|
| **London Amendment** | 1990 | Accelerated CFC phase-out |
| **Copenhagen Amendment** | 1992 | HCFC controls |
| **Montreal Amendment** | 1997 | Methyl bromide controls |
| **Beijing Amendment** | 1999 | Bromochloromethane |
| **Kigali Amendment** | 2016 | **HFC phase-down** (climate benefit) |

### Kigali Amendment (2016) — Key Extension

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Adopted** | 2016 (Kigali, Rwanda) |
| **Subject** | Phase-down of HFCs (hydrofluorocarbons) |
| **Climate impact** | HFCs are potent GHGs (1000x CO₂) |
| **India commitment** | Freeze HFC consumption 2028; 85% reduction by 2047 |
| **Dual benefit** | Ozone protection + climate mitigation |
| **Entered force** | 2019 |

### Montreal Protocol Success Story

| Achievement | Detail |
|------------|--------|
| **Universal ratification** | Only treaty ratified by every UN member |
| **Ozone layer recovery** | Projected recovery to 1980 levels by 2060 |
| **Antarctic ozone hole** | Stabilised and slowly recovering |
| **GHG co-benefit** | Prevented 135 billion tonnes CO₂eq (Kigali adds more) |
| **Most successful treaty** | UNEP calls it most effective environmental agreement |

| **Ozone Cell awareness** | World Ozone Day programmes in UP |

## Important Facts

- **16 September** — World Ozone Day (International Day for Preservation of Ozone Layer).

# Ramsar Convention

## Definition

The **Convention on Wetlands of International Importance (Ramsar Convention, 1971)** is an **intergovernmental treaty** for the **conservation and wise use of wetlands** — providing the framework for **national action and international cooperation** regarding wetland conservation and sustainable use.

**Adopted:** 2 February 1971 (Ramsar, Iran).  
**World Wetlands Day:** 2 February.  
**India ratified:** 1981.  
**India's Ramsar sites:** 80+ (as of 2024).

## Explanation

### Ramsar Convention — Key Details

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Adopted** | 2 February 1971 (Ramsar, Iran) |
| **Entered into force** | 21 December 1975 |
| **Parties** | 172 countries |
| **Secretariat** | Gland, Switzerland (IUCN headquarters) |
| **India ratified** | 1981 |
| **World Wetlands Day** | 2 February |
| **India's sites** | 80+ Ramsar sites (2024) |

### Three Pillars of Ramsar Convention

| Pillar | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| **1. Wise use** | Sustainable use of all wetlands |
| **2. Ramsar sites** | Designate and protect wetlands of international importance |
| **3. International cooperation** | Shared wetlands and species management |

### Criteria for Ramsar Site Designation

| Criterion | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Criterion 1** | Representative, rare, or unique wetland type |
| **Criterion 2** | Supports vulnerable, endangered, or critically endangered species |
| **Criterion 3** | Supports biodiversity (120+ waterbird species or 1% population) |
| **Criterion 4** | Supports plant/animal species at critical life stages |
| **Criterion 5** | Regularly supports 20,000+ waterbirds |
| **Criterion 6** | Supports 1% of a waterbird species population |
| **Criterion 7** | Supports significant fish species |
| **Criterion 8** | Important food source, spawning ground, nursery |
| **Criterion 9** | Regularly supports 1% of a fish species population |

# CITES

## Definition

The **Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES)** is an **international agreement** ensuring that **international trade in specimens of wild animals and plants does not threaten their survival** — regulating trade through a **system of permits and certificates**.

**Adopted:** 3 March 1973 (Washington, D.C.).  
**Entered into force:** 1 July 1975.  
**World Wildlife Day:** 3 March.  
**India ratified:** 1976.

## Explanation

### CITES — Key Details

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Adopted** | 3 March 1973 (Washington, D.C.) |
| **Entered into force** | 1 July 1975 |
| **Parties** | 184 countries |
| **Secretariat** | Geneva, Switzerland |
| **India ratified** | 1976 |
| **World Wildlife Day** | 3 March (UN declared 2013) |
| **Administered by** | UNEP (originally IUCN initiative) |

### CITES Appendices — Three Categories

| Appendix | Protection Level | Trade Regulation | Examples |
|----------|-----------------|------------------|----------|
| **Appendix I** | Highest — threatened with extinction | International commercial trade prohibited (except exceptional circumstances) | Tiger, elephant, rhino, lion, orangutan, sea turtles |
| **Appendix II** | Not necessarily threatened now, but trade must be controlled | Trade permitted with export permit | Most CITES species; American ginseng, corals, orchids |
| **Appendix III** | Protected in at least one country | Trade permit required from listing country | India-listed species (blackbuck, nilgai, peacock) |

### CITES vs WPA Schedules — Comparison

| Feature | CITES | WPA 1972 |
|---------|-------|----------|
| **Scope** | International trade | Domestic protection |
| **Categories** | Appendix I, II, III | Schedule I–VI |
| **India law** | Implemented via WPA | Direct domestic law |
| **Tiger** | Appendix I | Schedule I |
| **Elephant** | Appendix I | Schedule I |
| **Peacock** | Not listed (domestic) | Schedule I |
| **Authority** | Management + Scientific Authority | Chief Wildlife Warden |

### CITES Institutional Framework (India)

| Authority | Role |
|-----------|------|
| **Management Authority** | MoEFCC — issues export/import permits |
| **Scientific Authority** | NBWL/ZSI — scientific advice on trade |
| **WCCB** | Wildlife Crime Control Bureau — CITES enforcement |
| **WPA 1972** | Domestic law implementing CITES |
| **Customs** | Border enforcement of CITES permits |

### CITES and India

| Feature | India Detail |
|---------|-------------|
| **Ratified 1976** | Early ratifier |
| **WPA 1972** | Implements CITES domestically |
| **Appendix I species** | Tiger, elephant, rhino, lion, leopard |
| **WCCB** | Enforces CITES anti-trafficking |
| **Ivory trade ban** | Elephant ivory strictly prohibited |
| **Tiger bone trade** | Banned under CITES + WPA |
| **Rosewood (Dalbergia)** | Appendix II — regulated trade |

### CITES and Uttar Pradesh

| Feature | UP Detail |
|---------|----------|
| **Tiger trade** | Dudhwa TR — anti-poaching under CITES |
| **Elephant** | Terai region — ivory trade prevention |
| **Gharial** | Appendix I — Katarniaghat protection |

| **WCCB operations** | Anti-trafficking in UP wildlife markets |
| **Blackbuck** | Appendix III (India-listed) |

## Important Facts

- **Adopted 3 March 1973** — Washington, D.C.
- **3 March** — World Wildlife Day (UN, since 2013).
- **184 parties** — major international wildlife trade treaty.
- **Three Appendices** — I (highest), II, III.
- **India ratified 1976** — WPA 1972 implements CITES.
- **Appendix I** — commercial trade prohibited.
- **WCCB** — enforces CITES in India.
- **Not a conservation convention** — regulates trade only.
- **Secretariat** — Geneva, Switzerland.
- **CoP meetings** — every 3 years.

# Vienna Convention

## Definition

The **Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer (1985)** is the **framework international agreement** relating to the **protection of the ozone layer** — providing the **legal basis for the Montreal Protocol (1987)** which contains the **specific phase-out schedules** for ozone-depleting substances.

**Adopted:** 22 March 1985 (Vienna, Austria).  
**Entered into force:** 22 September 1988.  
**Protocol:** Montreal Protocol 1987.

## Explanation

### Vienna Convention — Key Details

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Adopted** | 22 March 1985 (Vienna, Austria) |
| **Entered into force** | 22 September 1988 |
| **Parties** | 198 countries (universal) |
| **Type** | Framework convention (general obligations) |
| **Protocol** | Montreal Protocol 1987 (specific measures) |
| **Secretariat** | UNEP (Nairobi) |
| **India ratified** | 1991 |

### Vienna Convention vs Montreal Protocol

| Feature | Vienna Convention 1985 | Montreal Protocol 1987 |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Type** | Framework convention | Protocol (specific measures) |
| **Obligations** | General — cooperate, exchange info | Specific — phase-out schedules for ODS |
| **Binding targets** | No specific targets | Legally binding phase-out dates |
| **Substances** | Not specified | CFCs, Halons, HCFCs, etc. |
| **Relationship** | Parent framework | Implements Vienna Convention |
| **Success** | Framework only | Most successful environmental treaty |

### Key Provisions of Vienna Convention

| Provision | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Cooperation** | Parties cooperate on ozone research and monitoring |
| **Information exchange** | Share scientific and technical information |
| **Protocol adoption** | Framework allows adoption of protocols (Montreal 1987) |
| **Technology transfer** | Transfer ozone-friendly technologies to developing countries |
| **Financial mechanism** | Multilateral Fund for developing country compliance |

### Ozone Layer Science

| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| **Ozone layer location** | Stratosphere (15–35 km altitude) |
| **Function** | Absorbs 97–99% of UV-B radiation |
| **ODS impact** | CFCs release chlorine that destroys ozone molecules |
| **Ozone hole** | Antarctic hole discovered 1985 (same year as Vienna Convention) |
| **Recovery** | Expected return to 1980 levels by 2060 (Montreal success) |
| **UV-B harm** | Skin cancer, cataracts, crop damage, immune suppression |

### Vienna-Montreal and India

| Feature | India Detail |
|---------|-------------|
| **Vienna ratified 1991** | Montreal ratified 1992 |
| **Ozone Cell 1991** | Established before ratification |
| **ODS Rules 2000** | Under EPA 1986 — implements both conventions |
| **HPMP** | HCFC Phase-out Management Plan |
| **Kigali Amendment** | HFC phase-down under Montreal |
| **Success** | India met all Montreal phase-out deadlines |

## Important Facts

- **Adopted 22 March 1985** — Vienna, Austria.
- **Framework convention** — Montreal Protocol is the implementing protocol.
- **198 parties** — universal ratification.
- **Ozone hole discovered 1985** — same year as Vienna Convention.
- **Montreal Protocol 1987** — specific ODS phase-out under Vienna.
- **India ratified Vienna 1991** — Montreal 1992.
- **Ozone Cell (MoEFCC)** — India's focal point.
- **ODS Rules 2000** — under EPA 1986.
- **16 September** — World Ozone Day (Montreal adoption).
- **Ozone recovery by 2060** — due to Montreal Protocol success.

# Basel Convention

## Definition

The **Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and Their Disposal (1989)** is an international treaty regulating the **movement of hazardous wastes across national boundaries** — aiming to **minimise generation and transboundary movement** of hazardous wastes and ensure their **environmentally sound management and disposal**.

**Adopted:** 22 March 1989 (Basel, Switzerland).  
**Entered into force:** 5 May 1992.  
**India ratified:** 1992.

## Explanation

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Adopted** | 22 March 1989 (Basel, Switzerland) |
| **Entered into force** | 5 May 1992 |
| **Parties** | 190 countries |
| **Secretariat** | Geneva, Switzerland |
| **India ratified** | 1992 |
| **Focus** | Hazardous waste transboundary movement control |

### Key Principles of Basel Convention

| Principle | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Prior Informed Consent (PIC)** | Importing country must consent before waste shipment |
| **Environmentally sound management** | Wastes must be disposed safely |
| **Minimise generation** | Reduce hazardous waste at source |
| **Domestic treatment priority** | Treat waste in country of generation |
| **Ban Amendment** | Prohibits hazardous waste export from OECD to non-OECD countries |

### Basel Convention Waste Categories

| Category | Examples |

| **Industrial wastes** | Chemical, heavy metal wastes |

| **Persistent Organic Pollutants** | POPs waste (linked to Stockholm Convention) |
| **Plastic waste** | Regulated under Basel amendments |

# Rotterdam Convention

## Definition

The **Rotterdam Convention on the Prior Informed Consent (PIC) Procedure for Certain Hazardous Chemicals and Pesticides in International Trade (1998)** is an international treaty promoting **shared responsibility and cooperative efforts** in the **international trade of hazardous chemicals** — giving importing countries the **right to decide whether to accept imports** of certain hazardous chemicals.

**Adopted:** 10 September 1998 (Rotterdam, Netherlands).  
**Entered into force:** 24 February 2004.  
**India ratified:** 2005.

## Explanation

### Rotterdam Convention — Key Details

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Adopted** | 10 September 1998 (Rotterdam, Netherlands) |
| **Entered into force** | 24 February 2004 |
| **Parties** | 165 countries |
| **Secretariat** | FAO + UNEP (joint, Rome/Geneva) |
| **India ratified** | 2005 |
| **Core mechanism** | Prior Informed Consent (PIC) procedure |

### Rotterdam PIC Procedure

```
EXPORTING COUNTRY intends to export listed chemical
        ↓
NOTIFIES SECRETARIAT (FAO/UNEP)
        ↓
SECRETARIAT forwards to IMPORTING COUNTRY
        ↓
IMPORTING COUNTRY DECIDES: Consent / No consent / Consent with conditions
        ↓
EXPORT PERMITTED ONLY IF importing country consents
        ↓
ENVIRONMENTALLY SOUND USE ensured
```

# Stockholm Convention on POPs

## Definition

The **Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs, 2001)** is a **global treaty to protect human health and the environment from Persistent Organic Pollutants** — chemicals that **persist in the environment, bioaccumulate in living organisms, and pose significant health risks** including cancer, reproductive disorders, and immune system damage.

**Adopted:** 22 May 2001 (Stockholm, Sweden).  
**Entered into force:** 17 May 2004.  
**India ratified:** 2006.  
**Note:** Different from **Stockholm Conference 1972** (UNCHE).

## Explanation

### Stockholm Convention on POPs — Key Details

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Adopted** | 22 May 2001 (Stockholm, Sweden) |
| **Entered into force** | 17 May 2004 |
| **Parties** | 186 countries |
| **Secretariat** | Geneva, Switzerland |
| **India ratified** | 2006 |
| **Goal** | Eliminate or restrict POPs production and use |

### Characteristics of POPs

| Characteristic | Detail |
|---------------|--------|
| **Persistent** | Resist degradation in environment (years to decades) |
| **Bioaccumulative** | Accumulate in fatty tissues of living organisms |
| **Long-range transport** | Travel far from source via air/water |
| **Toxic** | Harmful to human health and environment |
| **Semi-volatile** | Evaporate and deposit repeatedly (grasshopper effect) |

### POPs Listed in Stockholm Convention

|-------------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Aldrin** | **Endosulfan** (added 2011) |

# Minamata Convention

## Definition

The **Minamata Convention on Mercury** is a **global treaty to protect human health and the environment from anthropogenic emissions and releases of mercury and mercury compounds** — named after **Minamata Bay, Japan**, where **severe mercury poisoning (Minamata disease)** occurred due to industrial wastewater discharge in the 1950s–60s.

**Adopted:** 10 October 2013 (Kumamoto/Minamata, Japan).  
**Entered into force:** 16 August 2017.  
**India ratified:** 2018.

| **Named after** | Minamata disease tragedy, Japan |

### Minamata Disease — Historical Background

| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| **Location** | Minamata Bay, Kumamoto, Japan |
| **Period** | 1950s–1960s |
| **Cause** | Chisso Corporation discharged methylmercury in wastewater |
| **Impact** | 2,000+ people died; 10,000+ affected |
| **Symptoms** | Neurological damage, paralysis, birth defects |
| **Convention named** | To commemorate victims and prevent recurrence |
| **First major case** | Of industrial mercury poisoning globally |

### Key Provisions of Minamata Convention

| Provision | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Artisanal gold mining** | Reduce mercury use in small-scale gold mining |
| **Mercury supply** | Control mercury mining and trade |
| **Products** | Phase out mercury in products (thermometers, batteries, lamps) |
| **Manufacturing processes** | Control mercury use in chlor-alkali, vinyl chloride |
| **Emissions** | Control mercury emissions from coal plants, smelters |
| **Releases** | Control mercury releases to land and water |
| **Waste** | Environmentally sound mercury waste management |
| **Health** | Protect populations at risk from mercury exposure |

### Mercury Sources and Uses
