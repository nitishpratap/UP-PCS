## 6.1 Socio-Religious Reform Movements — Overview

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Socio-religious reform** | 19th–early 20th century movements that attacked social evils and reinterpreted religion |
| **Bengal Renaissance** | Cultural and reform flowering in Bengal centered on Calcutta elites |
| **Revivalism vs Reformism** | Revivalism returns to an ideal past (e.g. Vedas); reformism reshapes custom with reason and modern ethics |

### Causes — Why Reform Movements Rose

1. British rule and missionary criticism forced Indians to rethink caste, gender, and scripture.
2. Western education created a new middle class that valued reason, individual dignity, and print debate.
3. Social evils such as **Sati**, child marriage, ban on widow remarriage, and caste rigidity produced moral crisis.
4. Print culture and associations allowed reformers to organise beyond a single city.
5. Religious anxiety after 1857 pushed both modernists and traditionalists to redefine community identity.

### Socio-Religious Reform — How It Works

- In the nineteenth century, Indian society faced pressure from Christian missions, Western education, print culture, and colonial law. Reformers answered by purifying religion and attacking social evils from within Indian traditions.
- These movements were **regional**, not a single all-India party. Bengal produced Brahmo and widow-remarriage campaigns; Maharashtra produced Phule's anti-caste work and Prarthana Samaj; North India saw Arya Samaj and Aligarh/Deoband Muslim streams.
- Most reformers linked **faith and society**. They reinterpreted scriptures (Upanishads, Vedas, Quranic learning) and then used that reinterpretation to attack Sati, child marriage, caste humiliation, or educational backwardness.
- Reform often used **associations** (sabha/samaj), schools, journals, and petitions to the colonial government. Law became a tool: Sati ban (1829), Widow Remarriage Act (1856), Native Marriage Act (1872), Age of Consent Act (1891).
- Not every Indian leader welcomed colonial social laws. Cultural nationalists like **Tilak** feared that British intervention in marriage would weaken community autonomy, even when the social goal (protecting girls) looked progressive.
- Orthodoxy also organised. Bodies such as **Sanatan Dharma Rakshini Sabha** defended traditional Hindu practice against reform and missionary pressure. UPPCS often mixes reformist and orthodox names in matching questions.
- Muslim India developed two lasting answers after 1857: **Aligarh** for modern college education, and **Deoband** for traditional seminary revival. Both were reformist in their own frame, but they chose opposite educational strategies.
- For Prelims, success means mastering **person ↔ institution ↔ year ↔ one work/title**. Vague praise of "renaissance" without these triangles will not score.
- Trap: "All reformers supported every British social law." **Tilak** opposed the Age of Consent Act.
- Trap: "Brahmo Samaj and Arya Samaj are the same." Brahmo leans monotheist reform; Arya Samaj is Vedic revival with shuddhi and mass organisation.


> **Exam note:** Build memory around **matching tables**. Highest-yield names: Roy, Vidyasagar, Phule, Malabari, Karve, Dayanand, Sir Syed, Vivekananda, Besant.

## 6.2 Raja Ram Mohan Roy

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Raja Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833)** | Father of modern Indian renaissance; pioneer of Brahmo reform |
| **Atmiya Sabha (1815)** | Early discussion society founded by Roy in Calcutta |
| **Brahmo Sabha / Samaj (1828)** | Monotheistic reform association against idolatry and social evils |
| **Sati abolition (1829)** | Bentinck banned Sati with Roy's intellectual support |

### Causes — Why Roy Reform

1. He saw Sati, caste rigidity, and idolatry as corruptions of Hindu religion.
2. Christian missionary attacks forced a reasoned Indian response rooted in Upanishadic monotheism.
3. Colonial Calcutta exposed him to Western liberalism and rational debate.
4. He wanted Indians to combine modern knowledge with purified Indian spirituality.

### Course — Step by Step

1. Roy studied Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic, and English and wrote against idolatry and Sati.
2. **1815:** He founded **Atmiya Sabha** for religious discussion in Calcutta.
3. He campaigned publicly against **Sati** through petitions and writings.
4. **1828:** He founded the **Brahmo Sabha**, later known as Brahmo Samaj.
5. **1829:** **Lord William Bentinck** abolished Sati; Roy's campaign was decisive intellectual support.
6. He supported modern education, including English and scientific learning.
7. In **1830–33** he went to England as Mughal envoy and died at Bristol in **1833**.
8. Later Brahmo leaders such as Debendranath and Keshab continued his institutional legacy.

### Raja Ram Mohan Roy — How It Works

- Raja Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833) was a Calcutta-based scholar who knew Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic, and English. He used this learning to argue that pure Hinduism was monotheistic and did not require idolatry or cruel customs.
- He attacked **Sati**, caste arrogance, and priestly monopoly through books, petitions, and public debate. His method was reason and association politics, not armed revolt.
- In **1815** he founded the **Atmiya Sabha** as a discussion circle. In **1828** he founded the **Brahmo Sabha**, later famous as **Brahmo Samaj**, to institutionalise monotheistic worship and social reform.
- His campaign gave intellectual cover to the British ban on Sati. **Lord William Bentinck** abolished Sati in **1829**. Roy did not "pass the law" himself; he made the case that abolition was consistent with true Hindu ethics.
- Roy also backed modern education and a free press. He believed Indians needed Western science without abandoning purified Indian spirituality.
- Later Bengal reformers (Debendranath Tagore, Keshab Chandra Sen, Vidyasagar) worked in the public space he opened, even when they differed on pace and doctrine.
- He is remembered as the **Father of the Indian Renaissance** because he turned religious critique into organised modern reform.
- Trap: "Roy founded Arya Samaj." Arya Samaj was founded by **Dayanand Saraswati** in 1875.
- Trap: "Roy founded Satyashodhak Samaj." That was **Jyotiba Phule** (2022 Q84).
- Trap: "Roy alone abolished Sati." The legal act was Bentinck's; Roy supplied advocacy and legitimacy.


> **Exam note:** Roy = **Atmiya Sabha + Brahmo Samaj + Sati 1829**.

## 6.3 Brahmo Samaj & Bengal Reform Movements

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Brahmo Samaj** | Monotheistic reform movement begun by Ram Mohan Roy in Bengal |
| **Adi Brahmo Samaj** | Debendranath Tagore's conservative Brahmo stream |
| **Brahmo Samaj of India** | Keshab Chandra Sen's more radical stream |
| **Native Marriage Act (1872)** | Allowed civil/Brahmo marriage outside orthodox Hindu rites — **UPPCS 2019** |

### Course — Step by Step

1. **1828:** Roy founds Brahmo Sabha in Calcutta.
2. After Roy's death, **Debendranath Tagore** strengthens doctrine and organisation.
3. **Keshab Chandra Sen** radicalises social reform and expands the movement beyond Bengal.
4. Split produces **Adi Brahmo Samaj** and **Brahmo Samaj of India**.
5. Brahmos campaign for women's education, against child marriage, and for rational worship.
6. **1872:** **Native Marriage Act** legalises non-orthodox marriages associated with Brahmo practice.
7. Bengal also sees parallel work by Vidyasagar, Bethune, and other education reformers.
8. By late 19th century Brahmo influence declines relative to nationalist and revivalist currents, but its legacy remains.

### Brahmo Samaj — How It Works

- Brahmo Samaj began with Ram Mohan Roy's **Brahmo Sabha (1828)** in Calcutta. It taught worship of one formless God and rejected idol worship as essential religion.
- The Samaj became the institutional centre of the **Bengal reform movement**. Members used prayer meetings, journals, and schools to spread a rational, ethical Hinduism.
- After Roy, **Debendranath Tagore** consolidated the Samaj on a more scriptural and cautious path. Later, **Keshab Chandra Sen** pushed a more radical social programme and missionary style, which produced splits.
- Brahmo marriage practice needed legal recognition outside orthodox Hindu rites. This background connects to the **Native Marriage Act of 1872**, a direct year-based UPPCS fact.
- Bengal reform was larger than one samaj. Widow remarriage, girls' schooling, and vernacular textbooks (Vidyasagar and others) grew in the same Calcutta public sphere.
- The Samaj mattered because it showed Indians that religion could be reorganised through voluntary association rather than only through temple orthodoxy.
- Trap: "Native Marriage Act passed in 1856." **1856** is the Widow Remarriage Act; Native Marriage Act is **1872**.
- Trap: "Keshab founded Brahmo Samaj." **Roy** founded it; Keshab led a later, more radical phase.
- Trap: "Brahmo Samaj = Arya Samaj." Different founders, doctrines, and regions of strength.


> **Exam note:** Brahmo timeline = **1828 foundation → Debendranath → Keshab split → Native Marriage Act 1872**.

### Results

| Result | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| Organised monotheistic reform | Model for later samajs |
| Legal marriage reform **1872** | State recognition of reformed marriage |
| Bengal intellectual leadership | Seedbed of modern politics |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Founded **1828** by Roy
- Native Marriage Act **1872**
- Debendranath vs Keshab split
- Bengal = early reform capital
- Do not confuse **1856** Widow Remarriage with **1872** Native Marriage

## 6.4 Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820–1891)** | Bengal social reformer and Sanskrit scholar |
| **Hindu Widow Remarriage Act (1856)** | Law legalising widow remarriage; Vidyasagar's major campaign |
| **Sanskrit College, Calcutta** | Institution where he served as Principal — **2022 Q90** |

### Causes

1. He saw the misery of child widows as the worst cruelty of contemporary Hindu custom.
2. As a Sanskrit scholar he argued that widow remarriage had scriptural support.
3. Bengal's reform climate after Roy made legal campaigning possible.
4. He believed education, especially of women and in vernaculars, was the path to social progress.

### Course — Step by Step

1. Vidyasagar rose through Sanskrit learning and became a renowned scholar-administrator in Calcutta.
2. As **Principal of Sanskrit College, Calcutta**, he reformed teaching and widened access to Sanskrit education beyond a closed elite.
3. He collected scriptural arguments and organised petitions showing that Hindu texts could support widow remarriage.
4. Public controversy followed: reformers backed him; orthodoxy denounced the campaign as an attack on custom.
5. **1856:** The **Hindu Widow Remarriage Act** was passed, giving legal validity to widow remarriage.
6. He promoted girls' education, wrote accessible Bengali primers, and simplified Bengali prose for mass reading.
7. He cooperated with the broader Calcutta women's-education circle associated with **Bethune**, while remaining a scholar-activist rather than a samaj founder.
8. By the late nineteenth century his name had become the standard shorthand for widow remarriage and enlightened Sanskrit scholarship in Bengal.

### Vidyasagar — How It Works

- Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820–1891) was a Sanskrit scholar and educator in Bengal. His authority came from traditional learning, which he used to argue for modern social change from inside Hindu texts.
- His greatest legal campaign was **widow remarriage**. Through petitions and scriptural argument he helped secure the **Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act of 1856**.
- As **Principal of Sanskrit College, Calcutta**, he reformed curriculum and opened Sanskrit learning to a wider student body. This college tag is the exact match used in UPPCS 2022 Q90.
- He also fought for **girls' education**, wrote simple Bengali primers, and modernised Bengali prose so that ordinary readers could access knowledge.
- His method was scholarship + petition + school reform, not founding a lasting religious samaj like Brahmo or Arya.
- Pair him carefully: with **Roy** on Bengal reform chronology (Sati 1829 vs widow remarriage 1856); with **Bethune** on Calcutta girls' schooling; with **Karve** on widow work but different region and exam tags.
- Trap: "Vidyasagar abolished Sati." Sati was abolished in **1829** under Bentinck with Roy's support.
- Trap: "Vidyasagar founded Satyashodhak Samaj." That was **Phule**.
- Trap: "Widow Remarriage Act = 1872." Widow remarriage is **1856**; **1872** is Native Marriage Act.


> **Exam note:** Vidyasagar = **Widow Remarriage 1856 + Sanskrit College Principal**.

## 6.5 Jyotiba Phule & Satyashodhak Samaj

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Jyotiba / Jyotirao Phule (1827–1890)** | Maharashtra anti-caste reformer and educator |
| **Satyashodhak Samaj (1873)** | "Truth-seekers' society" founded by Phule — **2022 Q84** |
| **Savitribai Phule** | His wife; pioneer of girls' education in Maharashtra |
| **Gulamgiri** | Phule's famous critique of caste slavery |

### Causes

1. Phule experienced and observed caste humiliation under Brahmanical social order.
2. Christian missionary schools showed him the power of education for lower castes.
3. He rejected elite Bengal-style reform that ignored caste oppression of Shudras and Ati-Shudras.
4. He wanted a movement based on truth, equality, and education rather than Sanskrit orthodoxy.

### Course — Step by Step

1. Phule and **Savitribai** opened schools in the Pune region for girls and for children from lower castes who had been shut out of learning.
2. He publicly attacked Brahmanical monopoly over temples, rituals, and sacred knowledge, arguing that priests used religion to keep Shudras dependent.
3. He built a social base among lower castes and sympathetic reformers who wanted equality without waiting for elite Brahmo-style gradualism.
4. **1873:** He founded the **Satyashodhak Samaj** so members could seek truth, reject priestly mediation, and practise social equality as a community.
5. The Samaj organised meetings, challenged caste domination, and encouraged people to conduct ceremonies without exploitative priestly fees.
6. Phule wrote **Gulamgiri** and other works that compared caste oppression to slavery and demanded dignity for the oppressed.
7. His programme also backed widow remarriage and opposed child marriage as part of the same equality agenda.
8. Later non-Brahmin and Dalit politics in western India drew on the language and organisational example he left behind.

### Jyotiba Phule / Satyashodhak — How It Works

- Jyotirao (Jyotiba) Phule (1827–1890) worked in and around **Pune** in Maharashtra. Unlike many Bengal Brahmo reformers, his central target was **caste oppression** of Shudras and Ati-Shudras, not only widow customs or idolatry.
- Phule argued that Brahmanical monopoly over religion and knowledge kept lower castes in lifelong social slavery. Education was his main weapon against that monopoly.
- With his wife **Savitribai Phule**, he opened schools for girls and for lower-caste children. Savitribai is remembered as a pioneer of women's education in western India.
- In **1873** he founded the **Satyashodhak Samaj** ("Truth-seekers' Society"). Members rejected priestly mediation, questioned caste hierarchy, and promoted equality through truth-seeking and social service.
- Phule's book **Gulamgiri** attacked caste slavery in sharp language. His programme also supported widow remarriage and opposed child marriage within a broader equality agenda.
- Compared with **Prarthana Samaj** (Bombay elite reform around Ranade), Satyashodhak was more radical and lower-caste in social base. Compared with Brahmo Samaj, it put caste, not monotheism alone, at the centre.
- His work later inspired non-Brahmin and Dalit political currents in western India. For Prelims, remember him as an **anti-caste educator-organiser**, not as a Civil Disobedience or trade-union leader.
- Trap: "Satyashodhak Samaj founded by Raja Ram Mohan Roy / M.G. Ranade." Founder is **Jyotiba Phule** (2022 Q84).
- Trap: "Phule's primary association in 2022 Q1 is Peasant / Civil Disobedience / Trade Union Movement." Correct association is **Anti-caste Movement**.
- Trap: "Satyashodhak = Prarthana Samaj." Prarthana is Bombay elite reform; Satyashodhak is Phule's anti-caste body in the Pune region.


> **Exam note:** Phule = **anti-caste + Satyashodhak 1873 + schools with Savitribai**.

## 6.6 Behramji Malabari

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Behramji Malabari (1853–1912)** | Parsi reformer from Bombay who campaigned against child marriage |
| **Age of Consent Act (1891)** | Raised age of consent for girls; Malabari advocated it — **2024 Q17** |

### Causes

1. Child marriage caused severe harm to young girls' health and education.
2. Malabari used journalism and petitions to force a public debate.
3. He believed colonial law could protect women when orthodoxy refused reform.
4. Bombay's Parsi-reform milieu supported social legislation campaigns.

### Course — Step by Step

1. Malabari wrote articles and pamphlets exposing the suffering caused by infant marriage and the sexual abuse of very young wives.
2. Through the Bombay press he turned private household custom into a public moral and medical debate.
3. He lobbied British officials and Indian opinion for a higher legal age of consent for girls.
4. Reformers supported state protection; orthodox leaders and cultural nationalists warned that colonial law would invade Hindu marriage.
5. **1891:** The **Age of Consent Act** was passed, raising the age of consent in criminal law.
6. **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** emerged as a leading opponent, arguing against colonial interference even while the social problem remained real.
7. The controversy divided the Indian public sphere into social-reform and cultural-autonomy camps.
8. Malabari's name stayed attached to the anti-child-marriage crusade as the clearest Bombay reformer tag for that campaign.

### Behramji Malabari — How It Works

- Revise founder–year–place as one triangle. If any corner is wrong in an option, the whole match fails.

## 6.17 E.V. Ramaswami Naicker (Periyar) — Exam Add-on

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **E.V. Ramaswami Naicker (Periyar)** | Tamil social reformer; Self-Respect Movement leader |

### Periyar — How It Works

- **E.V. Ramaswami Naicker**, known as **Periyar**, was a Tamil social reformer who later led the **Self-Respect Movement** against caste hierarchy and Brahmanical social power in South India.
- Early in his public life he participated in the **Non-Cooperation Movement** and worked within the broader nationalist atmosphere of the early 1920s.
- In **1925** he left the Indian National Congress. The exit year is a direct Prelims fact and should be memorised with his name.
- After leaving Congress he built a more radical anti-caste and rationalist politics. The Self-Respect programme attacked caste ritual, inequality, and social domination.
- He is not part of the classic Bengal/Maharashtra samaj list (Brahmo, Arya, Satyashodhak), but UPPCS places him under Social Reform Movements through the 2023 question.
- Do not confuse the person Periyar with **Periyar Wildlife Sanctuary** or **Periyar river** questions in Environment/Geography papers.
- Trap: "Periyar never joined Congress campaigns." He did participate in Non-Cooperation before breaking away.
- Trap: "Periyar left Congress in 1930." The tested year is **1925**.
- Trap: "Only one of the 2023 statements is correct." Both statements are correct: NCM participation + 1925 exit.


> **Exam note:** Periyar = **NCM participant + left Congress 1925 → Both**.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- 2023 Q44 answer: **Both 1 and 2**
- Left Congress: **1925**
- Participated in Non-Cooperation Movement
- Later Self-Respect Movement leader
- Not a wildlife sanctuary question (different 'Periyar')
