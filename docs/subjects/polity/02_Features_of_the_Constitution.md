## 2.0 Features of the Constitution — Overview

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Salient features** | Distinctive structural and philosophical characteristics that define the Indian Constitution as a whole |
| **Written Constitution** | Constitution embodied in a formal enacted document (unlike unwritten UK conventions-heavy model) |
| **Living Constitution** | Constitution that adapts through amendments, interpretation, and political practice |

### How It Works

- UPPCS rarely asks “list all features”; it tests **one feature deeply** — Preamble words, federal label, parliamentary A/R, Basic Structure case, scholar–concept match.
- The Constitution is a **compromise document**: federal yet unitary-tilted; parliamentary yet with a powerful elected President; rights-based yet Directive Principles guide policy.
- “Features” overlap with borrowed features — here the ask is **what kind of polity India is**, not only which country donated which clause.
- Length, amendment blend, federal design, parliamentary executive, judiciary design, citizenship, franchise, and Preamble philosophy form the core exam set.
- Emergency provisions and single Constitution for Centre and States strengthen the unitary bias inside a federal form.
- After the **73rd and 74th Amendments (1992; in force 1993)**, the Constitution also recognises a **third tier** of government (Panchayats/Municipalities) — UPPCS 2020 direct ask.
- Basic Structure doctrine is the judicial limit that protects identity of these features from destructive amendment.
- Always separate **descriptive label** (quasi-federal, parliamentary) from **justiciable right** (Preamble ideals are not directly enforceable as FR).

## 2.1 Lengthiest Written Constitution

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Lengthiest written Constitution** | India’s Constitution has the largest volume of articles/schedules among major national constitutions |
| **Reasons for length** | Historical detail, geographical diversity, single constitution for Centre–States, legal drafting style, special provisions |

### How It Works

- India adopted a **detailed written** Constitution rather than a short skeleton plus conventions (UK style).
- Length comes from: (1) vastness and diversity of India; (2) borrowing detailed administrative clauses from GOI Act 1935; (3) one Constitution for both Centre and States; (4) elaborate FR, DPSP, emergency, services, elections, language, and tribal-area provisions; (5) lawyerly drafting aiming to reduce ambiguity.
- Originally (1950) it had **395 Articles** in **22 Parts** and **8 Schedules**; present size is larger (~**470 Articles**, **25 Parts**, **12 Schedules** in standard Laxmikanth update framing) because of amendments adding Parts/Articles/Schedules.
- Being written does **not** mean completely rigid — amendment procedures vary.
- Comparison trap: USA Constitution is short and skeletal; India is elaborate and code-like.
- Length is a **salient feature**, not a defect by itself — it tries to settle centre–state and minority questions in the text.
- Schedules and appendices-style detail (oaths, languages, allocation of seats) inflate volume without changing the core philosophy.
- For prelims, pair “lengthiest” with “why” — diversity + 1935 inheritance + centre–state single document.

> **Exam note:** “Lengthiest” = India; do not confuse with “most rigid” (USA is often taught as more rigid).

### Exam Facts (raata)

- India has the lengthiest written Constitution of any sovereign country.
- Original: 395 Articles, 22 Parts, 8 Schedules (standard teaching figures).
- One Constitution covers Centre and States.
- GOI Act 1935 contributed much structural bulk.
- Detailed rights, DPSPs, emergencies, and special provisions add length.
- Written ≠ fully rigid.
- USA Constitution is comparatively brief.
- Length is listed among Laxmikanth’s salient features.

## 2.2 Blend of Rigidity and Flexibility

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Rigid Constitution** | Requires a special, difficult procedure for amendment (e.g. USA) |
| **Flexible Constitution** | Can be amended by ordinary legislative process (e.g. UK in practice) |
| **Indian blend** | Some amendments by simple majority; many by special majority; some need State ratification |

### How It Works

- Article **368** provides the main amendment framework, but not all constitutional changes go through 368 alone.
- **Simple majority** of Parliament can alter some provisions (e.g. creation/alteration of States under Arts. 2–4 — “not amendment” in 368 sense in standard teaching).
- **Special majority** (Art. 368) is required for most constitutional amendments.
- **Special majority + ratification by half of State legislatures** is required for federal provisions (e.g. election of President, extent of executive power, Seventh Schedule, representation of States in Parliament, Art. 368 itself).
- This three-track design is why India is called a blend of rigidity and flexibility.
- Flexibility allows social reform amendments (abolition of privy purses, bank nationalisation-era changes, GST architecture).
- Rigidity protects federal bargains and core institutional design.
- Basic Structure doctrine adds a **judicial rigidity** even where formal amendment procedure is satisfied.
- Trap: “Indian Constitution is completely flexible like UK” = FALSE.
- Trap: “Indian Constitution is as rigid as USA” = FALSE.

> **Exam note:** Blend = simple / special / special+State ratification — three procedural speeds.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- India = partly rigid, partly flexible.
- Art. 368 = principal amendment article.
- Federal clauses need State ratification (half the States).
- Some changes by simple majority outside 368 label.
- Basic Structure limits even special-majority amendments.
- UK = flexible model contrast; USA = rigid model contrast.
- 24th Amendment affirmed Parliament’s amending power (subject later to Basic Structure).
- Blend is a classic salient-feature one-liner.

2. **(UPPCS-style pattern)** Amendment of federal provisions generally requires:

## 2.3 Federal System, Quasi-Federal Nature, Federal & Unitary Features

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Federal system** | Dual polity with constitutional division of powers between Centre and States |
| **Quasi-federal** | Federal constitution with strong unitary/centralising bias (K.C. Wheare on India) |
| **Unitary features** | Devices that strengthen the Centre or allow Union dominance |
| **Federal features** | Devices that recognise dual government and State autonomy |

### Federal Features of Indian Constitution

| Feature | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| Dual polity | Centre and States both exist under the Constitution |
| Written Constitution | Supreme document allocating powers |
| Division of powers | Union, State, Concurrent Lists (Seventh Schedule) |
| Supremacy of Constitution | Centre and States bound by the Constitution |
| Independent judiciary | SC umpires Centre–State disputes |
| Bicameralism | Rajya Sabha represents States (federal second chamber idea) |

### Unitary Features of Indian Constitution

| Feature | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| Strong Centre | More subjects + residuary with Union; emergency overrides |
| Single Constitution | One Constitution for Union and States |
| Single citizenship | No separate State citizenship |
| Flexibility of centre in States’ creation | Parliament can alter States (Art. 3) |
| Emergency provisions | Temporary unitary functioning |
| All-India Services | Centre-influenced bureaucracy in States |
| Integrated judiciary | One hierarchy under SC |
| Appointment of Governor | Centre appoints State Governor |
| Unequal Rajya Sabha representation | Not purely federal equality of States |
| Veto / assent dynamics & central legislation in Concurrent field | Centre can prevail |

### How It Works

- India is **federal in form** (dual government + lists + SC) but **unitary in spirit** during conflict/emergency — hence **quasi-federal**.
- **K.C. Wheare** called India quasi-federal; this is a direct UPPCS ask (2019).
- Independent judiciary with judicial review is a **federal feature**, but it is **not the reason** India is called quasi-federal (UPPCS 2020 A/R — both true, R does not explain A). Quasi-federal label comes from **strong Centre / unitary bias**, not from judicial review alone.
- **Granville Austin** emphasised **cooperative federalism** — Centre and States as a single mechanism for social revolution.
- **Morris-Jones** is linked to **bargaining federalism**; **Ivor Jennings** to **federalism with centralising tendency** (UPPCS 2024 match).
- Art. 1: India is a **Union of States** — States have **no right to secede** (UPPCS 2019). Advocacy of secession is **not** protected as free speech in the constitutional sense taught for this question.
- During National Emergency, the Constitution can operate almost as a unitary system — strongest unitary bias illustration.
- Residuary powers with the Centre (Canadian model) tilt federal balance upward.
- States are not “indestructible” like USA States — Parliament may alter boundaries/names under Art. 3.
- Concurrent List + Union paramountcy means States share fields but can be overridden.
- Third tier (local government) was constitutionally added in **1992** by 73rd/74th Amendments.
- Do not say India is “purely unitary” or “purely federal” — both extremes fail statement questions.

> **Exam note:** Scholar lock — **Wheare = Quasi**; **Austin = Cooperative**; **Morris-Jones = Bargaining**; **Jennings = Centralising**. Quasi ≠ explained merely by judicial review.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- India = federation with strong Centre / quasi-federal State.
- K.C. Wheare: Quasi-federal (UPPCS 2019).
- Federal features: dual polity, written constitution, division of powers, independent judiciary, bicameralism.
- Unitary features: single citizenship, strong Centre, emergency, Art. 3, integrated judiciary, All-India Services, Governors.
- Residuary powers → Centre.
- Emergency converts working to near-unitary.
- Cooperative federalism ↔ Granville Austin (UPPCS 2024).
- Word “Union of States” (Art. 1) signals indestructible Union, destructible States (standard teaching).

## 2.4 Parliamentary System & Presidential vs Parliamentary

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Parliamentary system** | Executive is drawn from and collectively responsible to the legislature (especially Lok Sabha) |
| **Presidential system** | President is head of government with fixed tenure; not responsible to legislature daily |
| **Nominal vs real executive** | President is constitutional head; Council of Ministers led by PM is real executive |

### Parliamentary Features in India

| Feature | Content |
|---------|---------|
| Majority party rule | PM usually leader of majority in Lok Sabha |
| Collective responsibility | Council of Ministers responsible to Lok Sabha (Art. 75) |
| Political homogeneity | Cabinet from same coalition/party framework ideally |
| Double membership | Ministers are members of legislature |
| Leadership of PM | PM is captain of the ship |
| Dissolution of Lok Sabha | Possible on advice — fusion of powers dynamics |
| Secrecy of procedure | Cabinet confidentiality |

### Presidential vs Parliamentary (comparison)

| Point | Parliamentary (India/UK) | Presidential (USA) |
|-------|--------------------------|--------------------|
| Executive | From legislature | Separate election |
| Tenure | Depends on confidence | Fixed term |
| Responsibility | To legislature | Not daily responsible to Congress |
| Separation | Fusion of executive–legislature | Stricter separation |
| Head of State vs Govt | Split (President + PM) | Combined in President |

### How It Works

- India chose the **parliamentary system** on the UK model with an elected republican President.
- The defining test: **executive responsibility to the legislature** — exactly the UPPCS 2025 Assertion–Reason lock.
- President is the **formal/constitutional** executive; PM + Council of Ministers is the **real** executive.
- Collective responsibility means the ministry must resign if it loses Lok Sabha confidence.
- Individual ministerial responsibility and parliamentary questions enforce accountability.
- Unlike the USA, the President of India is generally bound by aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Art. 74), subject to limited constitutional discretion pockets.
- Advantages claimed: responsible government, representation of diverse groups in cabinet, flexibility.
- Criticisms: unstable coalitions, cabinet dictatorship if majority is huge, decline of legislature — awareness for Mains-ish statements.
- Trap: “India has a presidential system because it has a President” = FALSE.
- Trap: “President of India is like US President in powers” = FALSE.

> **Exam note:** If A = parliamentary India and R = executive responsible to legislature → both true, R explains A (2025 Q54).

### Exam Facts (raata)

- India = parliamentary form of government.
- Real executive = Council of Ministers; nominal = President.
- Collective responsibility to **Lok Sabha**.
- Ministers are normally members of Parliament.
- USA = presidential contrast.
- Presence of President ≠ presidential system.
- Parliamentary system borrowed mainly from UK.
- UPPCS 2025 A/R locks the responsibility definition.

## 2.5 Preamble — Keywords, Ideals, Amendability

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Preamble** | Prefatory statement of the Constitution setting out source, nature of State, and objectives |
| **Objectives Resolution** | Nehru’s resolution (Dec 1946 / adopted Jan 1947) that shaped the Preamble |
| **42nd Amendment & Preamble** | **1976** — inserted **Socialist**, **Secular**, and **Integrity** (unity and integrity of the Nation) |

### Preamble Text (raata)

```
WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a
SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens:

JUSTICE, social, economic and political;
LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship;
EQUALITY of status and of opportunity;
and to promote among them all
FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation;

IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do
HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION.
```

### Keywords & Ideals Table

| Keyword / Ideal | Meaning (exam) | Count / Trap |
|-----------------|----------------|--------------|
| **Sovereign** | External independence + internal supremacy; not a colony/dependency | Original |
| **Socialist** | Social–economic justice; reduce inequality; Indian mixed-economy reading (not pure Marxian) | Added **42nd / 1976** |
| **Secular** | No official State religion; State treats all religions equally (Indian positive secularism teaching) | Added **42nd / 1976** |
| **Democratic** | Government based on popular will; free elections; accountable government | Original |
| **Republic** | Elected head of State; no hereditary monarch | Original |
| **Justice** | Social, Economic, Political | **3** |
| **Liberty** | Thought, Expression, Belief, Faith, Worship | **5** |
| **Equality** | Status and Opportunity | **2** |
| **Fraternity** | Dignity of the individual + unity and **integrity** of the Nation | **1** ideal (integrity added by 42nd) |

### Amendability of Preamble (dedicated lock)

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| Can Preamble be amended? | **Yes**, under Art. 368 (42nd Amendment did amend it) |
| Can Preamble be amended to destroy Basic Structure? | **No** |
| Is Preamble part of Constitution? | **Yes** after Kesavananda (1973); Berubari (1960) had said No |
| Is Preamble enforceable in courts as a right? | **No** (aids interpretation) |
| Who can amend Preamble? | Parliament by constitutional amendment procedure |

### How It Works

- Preamble answers four questions: **who** adopted (People of India), **what** State (Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic), **what ends** (Justice–Liberty–Equality–Fraternity), **when** (26 Nov 1949).
- **Justice–Liberty–Equality–Fraternity** order and counts are a direct UPPCS numeric ask (2018: **3, 5, 2, 1**).
- “Federal” is **not** a Preamble word (2021) — federal character comes from the body of the Constitution.
- **Sovereignty** and **Republic** were **not** added by 42nd — only **Socialist** and **Secular** (plus **integrity** in the fraternity clause) (2025 Q56).
- **Sovereign** means India is not subject to any external authority and is supreme internally.
- **Socialist** (after 42nd) signals commitment to social and economic justice and reducing inequalities — interpreted in Indian constitutional practice, not as a one-party socialist State.
- **Secular** (after 42nd) means the State has no religion of its own and must treat religions equally; secularism is also treated as Basic Structure.
- **Democratic** covers political democracy (elections, representation) and, with DPSPs/FR, social–economic democracy goals.
- **Republic** contrasts monarchy — President is elected, not hereditary.
- Legal nature: Preamble is **not enforceable** like Fundamental Rights (2019) — it aids interpretation.
- **Berubari Union (1960)** held Preamble is **not** part of the Constitution; **Kesavananda Bharati (1973)** held Preamble **is** part of the Constitution and relevant to Basic Structure.
- Amendability: Preamble can be amended (42nd did so) but **Basic Structure** elements reflected in Preamble cannot be destroyed.
- Source of authority is **popular sovereignty** — “We, the People”, not a British grant.
- Date in Preamble is **adoption date 26 Nov 1949**, not enforcement 26 Jan 1950.

> **Exam note:** 42nd added **Socialist + Secular** (not Sovereignty/Republic). Counts **3-5-2-1**. Not enforceable; is part of Constitution post-Kesavananda; amendable but within Basic Structure.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Preamble based on Objectives Resolution.
- Nature of Indian State: Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic.
- Justice 3; Liberty 5; Equality 2; Fraternity ideal.
- 42nd Amendment 1976: Socialist, Secular, Integrity.
- “Federal” not in Preamble.
- Preamble not directly enforceable.
- Preamble is part of the Constitution (Kesavananda).
- Adopted with Constitution on 26 Nov 1949 textually.
- Aids interpretation of ambiguous provisions.
- Cannot be used alone to strike ordinary laws as if it were Art. 13 FR.

## 2.6 Basic Structure Doctrine

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Basic Structure Doctrine** | Judicial principle that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution to destroy its basic/essential features |
| **Kesavananda Bharati case** | **1973** Supreme Court case that propounded Basic Structure |
| **Article 368** | Amendment procedure — power exists but is limited by Basic Structure |

### Landmark Chronology (must lock)

| Case / Event | Year | Holding (exam) |
|--------------|------|----------------|
| Shankari Prasad | **1951** | Parliament can amend FR |
| Sajjan Singh | **1965** | Affirmed amending power |
| Golaknath | **1967** | FR beyond amendatory reach |
| 24th Amendment | **1971** | Asserted Parliament’s power to amend any part including FR |
| Kesavananda Bharati | **1973** | Parliament can amend but **not** Basic Structure |
| 42nd Amendment | **1976** | Tried to expand amending power / Preamble words |
| Minerva Mills | **1980** | Reaffirmed Basic Structure; limited 42nd excesses |

### How It Works

- Basic Structure is **not written** as a closed list in the Constitution — it is a judicial doctrine evolving case by case.
- Golaknath blocked FR amendment; 24th Amendment responded; Kesavananda balanced power with implied limitation.
- UPPCS asks the **propounding case** (2020, 2022) and **chronology** with Golaknath/24th/42nd (2021, 2024).
- Preamble ideals feed Basic Structure content (secularism, democracy, republic) after Kesavananda.
- Doctrine applies to constitutional amendments; ordinary laws are tested mainly on FR/legislative competence.
- Trap: Golaknath ≠ Basic Structure propounding case.
- Trap: Basic Structure = 1951 Shankari Prasad — FALSE.

#### Elements of Basic Structure (standard Laxmikanth / judgment teaching set)

| # | Element often treated as Basic Structure |
|---|------------------------------------------|
| 1 | Supremacy of the Constitution |
| 2 | Sovereign, democratic and republican nature of the Indian polity |
| 3 | Secular character of the Constitution |
| 4 | Separation of powers among legislature, executive, judiciary |
| 5 | Federal character of the Constitution |
| 6 | Unity and integrity of the Nation |
| 7 | Welfare state / socio-economic justice (DPSP mandate) |
| 8 | Judicial review |
| 9 | Freedom and dignity of the individual |
| 10 | Parliamentary system of government |
| 11 | Rule of law |
| 12 | Harmony / balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles |
| 13 | Principle of equality |
| 14 | Free and fair elections |
| 15 | Independence of judiciary |
| 16 | Limited amending power of Parliament (doctrine itself) |

> **Exam note:** Propounded in **Kesavananda Bharati, 1973**. Sequence: Shankari → Sajjan → Golaknath → 24th → Kesavananda → 42nd. Memorise the **illustrative list**, not as an exhaustive closed schedule.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Basic Structure → Kesavananda Bharati **1973**.
- Golaknath **1967** (FR freeze attempt).
- 24th Amendment **1971**.
- 42nd Amendment **1976**.
- Shankari Prasad **1951**; Sajjan Singh **1965**.
- Minerva Mills **1980** reinforces doctrine.
- Parliament may amend but not abrogate Basic Structure.
- Doctrine is judicial, not a schedule list.
- Linked to protecting democracy, secularism, judicial review, federal character, and free and fair elections.

## 2.7 Single Citizenship & Universal Adult Franchise

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Single citizenship** | Every citizen is a citizen of India only — no separate State citizenship |
| **Universal adult franchise** | All adults meeting age/qualification vote without property/tax/sex barriers (Art. 326) |
| **Voting age** | **18 years** after **61st Constitutional Amendment, 1988** (earlier 21) |

### How It Works

- Single citizenship promotes national unity and equality of civic status across States — a **unitary feature** inside the federal system.
- Contrast: USA has dual citizenship (federal + state) in classical teaching.
- Indian citizenship is regulated mainly by the Constitution (Part II) and the **Citizenship Act, 1955** (modes of acquisition/loss — deeper detail can sit with Topic 4 if expanded there; here lock single citizenship as a feature).
- Universal adult franchise was a radical democratic choice in 1950 for a largely poor, illiterate population — shows faith in political equality.
- Article **326** elections to Lok Sabha and State Assemblies on adult suffrage.
- 61st Amendment lowered voting age from 21 to **18**.
- Franchise is equal — one person, one vote — subject to statutory disqualifications (non-citizen, unsound mind, corrupt practices as law provides).
- Together, single citizenship + adult franchise support the **democratic republic** identity in the Preamble.

> **Exam note:** Single citizenship = Canada/India teaching contrast with USA dual. Voting age 18 = 61st Amendment.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- India: single citizenship.
- USA: dual citizenship (contrast).
- Universal adult franchise from commencement (with age threshold).
- Art. 326 = adult suffrage for LS/Assembly elections.
- Voting age 18 since 61st Amendment 1988/89.
- Earlier voting age was 21.
- Single citizenship = unitary feature.
- Franchise = democratic feature.

## 2.8 Independent Judiciary & Integrated Judiciary

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Independent judiciary** | Judges free from executive/legislative control in adjudication; secured by appointment, tenure, salary, removal safeguards |
| **Integrated judiciary** | Single hierarchy: Subordinate Courts → High Courts → Supreme Court (one system, not separate federal/state court pyramids like USA) |

### How It Works

- Independence devices: security of tenure, fixed service conditions, removal only by impeachment-like process, expenses charged on Consolidated Fund, power to punish contempt, separation of judiciary from executive (DPSP Art. 50 direction).
- Independence enables **judicial review** and Basic Structure enforcement.
- Integration means SC is the apex of **one** judicial system for Centre and States — appeals and supervisory jurisdiction bind the structure.
- USA contrast: dual court system (federal courts + state courts) — India rejected that model.
- Integrated judiciary is also listed as a **unitary feature** because it centralises final interpretation in the SC.
- High Courts are not final in the same way as US State supreme courts for federal questions — SC can hear appeals/SLPs.
- Trap: Independent ≠ unelected legislature; judiciary interprets, does not replace Parliament’s policy role (judicial activism debates aside).
- Trap: Integrated ≠ lack of High Court autonomy in ordinary administration; hierarchy still exists.

> **Exam note:** India = **independent + integrated**. USA = independent but **dual** court system.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Judiciary is independent of executive and legislature.
- Judiciary is integrated under the Supreme Court.
- Judicial review is a Basic Structure element (teaching).
- Integration = unitary feature; independence = democratic/constitutional feature.
- Art. 50 directs separation of judiciary from executive.
- Impeachment-like removal protects tenure.
- Contrasts with USA dual judiciary.
- SC is federal umpire + highest appellate court.

## 2.10 Cooperative Federalism & Competitive Federalism

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Cooperative federalism** | Centre and States work jointly as partners for common goals (Austin’s reading of Indian Constitution) |
| **Competitive federalism** | States compete with each other for investment, rankings, and performance (post-liberalisation governance idea) |
| **Bargaining federalism** | Centre–State relations shaped by political negotiation (Morris-Jones) |

### How It Works

- Cooperative federalism emphasises shared rule: concurrent list, Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils, GST Council, centrally sponsored schemes with State delivery.
- Granville Austin argued the Indian Constitution was designed for **cooperative federalism** — a social-revolutionary compact.
- Competitive federalism rose in policy discourse as States bid for capital, ease-of-doing-business, and outcome rankings — complementary, not a constitutional text term.
- UPPCS 2024 matching places Cooperative with Austin and Bargaining with Morris-Jones.
- Emergency and Art. 356 show the limits of cooperation when Centre asserts supremacy.
- NITI Aayog era language often stresses cooperative + competitive federalism together.
- Trap: Competitive federalism ≠ Wheare’s quasi-federal label.
- Trap: Cooperative federalism ≠ absence of Centre–State conflict.

> **Exam note:** Match **Austin–Cooperative**, **Morris-Jones–Bargaining**, **Wheare–Quasi**, **Jennings–Centralising**.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Cooperative federalism → Granville Austin.
- Bargaining federalism → Morris-Jones.
- Quasi-federal → K.C. Wheare.
- Centralising tendency → Ivor Jennings.
- GST Council = cooperative institution example.
- Competitive federalism = States competing for growth/investment.
- Both cooperative and competitive appear in contemporary governance vocabulary.
- Constitutional design leans cooperative; political practice mixes conflict and bargain.

## 2.11 Constitutional Principles — Rule of Law, Supremacy, Separation, Checks, Conventions

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Rule of Law** | Absolute supremacy of law; equality before law; constitution-centred legal order (Dicey core adapted to India) |
| **Constitutional Supremacy** | Constitution is the highest law; all organs derive power from it |
| **Separation of Powers** | Distinct functions of legislature, executive, judiciary |
| **Checks and Balances** | Each organ can restrain the others to prevent tyranny |
| **Constitutional Conventions** | Unwritten political practices treated as binding in spirit (more UK-famous; India has some, e.g. aid-and-advice norms historically) |

### How It Works

- India follows **constitutional supremacy**, not parliamentary sovereignty of the classical UK type — laws inconsistent with the Constitution can be struck down.
- **A.V. Dicey’s Rule of Law** (classical three points adapted in Indian teaching):
  1. Absolute supremacy of law — absence of arbitrary power.
  2. Equality before the law — equal subjection of all classes to ordinary law of the land.
  3. Predominance of legal spirit — rights as result of court decisions / constitutional guarantees (Indian version emphasises constitutional rights + judicial enforcement).
- Rule of Law underpins equality (Art. 14) and limits arbitrary executive action.
- **PIL** expands access to justice for weaker sections and is treated as essential for Rule of Law in India (UPPCS 2025 A/R — both true, R explains A).
- Separation of powers in India is **not rigid** like USA; parliamentary fusion mixes executive and legislature, while judiciary remains distinct.
- Checks and balances appear as: judicial review, parliamentary no-confidence, limited presidential return of bills, bicameralism, federal division, Basic Structure review of amendments.
- **Constitutional conventions** in India (examples):
  - President acts on aid and advice of Council of Ministers (now largely constitutionalised in Art. 74, but convention history matters).
  - Prime Minister is the leader who can command Lok Sabha confidence.
  - Cabinet secrecy / collective responsibility practices.
  - Speaker’s impartiality norms in the House.
- India’s written text + SC jurisprudence reduce reliance on pure convention compared with UK, but conventions still lubricate parliamentary practice.
- Basic Structure protects Rule of Law, democracy, and separation elements from destructive amendment.
- **Synthesis feature:** Parliament can amend the Constitution (parliamentary side); judiciary can invalidate unconstitutional amendments that destroy Basic Structure (judicial supremacy side).
- Trap: “India has absolute separation of powers like USA” = FALSE.
- Trap: “Parliament is sovereign like UK” = FALSE — Constitution is supreme.

> **Exam note:** India = **Constitutional supremacy + limited separation + strong judicial review**. Dicey 3 points + PIL–Rule of Law A/R (2025).

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Constitution is supreme law of the land.
- Rule of Law = anti-arbitrariness + equality before law (Dicey core).
- PIL aids Rule of Law by widening access to justice (2025).
- Separation of powers exists but is not American-rigid.
- Checks and balances operate through review, responsibility, federalism.
- Conventions matter less than in UK but are not zero — PM from majority, cabinet secrecy, Speaker norms.
- Judicial review enforces constitutional supremacy.
- Basic Structure protects these principles.
- Parliamentary amending power + judicial Basic Structure review = synthesis feature.

## Common Traps — Don't Fall For These

1. **“Federal” in Preamble** — NOT present (2021).
2. **42nd added Sovereignty/Republic** — FALSE; added **Socialist + Secular** (+ integrity) (2025).
3. **Preamble enforceable like FR** — FALSE (2019).
4. **Justice–Liberty–Equality–Fraternity counts** — **3,5,2,1** only (2018).
5. **Quasi-federal = Austin** — FALSE; **Wheare** (2019). Cooperative = **Austin** (2024).
6. **Basic Structure = Golaknath** — FALSE; **Kesavananda 1973**.
7. **India has presidential system because it has a President** — FALSE.
8. **Dual citizenship in India** — FALSE; single citizenship.
9. **Absolute separation of powers like USA** — FALSE.
10. **Parliamentary sovereignty like UK** — FALSE; constitutional supremacy.
11. **Residuary powers with States (USA model) in India** — FALSE; with Centre.
12. **Berubari still controls Preamble status** — Overruled/shifted by Kesavananda: Preamble **is** part.
13. **Voting age always 18 since 1950** — FALSE; became 18 by **61st** Amendment.
14. **Integrated judiciary means High Courts have no role** — FALSE; hierarchy exists under SC.
15. **Competitive federalism = Wheare’s label** — FALSE; Wheare = quasi-federal.
16. **Quasi-federal explained by judicial review alone** — FALSE (2020 A/R); both may be true but R does not explain A.
17. **States can secede / secession speech fully protected** — FALSE (2019); no secession right.
18. **Third tier added in 1990/91** — FALSE; **1992** (73rd/74th Acts).
19. **Parliamentary system originated in France/Switzerland** — FALSE; first in **UK** (2018).
20. **Preamble cannot be amended at all** — FALSE; 42nd amended it (within Basic Structure limits).
21. **Basic Structure is an exhaustive closed list in the Constitution text** — FALSE; judicial illustrative doctrine.
22. **India = parliamentary sovereignty like UK** — FALSE; constitutional supremacy + Basic Structure.
