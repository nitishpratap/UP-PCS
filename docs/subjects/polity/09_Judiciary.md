## Integrated vs independent — the frame

India’s judiciary is **both** integrated (one hierarchy) **and** independent (separate from the other organs) — those are **not** the same claim.

- **Integrated:** **one** hierarchy: district/sessions → High Court → Supreme Court. SC is the **apex for the Union and the States**. Canadian flavour.
- **Independent:** separate from the executive/legislature (Arts. **124–147, 214–231, 50**). Not the same thing as “integrated”.
- **USA contrast:** the USA runs a **dual** system — federal courts apply federal law, and state courts apply state law.
- The **US Supreme Court** is **not** the regular apex court for state-court appeals.
- India has **no** such dual split; its judiciary is fully integrated.
- **Part / Articles:** SC: **Part V, Ch. IV, Arts. 124–147**. HC: **Part VI, Ch. V, Arts. 214–231**. Subordinate: **233–237**. Tribunals: **Part XIVA (323A–323B)**.
- **Art. 50:** DPSP — separate the judiciary from the executive in the **public services of the State**.

**Predecessor**
- The **Federal Court of India** was set up on **1 October 1937**, under the **Government of India Act, 1935**.
- Its first Chief Justice was Sir **Maurice Gwyer**.
- Appeals from the Federal Court still went to the **Privy Council** in London.
- The **Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949** ended these residual appeals.
- The **Supreme Court of India** was inaugurated on **28 January 1950**.
- Its first CJI was **H.J. Kania**.
- Its original strength was the **CJI plus 7 judges**, totalling **8**.

> **Exam note:** “Independent” ≠ “integrated”. A court can be independent in a dual system (USA). India is **both**.

## Supreme Court — Arts. 124–147 (elaboration)

**Part V, Ch. IV:** the Union’s apex court — appointment, jurisdiction, and “complete justice”.

- **Art. 124:** The Constitution establishes the **Supreme Court of India**.
- Under **Art. 124(2)**, the **President** appoints every Supreme Court judge after consultation.
- A Supreme Court judge holds office until the age of **65**.
- A Supreme Court judge resigns by writing to the **President**.
- A Supreme Court judge can be removed only through the procedure in **Art. 124(4)**.
- **Arts. 124A–C** created the **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)**.
- The **99th Amendment (2014)** inserted these three articles.
- The Supreme Court **struck down** the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act in **2015** — the text of 124A–C is now dead law.
- **Art. 125** lets Parliament fix the salaries of Supreme Court judges by law.
- These salaries are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**.
- A judge's salary cannot be reduced to their disadvantage after appointment, except during a **Financial Emergency (Art. 360)**.
- **Art. 126** lets the President appoint an **Acting Chief Justice of India** when the office is vacant or the CJI is absent.
- By convention, the President appoints the **senior-most** available Supreme Court judge as Acting CJI.
- **Art. 127** allows the appointment of an **ad hoc judge** to the Supreme Court.
- An ad hoc judge must be a **sitting High Court judge** who is qualified to be a Supreme Court judge.
- This appointment needs the CJI's request, the President's **previous consent**, and consultation with that judge's High Court Chief Justice.
- An ad hoc judge is appointed only when the Supreme Court lacks a **quorum** of judges.
- **Art. 128** allows a **retired judge** to sit and act as a Supreme Court judge.
- That retired judge must be a former **Supreme Court judge**, or a High Court judge otherwise qualified for the Supreme Court.
- This requires the consent of both the **President** and the judge concerned.
- **Art. 129** makes the Supreme Court a **court of record**.
- Its acts and proceedings are recorded and treated as evidence.
- The Supreme Court has the power to **punish for contempt of itself**.
- **Art. 130** fixes the Supreme Court's seat at **Delhi**.
- The CJI may, with the President's approval, appoint another place as an additional seat.
- In practice, the Supreme Court has **no permanent outstation benches**.
- **Art. 131** gives the Supreme Court **exclusive original jurisdiction** in federal disputes.
- This jurisdiction covers disputes between the Union and a State, or between States.
- Art. 131 does **not** extend to enforcement of Fundamental Rights or to disputes involving a private party.
- **Art. 132** allows an appeal to the Supreme Court from a High Court judgment on a **constitutional** question.
- This appeal needs a High Court certificate that the case involves a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution**.
- **Art. 133** allows a **civil appeal** to the Supreme Court from a High Court judgment.
- The **30th Amendment (1972)** removed the earlier requirement of a minimum ₹20,000 value for such an appeal.
- **Art. 134** allows a **criminal appeal** to the Supreme Court from a High Court judgment.
- The appeal is automatic if the High Court has reversed an acquittal and sentenced the accused to **death**.
- It is also automatic if the High Court has withdrawn a case from a subordinate court and itself sentenced the accused to death.
- In other criminal matters, an appeal under Art. 134 needs the High Court's **certificate**.
- **Art. 134A**, inserted by the **44th Amendment (1978)**, requires a High Court to consider granting a certificate under Arts. 132, 133 or 134 as soon as it delivers the judgment.
- **Art. 135** lets the Supreme Court exercise the jurisdiction of the old **Federal Court** in any leftover matters, until Parliament provides otherwise.
- **Art. 136** gives the Supreme Court a discretionary power to grant **Special Leave to Appeal (SLP)**.
- This SLP may be granted against the judgment or order of **any court or tribunal** in India.
- Art. 136 does **not** apply to any court or tribunal constituted under a law relating to the **armed forces**.
- **Art. 137** allows the Supreme Court to **review** its own judgment or order.
- **Art. 138** allows Parliament to **enlarge** the Supreme Court's jurisdiction by law.
- **Art. 139** allows Parliament to confer on the Supreme Court the power to issue writs for purposes **other than** Fundamental Rights.
- **Art. 139A**, inserted by the **44th Amendment**, allows the Supreme Court to transfer a case from one High Court to another (or to itself) when it involves the same question of law as a case already before it.
- **Art. 140** allows Parliament to confer on the Supreme Court any further powers that are necessary to exercise its existing jurisdiction.
- **Art. 141** makes the law declared by the Supreme Court **binding on all courts** within India.
- The Supreme Court itself is **not bound** by Art. 141 and may overrule its own earlier judgment.
- **Art. 142** empowers the Supreme Court to pass any decree or order necessary to do **complete justice** in a case before it.
- This power under Art. 142 is unique to the Supreme Court — the High Courts have **no equivalent article**.
- A decree or order passed under Art. 142 is enforceable throughout India.
- **Art. 143** allows the President to refer a question to the Supreme Court for its **advisory opinion**.
- An advisory opinion under Art. 143 is **not binding** on the President.
- Under **143(1)**, the Court *may* decline to answer a reference on a general question of law or fact.
- Under **143(2)**, for a dispute arising out of a pre-Constitution treaty or agreement, the Court *shall* report its opinion.
- **Art. 144** requires all civil and judicial authorities to **act in aid** of the Supreme Court.
- **Art. 145** lets the Supreme Court make rules of practice and procedure, with the President's approval.
- Under **145(3)**, a bench of at least **5** judges must decide any case involving a substantial question of constitutional interpretation, or a reference under Art. 143.
- **Art. 146** provides that the Supreme Court's officers and staff are appointed by the **CJI** (or a person the CJI directs).
- The Supreme Court's administrative expenses, including staff salaries and pensions, are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**.
- **Art. 147** clarifies that "interpretation of this Constitution" also covers, where relevant, the **Government of India Act, 1935** and the **Indian Independence Act, 1947**.

**Language (Art. 348):** until Parliament otherwise provides, **English** is the language of the SC and of every HC. A Governor, with the President’s prior consent, may authorise **Hindi** (or the official State language) in HC proceedings; judgments still have an English version.

## Supreme Court — composition & organisation

**CJI + judges** sit at Delhi; strength is by **ordinary law**, not a 368 amendment.

- The Supreme Court is established by **Art. 124(1)** — not by Art. 214 (that is the High Court) or Art. 226 (HC writs).
- Its seat is fixed at **Delhi** under Art. 130, with **no regular benches** outside Delhi.
- The Supreme Court's **strength** is fixed by Parliament through an **ordinary law**, not a constitutional amendment.
- Its original strength in 1950 was **8** judges.
- That strength grew over time: **11** (1956), **14** (1960), **18** (1977), **26** (1986), and **31** (2008).
- The **Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019** raised the strength to **34**, that is, the CJI plus 33 judges.
- A Supreme Court judge holds office until the age of **65**, with **no extension** of a sitting judge's term.
- A judge also gets **no second term** after retirement.
- A judge resigns by writing to the **President**, not to the CJI.
- The oath of a Supreme Court judge is set out in the **Third Schedule**.
- This oath is taken before the **President**, or a person appointed by the President for that purpose.
- The oath requires the judge to bear true faith to the Constitution, uphold its sovereignty, and perform duties without fear or favour.
- Under **Art. 126**, the President appoints an **Acting CJI**; by convention, this is the senior-most available Supreme Court judge.
- Under **Art. 127**, an **ad hoc judge** must be a **sitting High Court judge**, appointed only to fill a quorum gap.
- A **retired** judge sitting under Art. 128 is a different case altogether.
- A retired judge sits under **Art. 128** with the consent of both the **President** and the judge concerned.
- Such a retired judge draws allowances, but this is **not** a fresh tenure as a "sitting" judge past the age of 65.
- Under **Art. 125**, Parliament fixes the salaries of Supreme Court judges.
- These salaries were originally set out in the **Second Schedule**.
- They are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**.
- A judge's salary cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment, except during a Financial Emergency.
- An ordinary Supreme Court bench usually has **2–3** judges.
- A **Constitution Bench** must have at least **5** judges.
- The largest-ever bench was **13** judges, in *Kesavananda Bharati*.
- By convention, the **senior-most** Supreme Court judge becomes the CJI.
- This convention was broken in **1973**, when **A.N. Ray** was appointed CJI, superseding Justices Shelat, Hegde and Grover after *Kesavananda*.
- It was broken again in **1977**, when **M.H. Beg** was appointed CJI, superseding **H.R. Khanna**.
- Seniority as the basis for appointing the CJI was restored in **1978**, with **Y.V. Chandrachud** becoming CJI.

### Qualifications (Art. 124(3))
Citizen of India **and** any **one** of:
1. **High Court judge** for at least **5 years** (in succession), **or**
2. **Advocate of a High Court** for at least **10 years** (in succession), **or**
3. a **distinguished jurist** in the President’s opinion.

## Appointment & Collegium — Judges Cases

**Text of Art. 124(2):** every SC judge is appointed by the **President** (warrant under hand and seal) after **consultation** with such SC/HC judges as he deems necessary. For a judge other than the CJI, the **CJI must always be consulted**.

“Consultation” was defined by case law — **not** by the original text. The Collegium is **extra-constitutional** (judge-made).

**First Judges Case** (*S.P. Gupta*, **1981**)
- The Court held that "consultation" in Art. 124(2) does **not** mean concurrence.
- This gave the **executive primacy** in judicial appointments.
- The judgment also upheld the transfer of High Court judges without their consent, though this was later narrowed.

**Second Judges Case** (*SCAORA*, **1993**)
- The Court reinterpreted "consultation" to effectively mean **concurrence**.
- This gave **primacy to the CJI**, understood as an institution and not as an individual.
- This judgment created the **Collegium**: the CJI plus the **2** senior-most Supreme Court judges.
- The government is **bound** to appoint a name if the Collegium reiterates it.

**Third Judges Case** (Art. **143** reference, **1998**)
- For **Supreme Court** appointments, the Collegium is the CJI plus **4** senior-most judges.
- For **High Court** appointments and transfers, the Collegium is the CJI plus **2** senior-most judges.
- The Collegium must also consult the Chief Justice and 2 senior colleagues of the concerned High Court.
- It must further consult the senior-most Supreme Court judge who comes from the recommendee's parent High Court.
- If **two or more** Collegium judges dissent, the name is **not** sent to the government.
- The CJI's solo opinion, without the Collegium, does **not** amount to "consultation".

**NJAC / Fourth Judges Case** (*SCAORA*, **16 October 2015**)
- The Supreme Court struck down the **99th Amendment** and the **NJAC Act, 2014** by a **4:1** majority.
- Justice **Khehar** wrote the majority opinion; Justice **J. Chelameswar** dissented.
- The Court held that the NJAC violated the **basic structure** by compromising judicial independence.
- The **Collegium system was revived** as a result.

### NJAC as it stood (must-know composition)

- The **CJI** chaired the NJAC as an ex officio member.
- Two **senior-most Supreme Court judges** were ex officio members.
- The Union **Law Minister** was an ex officio member.
- Two **eminent persons** were nominated by a committee of the PM, the CJI, and the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha (or the leader of the largest opposition party).
- **One** of these two eminent persons had to be from the SC/ST/OBC/Minority/**Women** categories.
- An eminent person's tenure was **3 years**, with no re-nomination.
- If **any two** members disagreed with a recommendation, the appointment was **not** made — this was the NJAC's veto rule.

> **Exam note:** Chronology **1981 executive → 1993 CJI+2 → 1998 CJI+4 → 2014 NJAC in the text → 2015 struck down**. Collegium is **not** in the Constitution; NJAC **was**, and still fell.

**Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) after 2015:** The Collegium recommends a name, the Law Ministry and Intelligence Bureau send their inputs, and the PM then advises the President. The government may **return** a name once with reasons, but a **reiteration** by the Collegium binds the executive.

**Recommending the CJI:** the outgoing CJI recommends the **senior-most** Supreme Court judge as successor. The appointment is still formally made by the **President**.

## Transfer of judges (Art. 222)

- The **President** may transfer a High Court judge from one High Court to another under **Art. 222**.
- This transfer needs **consultation with the CJI**, which in practice means the Collegium (after 1993/98).
- The **consent of the judge** being transferred is **not** required (*Union of India v. Sankalchand Sheth*; confirmed in the Second Judges Case).
- A transfer is **not**, in law, treated as a punishment.
- A transfer is still **justiciable** if it is shown to be punitive or made in mala fide.
- Art. 222 applies **only to High Court judges** — Supreme Court judges are never "transferred".
- As a matter of policy, the Chief Justice of a High Court is ordinarily an **outsider** brought in by transfer.
- The **15th Amendment** provided a compensatory allowance for judges transferred under Art. 222.
- For any transfer, the Collegium consults the Chief Justices of **both** High Courts involved — the one the judge is leaving and the one they are joining.

## Removal / impeachment of judges (Arts. 124(4)–(5), 217(1)(b), 218)

Same process for **SC and HC**. Popularly “impeachment”; constitutionally it is an **address to the President** — **not** Art. 61.

- The only grounds for removal are **proved misbehaviour** or **incapacity** — no other ground is available.
- Under the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**, a removal motion needs **100** Lok Sabha members or **50** Rajya Sabha members to sign it.
- The Speaker or the Chairman may **admit or refuse** that motion.
- If admitted, a **3-member inquiry committee** is formed: an **SC judge**, a **High Court Chief Justice**, and a **distinguished jurist**.
- If this committee **clears** the judge, the motion automatically **drops**.
- If the committee finds the judge guilty, each House must pass the address by a **special majority** — a majority of the **total membership** plus **two-thirds of members present and voting**.
- This special-majority formula is the **same** as Art. 368's first limb, but a judge's removal is **not** a constitutional amendment and needs no State ratification.
- Both Houses must pass the address in the **same session**.
- The **President** then issues the actual order of removal.
- Since **1997**, the judiciary also runs an **in-house inquiry** for misconduct that falls short of removal; this process is not in the Constitution.

**No judge has ever actually been removed by this procedure.**
- **V. Ramaswami** (1993): the inquiry committee found him guilty, but the Lok Sabha motion **failed** because the Congress party abstained.
- **Soumitra Sen** (Calcutta HC, 2011): the Rajya Sabha **passed** the motion, but he **resigned** before the Lok Sabha could vote.
- **CJI Dipak Misra** (2018): a Rajya Sabha notice was moved, but the Vice-President as Chairman **did not admit** it.

> **Exam note:** Resignation → **President**. Removal → **President after parliamentary address**. The **CJI cannot** remove a judge.

## Independence of the judiciary

Constitutional shields so the executive cannot pack, starve, or discuss the courts at will.

**Mode of appointment**
- Judges are appointed through the **Collegium** system, so the executive alone cannot pack the court.

**Security of tenure**
- A Supreme Court or High Court judge can be removed only through the address procedure in **Art. 124(4)**.

**Fixed service conditions**
- A judge's salary is **charged** on the Consolidated Fund rather than voted on annually.
- That salary cannot be reduced to the judge's disadvantage after appointment.
- The only exception is a **Financial Emergency under Art. 360**.

**Charged expenditure**
- The salaries of Supreme Court judges, and the Court's administrative expenses under **Art. 146**, are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India**.
- The salaries of High Court judges are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of the State**.
- Under **Art. 221**, the pensions of High Court judges are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India**.

**Conduct not discussed**
- Parliament, under **Art. 121**, cannot discuss a judge's conduct in the discharge of their duties.
- A State legislature is under the same restriction by **Art. 211**.
- The only exception is a discussion during a motion for that judge's removal.

**Ban on practice after retirement**
- Under **Art. 124(7)**, a retired Supreme Court judge cannot plead or act before **any court or authority in India**.
- Under **Art. 220**, a retired High Court judge cannot practise before that **same** High Court or the courts subordinate to it.
- A retired High Court judge **may** practise before the Supreme Court and other High Courts.
- The **7th Amendment** replaced what had earlier been an absolute ban on a retired High Court judge's practice.

**Contempt power**
- The Supreme Court can punish for contempt of itself under **Art. 129**.
- A High Court has the same power under **Art. 215**.

**Own staff**
- The Supreme Court's officers and staff are appointed by the **CJI**, under **Art. 146**.
- A High Court's officers and staff are appointed by its **Chief Justice**, under **Art. 229**.

**Jurisdiction not easily cut**
- Parliament cannot take away the Supreme Court's writ jurisdiction under **Art. 32**.
- The **42nd Amendment** tried to curb judicial review by inserting Arts. 32A, 131A and 226A.
- The **43rd Amendment** restored the earlier position by repealing those three articles.

**Separation from the executive**
- **Art. 50**, a Directive Principle, calls for separating the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State.
- The **CrPC, 1973** implemented this by separating executive magistrates from judicial magistrates in most States.

**Power to make rules**
- The Supreme Court makes its own rules under **Art. 145**, with the President's approval.
- A High Court makes its own rules under **Art. 225** and its inherent powers.

## Court of Record (Arts. 129, 215)

| | Supreme Court (129) | High Court (215) |
|--|---------------------|------------------|
| Status | **Court of record** | **Court of record** |
| Meaning | Acts and proceedings recorded as **evidence**; power to **punish for contempt of itself** | Same |
| Subordinate contempt | Of itself and of courts **subordinate to it** | Of itself and of courts subordinate to it |
| “Complete justice” | **Art. 142 — SC only** | **No** 142 analogue |

- **Art. 141:** the law declared by the Supreme Court binds **all courts** within India.
- The Supreme Court itself **can overrule** its own earlier judgment through a larger bench.
- One High Court's judgment **does not bind** another High Court.
- **Art. 142:** the Supreme Court may pass any order needed for **complete justice**, and that order is enforceable throughout India.
- **Art. 144:** all civil and judicial authorities must **act in aid** of the Supreme Court.

## Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

Four buckets in the exam: **original · writ · appellate · advisory**, plus **review / SLP / other**.

### 1. Original — three different originals (do not mix)

- **Art. 131** gives the Supreme Court **exclusive** federal original jurisdiction.
- This covers disputes between the Union and a State, between the Union and States together, or between States.
- The dispute must involve a question of law or fact on which a **legal right** depends.
- **Art. 32** gives the Supreme Court original jurisdiction to enforce **Fundamental Rights only**.
- This Art. 32 jurisdiction is **not exclusive** — High Courts have a parallel writ power under Art. 226.
- **Art. 71** gives the Supreme Court **exclusive** original jurisdiction over disputes about the **election of the President or Vice-President**.
- If the Supreme Court later voids such an election, acts done by that President or Vice-President **before the date of the decision remain valid**.
- Parliament may regulate the procedure for such Art. 71 disputes by law.

**Art. 131 — cannot be used for**
- Enforcement of **FRs** (→ 32)
- Union/State **vs a private person** (or a PSU merely because the government holds shares — *State of Bihar v. UoI*)
- Disputes arising from a **pre-Constitution treaty / agreement / sanad** (proviso to 131) — those go, if at all, under **143(2)**

**Art. 262 — water (classic trap):** Parliament **may** provide a tribunal for inter-State river disputes and **may bar** the SC and other courts. The **Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 did bar** 131. So “SC has original jurisdiction over river-water disputes” is **false**.

### 2. Writ — Art. 32
- Art. 32 is itself a **Fundamental Right** — the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcing other FRs.
- The Supreme Court's five writs under Art. 32 are available **for Fundamental Right enforcement only**.
- Under **32(3)**, Parliament may empower **any other court** to issue these same writs within its local limits — this power is rarely used.
- Under **32(4)**, this right **shall not be suspended** except as the Constitution otherwise provides (that is, under **Art. 359**).
- **Art. 32** covers Fundamental Rights only; **Art. 226** additionally covers any other legal right.
- The **five writs** are: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto.

### 3. Appellate — Arts. 132–134A

- **Art. 132** allows an appeal from a High Court in a **civil, criminal, or other** case.
- This Art. 132 appeal needs an HC certificate that the case involves a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution**.
- **Art. 133** allows a **civil** appeal from a High Court.
- This needs a substantial question of law of general importance, and the HC's own opinion that the Supreme Court should decide it.
- The **30th Amendment (1972)** removed the old **₹20,000** pecuniary floor for this civil appeal.
- **Art. 134** allows a **criminal** appeal from a High Court in three situations.
- First, the HC on appeal has **reversed an acquittal** and sentenced the accused to **death**.
- Second, the HC has **withdrawn** a case from a subordinate court and itself sentenced the accused to death.
- Third, the HC has **certified** the case as fit for appeal to the Supreme Court.
- **Art. 134A**, added by the **44th Amendment (1978)**, requires the HC to **consider** granting a 132/133/134 certificate as soon as it delivers the judgment.
- Under **Art. 134(2)**, Parliament may enlarge this criminal appellate jurisdiction by law.
- The **Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Act, 1970** did this, allowing appeals as of right in specified cases of life imprisonment or a sentence of 10+ years.
- A certificate is **not** needed at all if the party instead goes under **Art. 136** (SLP).

### 4. Special Leave Petition — Art. 136 (widest)

- Under Art. 136, the Supreme Court **may** grant leave to appeal from **any** judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order.
- This leave can be granted against **any court or tribunal** in India, and is not limited to "final" orders.
- Granting leave is **not a right** for the party — it is entirely at the Supreme Court's **discretion**.
- Art. 136 is the **widest appellate window** in the Constitution.
- This SLP power **does not lie** against a court or tribunal relating to the **armed forces**.
- An SLP can **leapfrog** the High Court, and can even be filed from a tribunal that is not technically a "court".

### 5. Review — Art. 137
- Under Art. 137, the Supreme Court may review **its own** earlier judgment or order.
- This review follows the Supreme Court Rules, modelled on **Order XLVII of the CPC**.
- The recognised grounds are an **error apparent on the face of the record**, discovery of new evidence not earlier available despite diligence, or any other sufficient reason.
- A review petition is **not** a rehearing of the case on merits.
- A review is typically heard by a **smaller bench**, often the same judges, usually by circulation.
- A **curative petition** may follow only after a review has failed — see *Rupa Ashok Hurra* (2002) below.

### 6. Advisory — Art. 143

| | **143(1)** | **143(2)** |
|--|-----------|-----------|
| Who | **President** (CoM advice) | **President** |
| What | Question of **law or fact** of public importance | Dispute from a **pre-Constitution treaty / agreement** (the 131-proviso matters) |
| Can SC decline? | **Yes** | **No** — “the Court **shall** … report” |
| Binding? | **Not binding** (followed in practice) | Same — still advisory |
| Bench | **≥5** if it is a substantial constitutional question (145(3)) | Same |

**Famous 143 references (match-list):** *Delhi Laws Act* (1951) · *Berubari* (1960, cession needs Amd) · *Keshav Singh* (1965, privileges) · *Presidential Poll* (1974) · *Special Courts Bill* (1978) · *Cauvery* (1992) · **Third Judges** (1998) · *Gujarat Assembly election* (2002) · *2G / natural resources* (2012). NJAC was **litigation**, not 143.

### Other / residual
- Under **Art. 138**, Parliament may enlarge the Supreme Court's jurisdiction by law.
- Under **Art. 139**, Parliament may confer on the Supreme Court the power to issue writs for purposes other than Fundamental Rights.
- Under **Art. 140**, Parliament may grant the Supreme Court powers supplemental to its existing jurisdiction.
- Under **Art. 139A** (44th Amendment), the Supreme Court may transfer High Court cases that involve the **same question of law**.
- Under **Art. 317**, the President removes a **UPSC or SPSC** member only **after an inquiry by the Supreme Court** — this is not "impeachment".
- Under **Art. 329** and the Representation of the People Act, election petitions for an **MP or MLA** lie in the **High Court**, **not** the Supreme Court.
- Only **President and Vice-President** elections are decided as the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction, under **Art. 71**.

### 42nd / 43rd / 44th — judiciary

Emergency clipped judicial review; Janata restored it. **Not** a before/after pair table — three successive amendments.

- The **42nd Amendment (1976)** inserted **Art. 32A**, barring the Supreme Court from considering the constitutional validity of **State** laws.
- It also inserted **Art. 131A**, giving the Supreme Court **exclusive** jurisdiction over the validity of **Central** laws.
- It also inserted **Art. 226A**, barring High Courts from examining the validity of Central laws.
- It also inserted **Art. 228A**, and separately **narrowed Art. 226** and made **Art. 227** an administrative-only power.
- The **43rd Amendment (1977)** **repealed Arts. 32A, 131A and 226A**, so High Courts could **again** examine Central laws.
- The **44th Amendment (1978)** restored **Art. 226** and **Art. 227** to their full original scope.
- The 44th Amendment also added **Art. 134A** (certificate) and **Art. 139A** (transfer of cases).

> **Exam note:** 2019 A/R “SC has **exclusive** jurisdiction on constitutional validity of Central laws” is **false** because **43rd** killed **131A**.

## High Courts — Arts. 214–231 (elaboration)

**Part VI, Ch. V:** a High Court for each State — writs **wider** than the SC’s, age **62**.

- **Art. 214** provides that there **shall be** a High Court for each State.
- **Art. 215** makes every High Court a **court of record**, on the same footing as Art. 129 for the Supreme Court.
- **Art. 216** provides that a High Court consists of a Chief Justice and such other judges as the **President** may from time to time appoint.
- The Constitution places **no ceiling** on the number of judges of a High Court.
- **Art. 217** governs the appointment and tenure of a High Court judge.
- A High Court judge is appointed by the **President** and holds office until the age of **62**.
- A High Court judge resigns by writing to the **President**.
- A High Court judge's removal follows the same procedure as a Supreme Court judge, under **Art. 124(4)**.
- **Art. 218** applies the provisions of Arts. 124(4) and 124(5) to High Court judges as well.
- **Art. 219** requires a High Court judge to take oath before the **Governor** of the State (or, for a common High Court, before the Governor specified by the President).
- **Art. 220** restricts a retired High Court judge's practice.
- After the **7th Amendment**, a retired judge cannot practise before **that same** High Court or the courts subordinate to it, but **may** practise before the Supreme Court and other High Courts.
- **Art. 221** provides that a High Court judge's salary is determined by Parliament.
- That salary is **charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State**.
- A High Court judge's **pension**, however, is charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India**.
- **Art. 222** allows the President to transfer a High Court judge to another High Court, after consulting the CJI.
- The transferred judge's **consent is not required**.
- **Art. 223** allows the President to appoint an **Acting Chief Justice** of a High Court.
- This applies when the office of Chief Justice is vacant or the Chief Justice is temporarily absent; by convention, the senior-most judge of that HC is appointed.
- **Art. 224** provides for **additional** and **acting** judges of a High Court.
- An **additional judge** is appointed for a temporary increase in business or arrears, for a term of **up to 2 years**, and must in any case vacate office at **62**.
- An **acting judge** is appointed when a sitting judge is absent or is acting as Chief Justice.
- **Art. 224A**, inserted by the **15th Amendment**, lets the Chief Justice of a High Court, with the President's previous consent, request a **retired** High Court judge to sit and act.
- **Art. 225** continues the pre-Constitution jurisdiction of each High Court, subject to the Constitution and to any law.
- **Art. 226** gives a High Court writ jurisdiction for enforcing Fundamental Rights **and for any other purpose**.
- This makes Art. 226 **wider** than the Supreme Court's Art. 32.
- The **15th Amendment** allowed a High Court to issue such a writ if the cause of action arises wholly or in part within its territory, even if the authority concerned sits outside it.
- **Art. 227** gives a High Court the power of **superintendence** over all courts and tribunals in its territory, except those relating to the **armed forces**.
- This superintendence covers both **administrative and judicial** matters; the **44th Amendment** restored this full power after the **42nd Amendment** had made it administrative-only.
- **Art. 228** lets a High Court **withdraw** a case from a subordinate court if it involves a substantial question of constitutional interpretation, and decide that question itself.
- **Art. 229** provides that a High Court's officers and staff are appointed by its **Chief Justice**.
- The High Court's administrative expenses are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of the State**.
- **Art. 230** allows Parliament to extend a High Court's jurisdiction to a Union Territory, or to exclude a Union Territory from its jurisdiction.
- **Art. 231** allows Parliament, by an **ordinary law**, to establish a **common High Court** for two or more States, or for a State and a Union Territory.
- Creating a common High Court under Art. 231 is **not** a constitutional amendment under Art. 368.

## High Courts — organisation

**25 High Courts**; a judge of an HC is **not** a “mini SC judge” (age **62**, **no** distinguished-jurist limb).

- India has **25** High Courts, after Andhra Pradesh and Telangana split into separate High Courts on **1 January 2019**.
- The **strength** of a High Court is **not fixed** in the Constitution — the President decides it from time to time.
- A High Court judge holds office until the age of **62**.
- The **15th Amendment (1963)** raised this age from **60 to 62**.
- The **114th Constitutional Amendment Bill**, which proposed raising the HC retirement age to **65**, **lapsed**.
- A High Court judge resigns by writing to the **President**.
- The removal of a High Court judge follows the **same procedure** as for a Supreme Court judge.
- A High Court judge takes oath before the **Governor**.
- Under **Art. 217(2)**, a High Court judge must be a citizen of India and either a **judicial officer in India for 10 years**, or an **advocate of a High Court for 10 years** (in succession).
- Unlike the Supreme Court, there is **no "distinguished jurist"** route for a High Court judge.
- The President appoints a High Court judge after consulting the **CJI and the Governor** of the State (and the Chief Justice of that HC, if a judge other than the CJ is being appointed).
- The Collegium for this consultation is the CJI plus **2** senior-most Supreme Court judges, together with that High Court's Chief Justice and its two senior-most judges.
- By convention, the Chief Justice of a High Court is an **outsider** brought in by transfer.
- Even a transferred Chief Justice still takes oath before the **Governor of that State**.

**Chartered High Courts (Indian High Courts Act, 1861 → Letters Patent 1862) — oldest three, with original civil jurisdiction inside the presidency town:** **Calcutta, Bombay, Madras** (all **1862**). Calcutta is the **first** (1 July 1862).

## Allahabad High Court (UP Special)

**Largest High Court in India**; Lucknow is a **bench**, not a second HC.

- The **Indian High Courts Act, 1861** was the enabling statute; the actual **Letters Patent** for this HC were issued on **17 March 1866**.
- Its birth name was the **High Court of Judicature for the North-Western Provinces**.
- Its **first seat** was at **Agra** (1866), replacing the old **Sadr Diwani Adalat**.
- The seat **shifted to Allahabad in 1869**.
- It took the name **"High Court of Judicature at Allahabad"** under a supplementary Letters Patent in **1919**.
- Its **first Chief Justice** was Sir **Walter Morgan**.
- The **Lucknow Bench** was created by the **United Provinces High Courts (Amalgamation) Order, 1948**.
- This Order merged the **Chief Court of Oudh** (which had existed at Lucknow from **1925**) into the Allahabad High Court.
- Lucknow became a **permanent bench**, while the **principal seat** stayed at **Allahabad (Prayagraj)**.
- The Lucknow Bench handles the **Avadh/Oudh** districts, classically **12** in number, while the rest of UP is heard at Allahabad.
- Its sanctioned strength is **160** judges, making it the **largest High Court in India**.
- Its jurisdiction covers the whole of **Uttar Pradesh** only — Uttarakhand has had its own **Nainital High Court** since 2000.

> **Exam note:** Lucknow is a **bench**, not a second High Court and not a “common HC”. Allahabad is **not** a Chartered HC of 1862.

## Jurisdiction of a High Court

**226 is the engine**; **227** is superintendence — neither is a substitute for appeal.

- Under **Art. 225**, along with its charter or statute, a **Chartered High Court** has **original civil jurisdiction** for its presidency town.
- This original jurisdiction also covers company matters, matrimonial cases, admiralty, and contempt.
- The biggest slice of original jurisdiction is the **writ power under Art. 226**.
- Under **Art. 226**, a High Court can issue writs for enforcing **Fundamental Rights, and for any other legal right**.
- This writ jurisdiction is **discretionary** — the HC may refuse relief for laches or if an alternative remedy exists.
- The **15th Amendment** lets a High Court issue a writ if the **cause of action** arises wholly or in part inside its territory, even if the authority concerned sits outside.
- Under **226(4)**, this power does **not** affect the Supreme Court's power under **Art. 32**.
- A High Court's **appellate** jurisdiction covers appeals from district, sessions, and other courts as the law provides.
- **Letters-patent appeals** are a special appellate route available in the Chartered High Courts.
- Under **Art. 227**, a High Court has **supervisory** power over all courts and tribunals in its territory, except those relating to the armed forces.
- This supervisory power covers both **administrative and judicial** matters, a scope the **44th Amendment** restored.
- Neither Art. 226 nor Art. 227 is meant to be a **substitute for a regular appeal**.
- Under **Art. 215**, a High Court is a **court of record**: it can record its proceedings as evidence and punish for contempt of itself.
- Under **Art. 235**, the **control** over district judges and the judicial service — posting, promotion, and leave — rests with the **High Court**.
- The **appointment** of district judges, by contrast, is governed by **Art. 233** (see Subordinate Courts below).
- Under **Art. 228**, a High Court may **withdraw** a case from a subordinate court if it raises a substantial question of constitutional interpretation.

### SC writ vs HC writ

| | Art. 32 (SC) | Art. 226 (HC) |
|--|--------------|----------------|
| Nature | **Fundamental Right** | Constitutional right (**not** a FR) |
| Scope | **FR only** | FR **+ any other legal right** |
| Territorial | All-India | Territory / **cause of action** (15th) |
| Against | State (Art. 12) | State; some writs (habeas, quo warranto) can reach private persons |
| Refusal | SC ordinarily **cannot** refuse if an FR is breached | HC **may** refuse (discretion) |
| Suspend | Only **359** | Not a FR — but 226 itself was **narrowed by 42nd, restored by 44th** |

## Judicial Review

Power of courts to test **constitutionality** of legislative and executive action and strike down what is ultra vires. Itself **basic structure** (*Kesavananda* 1973; *Minerva Mills* 1980; *L. Chandra Kumar* 1997). **Not one article** — scattered.

- **Art. 13** declares that any law inconsistent with the Fundamental Rights is **void**.
- **Arts. 32 and 226** provide the writs that operationally enforce judicial review.
- **Art. 131** (federal original), **Art. 136** (SLP), and **Art. 143** (advisory) also carry judicial-review functions.
- **Arts. 245–246, 251 and 254** decide legislative **competence** and settle **repugnancy** between Union and State laws.
- **Art. 227** gives a High Court superintendence over courts **and tribunals** in its territory.
- **Art. 372** continues pre-Constitution laws, but only **subject to** the Constitution.

**Three slices:** review of **legislation** · of **administrative action** · of **judicial decisions** (appeal / revision / 136).

**JR ≠ appeal.** Appeal rehear **merits** (error of fact/law). JR asks only: did the authority have **jurisdiction**, follow **procedure**, and stay inside the **Constitution**? Administrative-law grounds (exam shorthand): **illegality · irrationality (Wednesbury) · procedural impropriety**.

| India | USA | UK |
|-------|-----|-----|
| **Explicit** (13, 32, 226…) | *Marbury v. Madison* (implied) | **Parliamentary supremacy** — no JR of primary Acts |
| JR = **basic structure** | No “basic structure” label | — |

- ***Kesavananda Bharati* (1973)** held that even **constitutional amendments** are subject to judicial review under the basic structure doctrine.
- ***Minerva Mills* (1980)** held that judicial review is **itself part of the basic structure**, and struck down the 42nd Amendment's over-reaching **Art. 31C**.
- ***I.R. Coelho* (2007)** held that laws placed in the **9th Schedule** after **24 April 1973** remain open to judicial review if they damage the basic structure.
- ***S.P. Sampath Kumar* (1987)** upheld the CAT and treated it as an **effective substitute** for the High Court — tribunal order could go **directly to the SC**, skipping the HC.
- ***L. Chandra Kumar* (1997)** overruled that substitute theory.
- The Court held that **HC power under Arts. 226/227 over tribunals is part of the basic structure** and cannot be excluded.
- After *L. Chandra Kumar*, a tribunal is only a **court of first instance** that **supplements** (does not replace) the HC’s place in the hierarchy.

> **Exam note:** 42nd clipped JR (**32A / 131A / 226A**); **43rd** restored. A tribunal clause that “excludes all courts except the SC” **cannot** oust the HC.

## Judicial Activism vs Judicial Restraint vs Overreach

Term **“judicial activism”**: Arthur **Schlesinger Jr., 1947** (USA). In India it is **post-Emergency**, via PIL + Art. **21** expansion + Art. **142**. **Not** in the Constitution.

| | **Activism** | **Restraint** | **Overreach** |
|--|--------------|---------------|---------------|
| Meaning | Court fills a **gap** where the other organs have failed | Court **defers**; decides only what is necessary | Court **takes over** legislative/executive policy |
| Vehicle | PIL, 142, expansive **21** | Literal reading; *A.K. Gopalan* | Continuing mandamus that runs a ministry |
| Examples | *Maneka*; *Hussainara*; *Vishaka*; *M.C. Mehta*; *Olga Tellis* | Refusal to rewrite a tax statute | Highway liquor ban / some firecracker / anthem directions (criticised as policy) |

**Art. 21 expansions that are activism-locks (match):** speedy trial · legal aid · livelihood (*Olga Tellis*) · privacy (*Puttaswamy*) · clean environment (*M.C. Mehta*) · dignity · emergency medical care (*Parmanand Katara*).

> **Exam note:** Activism can be **constitutional** (enforcing FRs). Overreach is activism that **violates separation of powers**. Restraint is a **doctrine of self-limit**, not an article. *ADM Jabalpur* is **bad restraint** (Emergency habeas).

## PIL (Public Interest Litigation)

**Not in the text.** Judge-made through **32** and **226**. USA 1960s civil-rights litigation is the cousin; India took it up **after the Emergency**.

**Traditional lis vs PIL**

| | Traditional | PIL |
|--|-------------|-----|
| Who | Aggrieved person only (**locus standi**) | Any **public-spirited** person / NGO; court may act **suo motu** |
| Form | Formal petition | Even a **letter / postcard** (**epistolary jurisdiction**) |
| Character | Adversarial | **Inquisitorial / collaborative** (court, amicus, reports) |
| Relief | Personal | For a **class** of victims who cannot come to court |
| Against | Private or State | Primarily **State / Art. 12 authorities** — not a private quarrel |

**Features (raata)**
1. Relaxed locus standi.  
2. Epistolary + **suo motu**.  
3. Inquisitorial (commissions, NHRC, CPCB reports).  
4. Preventive **and** remedial.  
5. Directed at **public injury**, not private gain.  
6. Court may appoint **amicus curiae**.  
7. Continuing mandamus (monitor compliance).  
8. Costs / dismissal for **frivolous** PILs.

**Who / what is not PIL (SC 1998 guidelines — exam “cannot”)**  
Personal / political / publicity interest · landlord–tenant · service matter of an individual · admission to a college · recovery of a loan · a dispute already in a competent court.

- ***Mumbai Kamgar Sabha* (1976)**, decided by Justice **Krishna Iyer**, is treated as an early precursor to PIL.
- ***Hussainara Khatoon* (1979)** was about the plight of undertrial prisoners.
- This case held that a **speedy trial** is part of the right to life under **Art. 21**, and is treated as the **first big PIL**.
- ***Sunil Batra* (1978/80)** dealt with prisoners' rights.
- ***PUDR* (Asiad workers, 1982)** dealt with bonded and contract labour.
- ***S.P. Gupta* (1981)** held that **any member of the public** with **sufficient interest** can move the court in a PIL.
- ***Bandhua Mukti Morcha* (1984)** dealt with bonded labour.
- This case also confirmed that even a **letter** can be treated as a petition.
- ***Olga Tellis* (1985)** was about the livelihood rights of pavement dwellers.

| | **Review (Art. 137)** | **Curative** |
|--|----------------------|--------------|
| Source | **137** + SC Rules (Order XLVII analogue) | Judge-made — ***Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra* (2002)** |

## Subordinate Courts (Arts. 233–237)

**Part VI, Ch. VI.** The “district judiciary” — **not** “lower” in independence; only in hierarchy.

- Under **Art. 233(1)**, district judges are **appointed by the Governor** of the State.
- This appointment needs **consultation with the High Court** of that State.
- **Art. 233(2)** governs a **direct recruit from the Bar** — a person not already in Union or State service.
- Such a recruit must be an **advocate for 7 years** and must be **recommended by the High Court**.
- A **promotee** from the judicial service does **not** need this 7-year Bar requirement.
- **Art. 233A**, inserted by the **20th Amendment (1966)**, **validated** certain earlier district-judge appointments that had been made without proper HC consultation.
- **Art. 234** governs recruitment to the **State judicial service**, for posts below district judge.
- This recruitment is by the **Governor**, after consulting the **High Court and the State Public Service Commission**.
- In practice, this service has two entry streams: **direct recruitment from the Bar** and **promotion** from within the service.
- **Art. 235** vests **control** over district courts and the courts subordinate to them in the **High Court**.
- This HC control covers posting, promotion, leave, transfer, and discipline of district judges and of the judicial service below them.
- Under **Art. 236**, "district judge" is defined to include a judge of a **city civil court**, an additional/joint/assistant district judge, the chief judge of a **small-cause court**, a sessions or additional or assistant sessions judge, and a chief (or additional) presidency magistrate.
- Under **Art. 236**, "judicial service" means the service of persons holding the post of district judge and other **civil judicial** posts **inferior to** district judge.
- **Art. 237** lets the Governor **direct** that Arts. 233–235 apply to certain classes of **magistrates**, subject to specified exceptions.

> **Exam note:** **Appointment of DJ = Governor + HC (233). Control after appointment = HC alone (235).** The Governor has **no** disciplinary role over the district judiciary. *All India Judges’ Association* cases: uniform service conditions; Centre was asked to consider an AIJS.

### District Courts — hierarchy & work

```
SC
 └─ High Court
     └─ District & Sessions Judge     ← SAME person: civil = DJ; criminal = Sessions
         ├─ Civil Judge (Senior Division)  ≈  Chief Judicial Magistrate
         └─ Civil Judge (Junior Division)  ≈  Judicial Magistrate 1st / 2nd class
```

- The **District and Sessions Judge** is one person holding two roles.
- As **District Judge**, they head the principal civil court of **original** jurisdiction and hear appeals from junior civil judges.
- As **Sessions Judge**, they try offences punishable with death, life imprisonment, or 10+ years, as the Code provides, and hear criminal appeals from magistrates.
- A **Civil Judge (Senior Division)**, who doubles as **Chief Judicial Magistrate**, handles higher-value original civil suits and can try criminal cases up to **7 years'** imprisonment under the CrPC.
- A **Civil Judge (Junior Division)**, who doubles as a **Judicial Magistrate**, handles the lowest original civil suits and petty crime as JM-I or JM-II.
- The **CrPC, 1973** separated **judicial** magistrates from **executive** magistrates, in line with **Art. 50**.
- Executive magistrates, such as the DM or SDM, handle only **preventive** and law-and-order work, not regular trials.
- On the civil side, an appeal moves from Junior Civil Judge to Senior/District Judge to the High Court and then the Supreme Court.
- On the criminal side, an appeal moves from a Judicial Magistrate to the Sessions Court to the High Court and then the Supreme Court, with **Art. 136** always available.

### Family Courts Act, **1984**
- A Family Court is set up by the **State Government**, in consultation with the **High Court**.
- Every district with a population of **1 million or more** *shall* have a Family Court; others *may* have one.
- A Family Court handles marriage, divorce, custody, maintenance, and property disputes between spouses.
- It must attempt **conciliation first**, before a regular trial.
- It is **not bound** by the strict procedure of the CPC, and counsellors sit with the judge.
- Its judge holds the rank of a **District Judge** or equivalent.

### All-India Judicial Service (Art. **312**)
- The **42nd Amendment** inserted the phrase "including an **all-India judicial service**" into **Art. 312**.
- Creating this service needs a **Rajya Sabha resolution** passed by **2/3 of members present and voting**, followed by a **Parliament law**.
- That Parliament law **may amend Chapter VI of Part VI**, and this is **not** treated as a constitutional amendment under Art. 368.
- The service **cannot** include any post **inferior to district judge** (312(3), read with Art. 236).
- The AIJS has **not been created till date**.
- The **1st and 14th Law Commission Reports** had recommended it.
- *All India Judges' Association* (1992) directed the Centre to consider creating it.
- A **1993 review** left the timing of its creation to the executive's discretion.

## Tribunals — Part XIVA (Arts. 323A–323B)

**42nd Amendment, 1976** inserted **Part XIVA**. (2023 trap: Tribunals ≠ **Part XI**. XI = Union–State relations.)

Why tribunals: specialised, faster, take load off HCs. They are **not** a parallel Supreme Court.

| | **Art. 323A** | **Art. 323B** |
|--|---------------|---------------|
| Subject | **Administrative** tribunals — Union / State **public services** | **Other** tribunals |
| Who may legislate | **Only Parliament** | **Parliament or a State legislature** (for matters in its list) |
| Coverage | Service matters of persons appointed to Union/State public services and posts | **Full 323B list:** levy/collection/enforcement of any **tax**; foreign exchange / import-export; **industrial and labour**; **land reforms**; urban ceiling; **elections to the legislature** (not President/VP/MP election-petitions under 71/329); production/supply of **foodstuffs** and other essential goods; rent/tenancy of urban property; and connected offences |
| Text on courts | May exclude **all courts except the SC** | Same in the text |
| After *L. Chandra Kumar* | Exclusion of **HC (226/227) is void**. Tribunal = **court of first instance** → HC **Division Bench** → SC | Same logic |

**Three separate tribunal stories — do not merge them in revision.**

| Track | Question | Cases / law |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| **A. Can the HC be skipped?** | Does the tribunal **replace** the HC in the hierarchy, or only sit **below** it? | *Sampath Kumar* → *L. Chandra Kumar* |
| **B. Who sits on the tribunal?** | Must members be judges, and must **judicial members** form the majority? | *Madras Bar Association* I / II / III |
| **C. How are members appointed?** | Can Parliament fix a **4-year term**, control search committees, etc.? | **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** — partly struck in MBA-III |

**Track A — substitute vs supplement (appeal path, not vocabulary)**

- **“Substitute”** (*S.P. Sampath Kumar*, **1987**): Parliament may set up the **CAT** as an **effective substitute** for the High Court in **service matters**.
- Under that design, the statutory “exclude all courts except the SC” clause was read to mean: **Tribunal → Supreme Court (SLP)** — the **HC was bypassed**.
- **“Supplement”** (*L. Chandra Kumar*, **1997**): the Court **overruled** the substitute theory.
- A tribunal is only a **court of first instance**; it **supplements** the ordinary judiciary by taking specialised cases **at the bottom** of the ladder — it does **not** replace HC supervision.
- **HC writ/supervisory jurisdiction (Arts. 226/227) over tribunals is part of the basic structure** and cannot be ousted by Part XIVA text or tribunal law.
- **Current appeal ladder:** Tribunal order → challenge before **HC (Division Bench, 226/227)** → **SC** (SLP/appeal). The old **Tribunal-only-then-SC** shortcut is **dead after 1997**.

**Track B — composition (*Madras Bar Association*)**

- ***Madras Bar Association v. Union of India* (2014):** NCLT/NCLAT must have **adequate judicial representation**; technical/administrative members cannot dominate.
- ***Madras Bar Association v. Union of India* (2020):** struck parts of the **Tribunal Reforms (Rationalisation and Conditions of Service) Ordinance / Act, 2020** on similar independence grounds.
- ***Madras Bar Association v. Union of India* (2021):** reviewed the **Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021** — see Track C.

**Track C — Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 (statutory reforms, not the Sampath/Chandra Kumar pair)**

- The Act tried to unify tribunal appointments, impose a **4-year term** (renewable), and reshape search-cum-selection committees.
- In **MBA-III (2021)**, the SC **struck down / read down** several clauses (including the rigid **4-year tenure** and executive-heavy appointment design) as damaging **tribunal independence**.
- **Exam lock:** constitutional position = *L. Chandra Kumar*; composition = *Madras Bar* line; **2021 Act is not the last word** — always pair the Act with **MBA-III**.

### CAT / SAT / JAT (Administrative Tribunals Act, **1985**)

- The **CAT** became functional on **1 November 1985**, with its Principal Bench at **New Delhi**.
- It started with **5** benches and has grown to around **19**, plus circuit benches.
- The CAT covers the **All-India Services**, Union civil services, civil posts under the Union, and specifically **notified** organisations.
- The CAT does **not** cover the **armed forces**, officers and servants of the **Supreme Court**, or the **secretarial staff of Parliament** (and, for a SAT, the analogous State-legislature staff).
- A **SAT** is set up by the Central Government **on a State's request**.
- Its Chairman and members are appointed by the **President**, after consulting the **Governor** of that State.
- A **JAT** is a single tribunal shared by **two or more** States.
- The composition of these tribunals is a **Chairman** (who is or has been a High Court judge), along with judicial members and administrative members.
- The current appeal path runs from CAT/SAT to the **High Court (Arts. 226/227)** and then to the Supreme Court.
- A direct "**only SLP**" path to the Supreme Court, bypassing the HC, was the **pre-1997** design before *L. Chandra Kumar*.

### Other tribunals (year / parent Act — match)

- **ITAT (Income Tax):** **1941** — oldest; 323B tax.
- **CAT:** Administrative Tribunals Act **1985** (323A).
- **NGT:** **National Green Tribunal Act, 2010**. Principal Bench **Delhi**; others Pune, Bhopal, Chennai, Kolkata. Chairperson = SC/HC judge.
- **AFT:** **Armed Forces Tribunal Act, 2007** — the reason CAT **excludes** armed forces. Appeal to SC.
- **NCLT / NCLAT:** Companies Act; NCLT from **2016**. Company law + IBC.
- **DRT / DRAT:** Recovery of Debts (banks/FIs), **1993**.
- **SAT (SEBI):** Securities Appellate Tribunal — capital markets (do **not** mix with State Administrative Tribunal).
- **NCDRC:** consumer — National Commission (not a 323A body).

## Legal aid, Lok Adalat, Gram Nyayalaya, Fast Track Courts

**Art. 39A** (42nd, 1976): equal justice and **free legal aid**. Statute: **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** (came into force **9 Nov 1995** = **National Legal Services Day**).

- **NALSA:** Patron-in-Chief **CJI**. Executive Chairman: a serving SC judge nominated by the CJI.
- **State Legal Services Authority:** Patron **HC CJ**. Executive Chairman: a serving HC judge.
- **District Legal Services Authority:** **District Judge** as Chairman.

**Who gets free legal aid (1987 Act — typical list):** women and children · SC/ST · industrial workmen · victims of disaster / trafficking / mass disaster · persons in custody · persons with disability · persons with income below the notified ceiling.

### Lok Adalat vs Permanent Lok Adalat

| | **Lok Adalat** | **Permanent Lok Adalat (2002, Ch. VIA)** |
|--|----------------|------------------------------------------|
| First (independent India) | **Gujarat, 1982** (statutory only from 1987) | Statutory from the **2002** amendment |
| Cases | **Pending** in a court **and** pre-litigation | **Pre-litigation only** |
| Subject | Civil + **compoundable** criminal | **Public utility services** only: transport, posts, telegraph, power/water, sanitation, hospitals, insurance, education… |
| Pecuniary cap | **None** | **₹1 crore** |
| If no settlement | Returned to the court — **cannot** impose a decision | **May decide on merits** |
| Award | **= civil-court decree. Final. No appeal.** (226 still open) | Same |
| Bench | Sitting/retired judicial officer + 2 others | Chairperson (**qualified to be a DJ**) + 2 persons with public-utility experience |

> **Exam note:** Regular Lok Adalat = **compromise only**. PLA = the one forum that can **adjudicate**. Do not mix **SAT (SEBI)** with **SAT (State Administrative Tribunal)** or with Lok Adalat.

### Gram Nyayalaya (Gram Nyayalayas Act, **2008**)

A **court** at the intermediate-Panchayat HQ — **not** a Gram Panchayat / Nyaya Panchayat.

- The Gram Nyayalaya idea came from the Law Commission's **114th Report**, aimed at justice at the doorstep under **Art. 39A**.
- The **Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008** came into force on **2 October 2009**.
- Its seat is the headquarters of the **intermediate Panchayat**, or a group of contiguous Gram Panchayats.
- It also holds **mobile** sittings in villages.
- Its judge, called the **Nyayadhikari**, holds the rank of a **Judicial Magistrate 1st Class** or **Civil Judge (Junior Division)**.
- The Nyayadhikari is appointed by the **State Government**, in consultation with the **High Court**.
- A Gram Nyayalaya handles both **civil** cases (First Schedule) and **criminal** cases (Second Schedule), of a petty or specified kind.
- It must attempt **conciliation first**.
- It is **not bound** by the Indian Evidence Act or the strict CPC/CrPC procedure, and is instead guided by **natural justice** and summary procedure.
- Only **one appeal** is allowed: a civil case goes to the **District Court**, and a criminal case to the **Sessions Court**.
- A Gram Nyayalaya is **not** the same as a Gram Panchayat or a Nyaya Panchayat — it is a proper **court**.

### Fast Track Courts

- Fast Track Courts originated from a recommendation of the **11th Finance Commission (2000)**.
- Around **1,734** such courts were sanctioned to clear long-pending **sessions** cases and undertrial backlogs.
- Fast Track Courts are **not** a constitutional creation.
- Their judges are usually **additional district or sessions judges** appointed under the scheme.
- Several States have **continued** these courts even after the Finance Commission funding period ended.
- Later, dedicated **Fast Track Special Courts (FTSCs)** were set up from **2019** for **rape and POCSO** cases, funded through the Department of Justice and the Nirbhaya Fund.

## Contempt of Court

**Inherent** in courts of record (**129 / 215**); the 1971 Act only **regulates**.

- The Supreme Court's power to punish for contempt of itself under **Art. 129**, and a High Court's under **Art. 215**, is **inherent** in their status as courts of record.
- Parliament may **regulate** this power (under Entry 77 of List I / Entry 14 of List III), but **cannot take the power away**.
- The **Contempt of Courts Act, 1971** replaced the earlier 1952 Act.
- The 1971 Act was based on the recommendations of the **Sanyal Committee (1961)**, chaired by H.N. Sanyal, then Additional Solicitor General.
- **Civil contempt** means the wilful **disobedience** of a judgment, decree, order, or undertaking given to a court.
- **Criminal contempt** covers any publication or act that **scandalises** the court, **prejudices** a pending proceeding, or **interferes** with the administration of justice.
- Under **section 12**, the punishment is simple imprisonment up to **6 months**, or a fine up to **₹2,000**, or both.
- An honest **apology** may purge the contempt and avoid punishment.
- Under **section 20**, no contempt proceeding can be started after **one year** from the date of the alleged act.
- Under **section 15**, a court may take cognizance of criminal contempt **suo motu**.
- It may also act on a motion by the Attorney General or Solicitor General (for the Supreme Court) or the Advocate General (for a High Court).
- It may also act on a motion by any other person, but only **with the written consent** of that law officer.
- **Suo motu** cognizance needs **no** such consent.
- The **2006 Amendment** made **truth** a valid defence, if it is in the **public interest** and stated in good faith.
- Other recognised defences include innocent publication or distribution, a fair and accurate report of an already **decided** case, fair criticism of a judicial act, and a good-faith complaint to a superior.
- **Art. 19(2)** lists **contempt of court** as one of the permitted restrictions on the freedom of speech under Art. 19(1)(a).
- A **High Court** punishes contempt of the courts subordinate to it.
- A subordinate court **cannot** try its own contempt case — it must **refer** the matter to the High Court.
- In ***Supreme Court Bar Association* (1998)**, the Court held it **cannot**, while punishing contempt, **suspend an advocate's licence** to practise.
- That disciplinary power belongs only to the **Bar Council**; the Supreme Court's own power is limited to punishment under Arts. 129/142.

> **Exam note:** 1971 Act is a **ceiling for HCs**; SC’s 129 power is **constitutional** and not confined by the Act, but the Act is used as a **guide**.

## ADR (basic)

**S. 89 CPC** (after 1999/2002 amendments): where it appears that there exist elements of a settlement, the court **shall** formulate terms and **may refer** the parties to one of five:

1. **Arbitration**  
2. **Conciliation**  
3. **Judicial settlement** including **Lok Adalat**  
4. **Mediation**  
5. (the fifth limb is judicial settlement / Lok Adalat as above — treat **five names** as: arbitration, conciliation, mediation, judicial settlement, Lok Adalat)

- In **negotiation**, the parties themselves reach an agreement, which binds them only once they sign it.
- In **mediation**, a neutral third party **facilitates** a settlement but does **not** impose one; the **Mediation Act, 2023** now gives such a settlement agreement statutory force.
- In **conciliation**, the neutral third party **may suggest** terms of settlement, under Part III of the **1996 Act**; the resulting settlement agreement has the same status as an arbitral award on agreed terms.
- In **arbitration**, the arbitrator actually **adjudicates** the dispute.
- The resulting **arbitral award** has the force of a court **decree**, under the **Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996** (based on the UNCITRAL model), as amended in 2015, 2019 and 2021.
- A regular **Lok Adalat** works only by **compromise**; a **Permanent Lok Adalat** can decide on **merits**. Either way, the result is a decree with **no appeal**.
- In **judicial settlement**, a judge acts as the settler, and the settlement takes the form of a court order.

**Other locks:** The **Commercial Courts Act, 2015** made **pre-institution mediation** compulsory for specified commercial suits. The **Mediation Act, 2023** enables online and court-annexed mediation. The constitutional hook for all of ADR remains **Art. 39A**.

> **Exam note:** Arbitration is **adjudicatory**. Mediation / conciliation / regular Lok Adalat are **consensual**. **PLA** is the hybrid that can decide merits. **FTC is not ADR** — it is a regular court on a fast track.

- **Subordinate court tries its own contempt:** it **refers** to the **HC**.
