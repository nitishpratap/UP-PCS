## 7.0 Natural Vegetation — Framework

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Natural vegetation** | Plant communities that grow without human cultivation |
| **Climatic climax** | Stable vegetation expected under a given climate if succession runs its course |
| **Biome** | Large regional ecosystem defined by climate and dominant vegetation |
| **Plant acclimatization** | Physiological adjustment of plants/organisms to a new climatic setting |

### Overview

Natural vegetation questions begin with climate control, then ask India forest types or world scrub names. Treat vegetation as a readable climate map: rainfall and temperature set the broad type; soil and relief fine-tune it.

#### How It Works

- Climate (temperature + rainfall) is the primary control on natural vegetation at continental scale.
- Soil depth, drainage, salinity and parent rock modify local patches inside the same climate zone.
- Relief and altitude create vertical vegetation belts on mountains.
- Biotic factors include grazing, fire, pests and human clearing.
- Time matters: recently disturbed land may hold secondary scrub, not climax forest.
- The exam phrase **vegetation is the true index of climate** is keyed to **Thornthwaite** in UPPCS 2018.
- Latitudinal diversity gradient: tropics hold more species than temperate and polar regions.
- Acclimatization is different from evolution: it is adjustment within an organism’s lifetime/physiology.
- Xerophytes (dry), hydrophytes (wet) and mesophytes (moderate) are moisture-adaptation classes.
- India’s school classification (evergreen, deciduous, thorn, montane, littoral) is enough for most Prelims stems.

> **Exam note:** Do not confuse Köppen’s climate letters with Thornthwaite’s vegetation-as-climate-index statement.

### Exam Facts (raata — traps only)

| # | Fact to remember |
|---|------------------|
| 1 | Thornthwaite is linked with 'vegetation is the true index of climate' |
| 2 | Species diversity generally falls from equator toward poles |
| 3 | Amazon/equatorial forests are classic high-biodiversity locks |
| 4 | Acclimatization is adjustment to climate, not a forest-type name |
| 5 | Human land use can erase climax vegetation even where climate would allow forest |

## 7.1 Forest Types of India — Classification

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Forest type** | A class of forest defined by climate, structure and characteristic species |
| **Champion and Seth** | Technical classification of Indian forest types used in forestry literature |
| **NCERT school types** | Evergreen, deciduous, thorn, montane, littoral/swamp used in Prelims |

### Overview

Indian forest-type questions almost always give rainfall or a region and ask the type, or give teak/sal/sundari and ask the belt. Memorise the rainfall ladder first, then attach iconic trees and regions.

#### How It Works

- Tropical evergreen needs the wettest climates (generally above about 200 cm).
- Tropical moist deciduous occupies the broad 100–200 cm monsoon belt and covers the largest area among Indian forest types.
- Tropical dry deciduous occupies about 70–100 cm rainfall and grades into scrub.
- Thorn forests occupy arid and semi-arid tracts, generally below about 70 cm.
- Montane forests replace latitude with altitude: temperature falls upward and vegetation belts stack.
- Littoral and swamp forests occupy coasts, deltas and tidal flats; mangroves are the flagship subset.
- Leaf shedding in deciduous forests is a dry-season water-saving strategy, not a winter frost strategy like temperate autumn colours.
- Commercial teak and sal belong mainly to deciduous belts, not evergreen rainforests.
- Rain-shadow interiors of the peninsula often hold dry deciduous or thorn vegetation even when nearby Ghats are wet.
- Plantation monocultures (teak, eucalyptus) are not the same as natural forest type, though papers may still ask region locks.

> **Exam note:** Largest area lock = moist deciduous. Wettest lock = evergreen. Driest lock = thorn.

#### Rainfall → Forest Type Ladder

| Approx. rainfall | Forest type | Iconic trees / note |
|------------------|-------------|---------------------|
| >200 cm | Tropical evergreen | Rosewood, mahogany, ebony; multilayer |
| 100–200 cm | Moist deciduous | Teak, sal; largest area |
| 70–100 cm | Dry deciduous | Open teak/sal; scrub edge |
| <50–70 cm | Thorn / desert scrub | Acacia, babool, ber, khair |
| Altitude stack | Montane | Oak, deodar, chir, alpine flora |
| Tidal coasts | Littoral / mangrove | Sundari, rhizophora |

### Exam Facts (raata — traps only)

|---|------------------|
| 1 | Moist deciduous forests cover the largest area among India’s major natural forest types |
| 2 | Evergreen forests need roughly more than 200 cm rainfall |
| 3 | Thorn forests are xerophytic and linked with Rajasthan–Gujarat and rain-shadow interiors |
| 4 | Teak and sal are deciduous identity trees |
| 5 | Mangroves are tidal/littoral, not montane |

## 7.2 Tropical Evergreen Forests

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Tropical evergreen forest** | Dense multilayer forest of hot, very wet climates that does not shed all leaves in a dry season |
| **Semi-evergreen forest** | High-rainfall forest with a short dry season and mixed evergreen–deciduous canopy |
| **Epiphyte** | Plant that grows on another plant for support, common in humid canopies |

### Overview

Tropical evergreen forests are the wet, dense, multilayer forests of India’s highest-rainfall belts. They do not show a complete dry-season leaf fall.

#### How It Works

- Mean annual rainfall is generally above about 200 cm with a short or absent dry season.
- Temperature stays high through the year in true tropical evergreen tracts.
- Canopy is multilayered; sunlight at the floor is limited.
- Trees are tall hardwoods: rosewood, mahogany, ebony, cinchona in suitable belts.
- Undergrowth is dense; climbers and epiphytes are common.
- Main India regions: Western Ghats windward slopes, north-east India, Andaman and Nicobar Islands.
- Semi-evergreen forests appear where rainfall is high but a short dry season exists.
- These forests are not India’s largest by area — deciduous belts are more extensive.
- Clearance for plantation crops (tea, rubber, spices) and shifting cultivation has fragmented many tracts.
- Do not place teak as the first evergreen identity tree; teak is deciduous.

> **Exam note:** Evergreen = wettest + multilayer + no full dry leaf-shed. Teak is the wrong identity tree here.

#### Evergreen — Species & Region Table

| Region | Typical trees / note |
|--------|----------------------|
| Western Ghats windward | Rosewood, mahogany, ebony; dense multilayer |
| North-East hills | Evergreen / semi-evergreen mix; high rainfall |
| Andaman & Nicobar | Island evergreen rainforest |
| Not identity trees | **Teak and sal** (those are deciduous) |


### Exam Facts (raata — traps only)

|---|------------------|
| 1 | Tropical evergreen forests need roughly more than 200 cm rainfall |
| 2 | Western Ghats, north-east India and Andamans are the classic India evergreen belts |
| 3 | Evergreen forests are dense and multilayered |
| 4 | Teak is not the signature evergreen tree |
| 5 | Epiphytes are common in humid equatorial/evergreen canopies |

## 7.3 Tropical Deciduous Forests (Moist & Dry)

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Tropical deciduous / monsoon forest** | Forest that sheds leaves in the dry season to conserve water |
| **Moist deciduous forest** | Deciduous forest of roughly 100–200 cm rainfall; teak–sal belt; largest India type |
| **Dry deciduous forest** | More open deciduous forest of roughly 70–100 cm rainfall |

### Overview

Tropical deciduous (monsoon) forests shed leaves in the dry season. Moist deciduous (about 100–200 cm) is India’s most extensive natural forest type; dry deciduous (about 70–100 cm) is more open.

#### How It Works

- Leaf fall in the dry season reduces water loss.
- Moist deciduous trees include teak, sal, shisham, bamboo in many tracts.
- Dry deciduous stands are shorter and more open; they grade into thorn scrub.
- Central India, Chota Nagpur, eastern MP, Chhattisgarh, Odisha interiors and Himalayan foothill Terai hold large deciduous belts.
- Sal is important in the northern and eastern moist deciduous belt including UP Terai.
- Teak is iconic in peninsular deciduous forests.
- These forests supplied colonial and modern timber economies — hence frequent map questions.
- After leaf fall, the forest floor receives more light and a grassy understorey may appear.
- Fire and grazing can push moist deciduous toward degraded scrub.
- UP’s remaining natural forests are largely Terai–bhabar moist deciduous and Vindhyan dry/scrub types.

> **Exam note:** Largest area in India = moist deciduous. Teak and sal = deciduous identity pair.

#### Deciduous — Species & Region Table

| Belt | Trees | Region cue |
|------|-------|------------|
| Moist deciduous | Teak, sal, shisham, bamboo | Central India, Chota Nagpur, Terai |
| Dry deciduous | Open teak/sal, tendu, palash | Drier peninsula, Bundelkhand fringe |
| UP Terai | Sal–teak moist deciduous | Dudhwa–Pilibhit belt |
| UP Vindhyan | Dry deciduous / scrub | Sonbhadra–Mirzapur–Chandauli |


### Exam Facts (raata — traps only)

|---|------------------|

| 3 | Dry deciduous forests occupy roughly the 70–100 cm rainfall belt |

## 7.4 Thorn Forests & Scrubs

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Thorn forest / desert scrub** | Open xerophytic woodland of arid and semi-arid climates |
| **Xerophyte** | Plant adapted to drought through form and physiology |
| **Rain-shadow scrub** | Dry vegetation on the leeward side of mountains |

### Overview

Thorn forests are xerophytic woodlands and scrubs of arid and semi-arid India. They occupy the driest end of the NCERT forest ladder.

#### How It Works

- Rainfall is generally below about 70 cm, often much less in true desert fringe.
- Trees are short, with thorns, small leaves, and deep roots.
- Typical species: acacia (babool), ber, khair, date palm in places, cacti-like euphorbias.
- Main regions: Rajasthan, Gujarat, rain-shadow interiors of the Deccan, parts of Haryana–Punjab fringe.
- Vegetation may be discontinuous with bare patches between clumps.
- Xerophytic adaptations include thorns, thick bark, and water-storing tissues.
- Overgrazing converts open thorn forest into degraded scrub and desertification risk.
- Thorn forest is not the same as alpine scrub; altitude and aridity are different controls.
- Mediterranean scrub names (Maquis, Chaparral) are world analogues, not Indian thorn synonyms.
- Canal irrigation in Rajasthan can create farm landscapes where thorn scrub once dominated.

> **Exam note:** Thorn = driest Indian type. Acacia/babool = identity. Do not confuse with Himalayan alpine scrub.

### Exam Facts (raata — traps only)

| # | Fact to remember |
|---|------------------|
| 1 | Thorn forests are xerophytic and linked with arid Rajasthan–Gujarat belts |
| 2 | Acacia, babool, ber and khair are typical thorn-forest trees |
| 3 | Rainfall is generally below about 70 cm in thorn tracts |
| 4 | World scrub matches (Maquis, Fynbos, Chaparral, Matorral) are separate from Indian thorn type |
| 5 | Thorn forest grades from dry deciduous as rainfall falls |

## 7.5 Montane Forests

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Montane forest** | Mountain forest whose composition changes with altitude |
| **Tree line / timberline** | Upper altitude limit of continuous tree growth |
| **Alpine meadow (bugyal)** | Grassy zone above the tree line in the Himalaya |
| **Shola** | Stunted evergreen montane forest patches of south Indian hills |

### Overview

Montane forests change with altitude because temperature and moisture change with height. The Himalaya is the classic Indian example of stacked vegetation belts.

#### How It Works

- Lower Himalayan slopes may hold tropical/subtropical forests where rainfall is adequate.
- Temperate belts include oak, chestnut, chir pine and deodar in western Himalaya.
- Higher belts hold conifers such as spruce, silver fir and then alpine juniper scrub.
- Above the tree line lie alpine meadows (bugyals) and then permanent snow.
- Eastern Himalaya is wetter; species composition differs from drier western Himalaya.
- South Indian hills (Nilgiris, Anaimalai, Palani) hold shola–grassland mosaics at high elevation.
- Assertion–Reason 2025 locks both: different Himalayan vegetation types exist because climate varies with altitude.
- Aspect (south-facing vs north-facing) also changes local moisture and tree lines.
- Montane forests are not classified by the same rainfall ladder as plains evergreen/deciduous types alone.
- Timberline altitude rises toward the equator and falls toward the poles.

> **Exam note:** Himalayan vegetation diversity is an altitude story. Both A and R true, R explains A (2025 Q21).

### Exam Facts (raata — traps only)

| # | Fact to remember |
|---|------------------|
| 1 | Himalayan vegetation types change with altitude because climate changes with altitude |
| 2 | Chir pine and deodar are important western Himalayan temperate trees |
| 3 | Alpine meadows lie above the tree line |
| 4 | Shola forests are high-elevation south-Indian montane patches |
| 5 | Montane belts replace the simple plains rainfall ladder with a temperature elevator |

## 7.6 Tidal / Littoral Forests & Mangroves

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Littoral / tidal forest** | Coastal and delta forests influenced by tides and salinity |
| **Mangrove forest** | Salt-tolerant tidal forest of muddy coasts and deltas |
| **Pneumatophore** | Upward breathing root that allows gas exchange in waterlogged mud |
| **Sundari** | Characteristic mangrove tree of the Sundarbans |

### Overview

Littoral and tidal forests occupy coasts and deltas. Mangroves are salt-tolerant tidal forests with breathing roots, concentrated in the Sundarbans and other Indian deltas and coasts.

#### How It Works

- Mangroves tolerate saline tidal water and waterlogged mud.
- Pneumatophores (breathing roots) allow gas exchange in anaerobic mud.
- Sundari tree gives the Sundarbans their name.
- Major India mangrove regions: Sundarbans (WB–Bangladesh), Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery deltas, Andaman & Nicobar, Gulf of Kachchh, Mumbai creek systems.
- Mangroves protect coasts from storm surge and support fisheries nurseries.
- MISHTI (Mangrove Initiative for Shoreline Habitats and Tangible Incomes) was announced in Union Budget 2023–24 to restore mangroves — not in 2019.
- Clearance for aquaculture and ports is a major threat.
- Mangroves are not montane and not thorn forests.
- India’s mangrove cover is tracked in ISFR chapters separately from inland forest types.
- Littoral strand vegetation (casuarina, pandanus) can occur on sandy coasts without true mangrove mudflats.

> **Exam note:** MISHTI year trap: Budget 2023–24, not 2019. Only restoration aim is correct in 2025 Q93.

#### Mangrove Distribution — India

| Region | Cue |
|--------|-----|
| Sundarbans (WB–Bangladesh) | Largest; sundari; tiger landscape |
| Bhitarkanika (Odisha) | East-coast mangrove; Olive Ridley coast nearby |
| Mahanadi / Godavari / Krishna / Cauvery deltas | East-coast delta mangroves |
| Andaman & Nicobar | Island mangroves |
| Gulf of Kachchh (Gujarat) | West-coast mangrove |
| Mumbai creek / Maharashtra coast | Urbanising mangrove patches |
| Pichavaram / Muthupet (TN) | South-east mangrove awareness |

## 7.8 Plant Acclimatization & Adaptations

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Acclimatization** | Adjustment of an organism to a changed climate or environment |
| **Xerophyte** | Plant adapted to drought |
| **Hydrophyte** | Plant adapted to aquatic or waterlogged habitats |
| **Mesophyte** | Plant adapted to moderate moisture |
| **Halophyte** | Salt-tolerant plant (includes many mangroves) |

### Overview

Acclimatization questions are definitional. Link adaptations to forest types: thorn = xerophytes; mangrove = halophytes with pneumatophores; evergreen = mesic/humid adaptations including epiphytes.

#### How It Works

- Acclimatization is physiological/behavioural adjustment, not speciation.
- Xerophytes show thorns, small leaves, deep roots, water storage.
- Hydrophytes may have aerenchyma, floating leaves, or reduced roots.
- Mangroves combine halophyte salt management with pneumatophores.
- Deciduous leaf fall is a seasonal drought adaptation in monsoon climates.
- Alpine plants grow low and cushion-like against cold and wind.
- Epiphytes draw moisture from air/rain while perched on other plants.
- Crop acclimatization matters when introducing a variety to a new agro-climatic zone.
- Vegetation belts themselves are the landscape expression of successful long-term adaptation.
- Do not confuse acclimatization with Champion–Seth forest-type names.

> **Exam note:** If options mix 'acclimatization' with a forest type, pick the definition — it is a process, not a biome.

### Exam Facts (raata — traps only)

| # | Fact to remember |
|---|------------------|
| 1 | Acclimatization means adjustment to a new climate |
| 2 | Thorn-forest plants are typically xerophytes |
| 3 | Mangroves are halophytes with breathing roots |
| 4 | Epiphytes are common in humid evergreen canopies |
| 5 | Deciduous leaf shed is a dry-season adaptation |

## 7.9 Protected Areas Framework (NP, WLS, BR)

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **National Park** | Protected area with high restriction on human rights and resource use |
| **Wildlife Sanctuary** | Protected for wildlife; some regulated rights may continue |
| **Biosphere Reserve** | Large area with core, buffer and transition zones under UNESCO/MAB idea |
| **In-situ conservation** | Conserving species inside natural habitats |
| **Ex-situ conservation** | Conserving species outside habitats — zoos, botanical gardens, gene banks |
| **Conservation / Community Reserve** | Newer WLPA categories involving community lands |

### Overview

UPPCS loves category traps (in-situ vs ex-situ) and park–state–species matches. Learn the legal-geography differences first, then memorise high-frequency parks.

#### How It Works

- National Parks generally allow no private land rights inside; protection is stricter.
- Wildlife Sanctuaries can allow limited grazing or other regulated rights.
- Biosphere Reserves are larger landscapes: **core** (strict), **buffer**, **transition** (human use).
- A Biosphere Reserve may contain National Parks and Sanctuaries inside it.
- In-situ examples: National Park, Sanctuary, Biosphere Reserve, sacred groves.
- Ex-situ examples: botanical garden, zoo, aquarium, seed bank — **botanical garden is NOT in-situ** (2018 Q54).
- Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 is the parent law for NP/WLS categories.
- Project Tiger (1973) and Project Elephant (1992) are species programmes layered on habitats.
- Biological Diversity Act, 2002 addresses access and benefit sharing and biodiversity institutions.
- Chronology lock: WLPA 1972 → Project Tiger 1973 → Project Elephant 1992 → BDA 2002.

> **Exam note:** Botanical garden = ex-situ. Sacred grove = in-situ. Memorise that pair for 2018-style traps.

#### Protected Area Categories — Comparison

| Category | Rights / use | Typical exam lock |
|----------|--------------|-------------------|
| **National Park** | Strict; private rights generally not allowed | Dudhwa (UP’s only NP) |
| **Wildlife Sanctuary** | Wildlife focus; some regulated rights possible | Chandraprabha; Katarniaghat |
| **Tiger Reserve** | NTCA core+buffer management overlay | Dudhwa / Pilibhit / Amangarh / Ranipur |
| **Biosphere Reserve** | Core + buffer + transition; sustainable use outer | Nokrek; Simlipal; Nilgiri |
| **Conservation Reserve** | Govt land near PAs; consultative | WLPA newer category |
| **Community Reserve** | Community/private land; participatory | WLPA newer category |
| **Sacred grove** | Community cultural protection | In-situ |
| **Botanical garden / zoo** | Ex-situ | 2018 trap |

## 7.13 Ramsar Sites — Biodiversity Geography

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Ramsar Convention** | 1971 treaty on wetlands of international importance (city of Ramsar, Iran) |
| **Ramsar site** | Wetland designated under the Convention |

### Overview

For this topic, treat Ramsar as biodiversity–geography matching, especially UP versus non-UP. Lake typology detail lives mainly in Topic 4; here you need names and states.

#### How It Works

- Ramsar sites are wetlands recognised for international ecological importance.
- UP has a large set of floodplain bird wetlands designated as Ramsar sites — **13 sites** after Surha Tal (2026), second among Indian states after Tamil Nadu in current public framing.
- Sites **in UP**: Sarsai Nawar Jheel (Etawah), Samaspur (Raebareli), Sur Sarovar (Agra), Haiderpur (Muzaffarnagar–Bijnor), Nawabganj (Unnao), Sandi (Hardoi), Saman (Mainpuri), Parvati Arga (Gonda), Bakhira (Sant Kabir Nagar), Upper Ganga stretch, **Surha Tal / Jai Prakash Narayan Bird Sanctuary (Ballia)**, and others as notified.
- **Surha Tal (Ballia)** is an oxbow lake on the Ganga floodplain (Central Asian Flyway). It was designated as a Ramsar site in **January 2026** and announced as **India’s 100th Ramsar site** on **World Environment Day, 5 June 2026**.
- Surha Tal is also a bird sanctuary (declared 1991; renamed after Jai Prakash Narayan in 2002). Ramsar status does not make it a National Park.
- **Ballia trap:** the district is still classed as near-nil **forest land** in UPPCS forest PYQs — that does **not** mean Ballia has no wetland of international importance.
- With Surha Tal, India became Asia’s leader in Ramsar count and third globally (after the UK and Mexico in the 2026 announcement framing).
- Sites **NOT in UP**: Rudrasagar (Tripura), Sultanpur (Haryana), Surinsar–Mansar (J&K).
- January 2020: **six** more Ramsar sites were added to Uttar Pradesh (2020 Q48).
- 2025 Q58: Rudrasagar and Sultanpur are the NOT-in-UP pair; Sarsai Nawar and Samaspur are in UP.
- 2021 Q17: Surinsar–Mansar Lakes are NOT in Uttar Pradesh.
- Chilika, Keoladeo, Loktak, Wular, Sambhar are famous national Ramsar landscapes beyond UP.
- Ramsar status does not automatically equal National Park status.
- For lake landforms (lagoon vs crater), cross-check Topic 4; for Prelims name–state traps, this section is enough.

> **Exam note:** Elimination order for NOT-in-UP: strike Sarsai Nawar and Samaspur first — both are UP. Add Surha Tal (Ballia) as the 100th-India / 13th-UP current-affairs lock. Do not confuse Surha Tal with Sur Sarovar (Agra).

## 7.14 Coral Reefs

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Coral reef** | Marine structure built by coral polyps and calcareous algae in clear warm seas |
| **Coral bleaching** | Stress response where corals expel symbiotic zooxanthellae and turn white |

### Overview

India’s reef questions ask location belts or bleaching causes. Reefs need warm, clear, shallow seas — not muddy delta mouths.

#### How It Works

- Major Indian coral areas: **Andaman and Nicobar**, **Lakshadweep**, **Gulf of Mannar**, **Gulf of Kachchh**.
- Corals live in symbiotic partnership with zooxanthellae algae.
- Warming beyond tolerance causes bleaching and can kill corals if prolonged.
- 2024 Q19: catastrophic bleaching/deaths in Andaman and Nicobar linked with about **2°C rise** in Indian Ocean temperature (not cooling).
- Sediment load, pollution and destructive fishing also damage reefs.
- Gulf of Mannar is a biosphere reserve with reef ecosystems.
- Coral islands of Lakshadweep are atolls/reef-derived landforms.
- Reefs are marine biodiversity hotspots, not mangrove mudflat systems.
- Cold upwelling or sudden cooling is not the usual Indian bleaching story in these PYQs.
- Protecting reefs supports fisheries and coastal tourism economies.

> **Exam note:** Bleaching = warming stress. If an option says temperature decrease, reject it for the Andaman mass-death PYQ.

#### Coral Regions of India — Comparison

| Region | Sea | Note |
|--------|-----|------|
| Andaman & Nicobar | Bay of Bengal / Andaman Sea | Mass bleaching PYQ (warming) |
| Lakshadweep | Arabian Sea | Atoll / coral islands |
| Gulf of Mannar | Between India–Sri Lanka | Also a Biosphere Reserve |
| Gulf of Kachchh | Arabian Sea (Gujarat) | West-coast reefs |

## 7.17 Biosphere Reserve Programme — Deep Dive

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **UNESCO MAB** | Man and the Biosphere Programme that frames biosphere reserves worldwide |
| **World Network of Biosphere Reserves** | UNESCO list of designated biosphere reserves |
| **Core zone** | Strictly protected inner zone; conservation first |
| **Buffer zone** | Surrounds core; research, education, limited use |
| **Transition zone** | Outer zone with settlements and sustainable economic activity |

### Overview

The Biosphere Reserve Programme is not only a name–state match list. UPPCS also expects the **three-zone model** and the idea that a BR can contain National Parks and Sanctuaries while allowing sustainable use in the transition zone.

#### How It Works

- Biosphere reserves join **conservation + research + sustainable livelihoods**.
- **Core** follows strict protection logic; extraction is minimal or nil.
- **Buffer** allows managed activities that do not hurt the core.
- **Transition** is where villages, farming and eco-development sit.
- India designates biosphere reserves nationally; some also enter the UNESCO World Network.
- A single BR may span more than one state (Nilgiri; Agasthyamalai; Achanakmar-Amarkantak).
- Biosphere status is different from Ramsar (wetlands) and from Project Tiger (species programme).
- Wrong-state pairs are the highest-yield trap: Nokrek≠Manipur; Simlipal≠MP; Nanda Devi≠UP.
- Forest Research Institute / ICFRE detail belongs mainly to Topic 10 — only FRI Dehradun awareness here if asked.

> **Exam note:** If a question asks the structure of a biosphere reserve, answer **core–buffer–transition**, not “same as national park”.

## 7.18 Forest Policy, Movements & Conservation Reserves (Exam Awareness)

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **National Forest Policy** | Policy frame for forest conservation and tree cover goals (major modern revision 1988) |
| **Conservation Reserve** | WLPA category on government land adjacent to PAs, with stakeholder consultation |
| **Community Reserve** | WLPA category on community/private land for wildlife, with community participation |
| **Sacred grove** | Community-protected forest patch (in-situ cultural conservation) |
| **Van Mahotsav** | Tree-planting festival tradition in India |

### Overview

These items appear as definition or chronology neighbours to vegetation/biodiversity geography. Keep them short but exact.

#### How It Works

- National Forest Policy **1988** emphasises environmental stability and a notional **33%** forest/tree cover goal for the country.

- Sacred groves are in-situ, not botanical gardens.
- Chipko (Uttarakhand hills) and Appiko (Western Ghats Karnataka) are forest-protection movements sometimes paired with vegetation questions.
- Social forestry and farm forestry raise tree cover outside dense natural forest — important for ISFR tree-cover reading.
- Jhum (shifting cultivation) clears forest patches in NE hills; it explains forest degradation patterns here and is taught fully under Agriculture.
- FRI Dehradun / ICFRE are forest institutions (full detail in Topic 10).

> **Exam note:** 33% is a **policy goal**, not India’s present ISFR forest-cover percentage.

### Legal / Administrative Forest Classes (awareness)

| Class | Meaning | Exam use |
|-------|---------|----------|
| **Reserved Forest** | Highest legal protection under Indian Forest Act; rights generally restricted | Protection intensity |
| **Protected Forest** | Notified forest with more limited restrictions than reserved | Mid-tier legal forest |
| **Unclassed Forest** | Forest land not reserved/protected; often community or revenue forest | Completes RFA total |
| **Recorded Forest Area (RFA)** | Sum of reserved + protected + unclassed notified forest | Differs from satellite **forest cover** |
| **Very Dense / Moderately Dense / Open Forest** | ISFR canopy-density classes | Density matching in advanced stems |

> **Exam note:** A district can have high recorded forest area but lower forest cover if canopy is degraded — do not treat the two phrases as synonyms.

### Plantation vs Natural Forest (boundary with Agriculture)

| Feature | Natural forest | Plantation |
|---------|----------------|------------|
| Origin | Spontaneous / climax or secondary | Planted estate |
| Species | Mixed | Often monoculture (teak, eucalyptus, tea, rubber) |
| Exam home | This topic | Tea/coffee/rubber also in Agriculture |
| Trap | Calling tea estate a tropical evergreen climax | Estate ≠ climax evergreen |
