## 5.0 Soil as a Resource — Framework

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Soil resource** | The usable stock of soils that support agriculture, forests, and ecosystems |
| **Edaphic** | Relating to soil as an environmental controlling factor |
| **Land degradation** | Decline in soil quality/productivity due to erosion, salinity, or misuse |

### Soil Framework — Overview

Soil is a living, layered natural body formed slowly and destroyed quickly by misuse. UPPCS tests soils as **type–region–crop triples**, **problem-soil remedies**, and **erosion–conservation methods**. Treat soil as a resource first, then lock each ICAR type.

#### Soil Resource — How It Works

- Soil is the thin surface layer of mineral particles, organic matter, water, and air that supports plant life.
- It is renewable only on a geological timescale; human erosion can strip centuries of formation in decades.
- **Edaphic factors** are soil-related controls on vegetation and crops, distinct from pure climate or biotic factors.
- UPPCS 2018 Q51 asks which term is concerned with soil; the answer is **edaphic**.
- Soil distribution in India mirrors parent rock, climate belts, and relief — Deccan Trap gives black soils, crystalline shields give red soils, and Indo-Gangetic plains give alluvium.
- Fertility depends on nutrients, structure, moisture, organic matter, and organisms, not colour alone.
- Problem soils (usar, acidic laterite uplands, eroded ravines) reduce productive area even inside fertile regions.
- Conservation is therefore as exam-important as classification; methods questions appear with movements and observance days.

> **Exam note:** **Edaphic = soil.** Climatic, biotic, and topography are other environmental factors, not synonyms for soil.

### Exam Facts (raata — traps only) — §5.0

| # | Fact to remember |
|---|------------------|
| 1 | Edaphic means related to soil |
| 2 | Soil forms slowly and can be destroyed quickly by misuse |
| 3 | Indian soils reflect parent rock, climate and relief |
| 4 | Both soil classification and soil conservation are high-scoring areas |

## 5.1 Soil Formation

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Weathering** | Breakdown of rocks in situ by physical, chemical, and biological agents |
| **Pedogenesis** | Development of soil horizons from weathered parent material |
| **Parent material** | Underlying rock or deposited sediment that supplies mineral matter |

### Soil Formation — Overview

Soil formation is controlled by five classical factors remembered as **CLORPT**. Exam questions rarely ask the acronym alone; they ask which factor dominates a named soil (basalt → black soil; heavy rain → laterite leaching).

#### Soil Formation — How It Works

- **Climate** controls weathering rate, leaching intensity, and organic decay; hot wet climates leach bases and create laterite.
- **Organisms (biotic factor)** add organic matter, mix horizons, and recycle nutrients through roots, microbes, and fauna.
- **Relief (topography)** decides drainage and erosion: steep slopes keep soils thin; valleys accumulate deeper soils.
- **Parent material** supplies mineral chemistry: basalt favours black soils; granite–gneiss favours red soils; alluvium inherits river sediments.
- **Time** allows horizon differentiation; young soils on fresh alluvium differ from mature residual soils on old plateaus.
- **Physical weathering** breaks rock by temperature change, frost, and abrasion without changing chemical composition.
- **Chemical weathering** alters minerals through hydration, oxidation, carbonation, and solution — intense under hot wet monsoon.
- **Biological weathering** uses roots, organic acids, and burrowing organisms to open and alter rock.
- Transport and deposition create **transported soils** such as alluvium; **residual soils** form on weathered bedrock in place.
- Human activity (tillage, irrigation, deforestation, mining) accelerates change and can degrade soils faster than nature rebuilds them.
- In India, monsoon seasonality drives strong wet-season leaching and dry-season capillary rise in many plains soils.
- The same climate on different parent rock yields different soils — basalt → black; granite → red; limestone settings differ again.

> **Exam note:** Same climate on different parent rock gives different soils — basalt → black; granite → red.

#### CLORPT — Factor Table

| Factor | Role | India example |
|--------|------|---------------|
| Climate | Weathering + leaching intensity | Laterite in hot wet Ghats/plateau edges |
| Organisms | Humus, nutrient cycling | Forest soils under Himalayan cover |
| Relief | Depth, drainage, erosion | Thin soils on steep Himalaya slopes |
| Parent material | Mineral base | Deccan Trap → regur |
| Time | Maturity of profile | Mature black soils vs young khadar |

### Exam Facts (raata — traps only) — §5.1

| # | Fact to remember |
|---|------------------|
| 1 | Five formation factors: climate, organisms, relief, parent material, time |
| 2 | Parent rock strongly controls soil colour and chemistry |
| 3 | High temperature plus heavy rain causes strong leaching |
| 4 | Steep slopes keep soils thin |
| 5 | Alluvial soil is a transported soil |

## 5.2 Soil Profile

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Soil profile** | Vertical sequence of soil horizons from surface to bedrock |
| **Horizon O** | Organic litter and decomposed organic matter at the top |
| **Horizon A** | Topsoil mixed with minerals and humus |
| **Horizon B** | Subsoil enriched by illuviation |
| **Horizon C** | Weathered parent material |
| **Horizon R** | Unweathered bedrock |

### Soil Profile — Overview

A soil profile is the exam diagram of pedogenesis. Know the order top to bottom and the difference between eluviation (removal) and illuviation (deposition).

#### Soil Profile — How It Works

- A complete residual profile typically shows **O–A–(E)–B–C–R** from surface to rock.
- **O horizon** is dominated by organic litter and humus, especially under forests.
- **A horizon** is the main topsoil of agriculture — minerals mixed with organic matter.
- **E horizon**, when present, is a leached zone of eluviation, often lighter in colour.
- **B horizon** receives clay, iron, aluminium, or lime washed from above (illuviation).
- **C horizon** is partly weathered parent material that has not yet become true soil.
- **R horizon** is hard bedrock.
- Young alluvial soils may show weak horizon differentiation compared with mature residual soils.
- Ploughing mixes O/A layers and can destroy natural structure if mismanaged.
- Depth of profile affects rooting, moisture storage, and suitability for deep-rooted crops.

> **Exam note:** Eluviation = washing **out** of upper layers; illuviation = washing **into** lower layers.

#### Horizons — Quick Table

| Horizon | Content | Exam tag |
|---------|---------|----------|
| O | Organic | Forest floors |
| A | Topsoil + humus | Crop root zone |
| E | Leached | Eluviation |
| B | Accumulation | Illuviation / subsoil |
| C | Weathered parent | Transition |
| R | Bedrock | Base |

### Exam Facts (raata — traps only) — §5.2

| # | Fact |
|---|------|
| 1 | Order O–A–B–C–R (E optional) |
| 2 | A = topsoil; B = subsoil accumulation |
| 3 | Eluviation vs illuviation is a favourite definition trap |
| 4 | Immature soils have weak horizons |

## 5.3 Soil Classification

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Soil classification** | Grouping soils by genesis, colour, texture, chemistry, and distribution |
| **ICAR classification** | Practical Indian grouping used in school/competitive geography |
| **Residual soil** | Formed in place from underlying rock |
| **Transported soil** | Formed from material brought by rivers, wind, or gravity |

### Classification — Overview

UPPCS uses the familiar school classification (alluvial, black, red, laterite, arid, saline, peaty, forest/mountain). Learn the **master table** first, then drill each type in §5.4–5.11.

#### Classification — How It Works

- Indian competitive exams follow ICAR/school categories more than full USDA Soil Taxonomy names.
- **Alluvial soils** dominate northern plains and many coasts and are the most extensive fertile group.
- **Black soils** are residual on Deccan Trap basalt and famous for cotton.
- **Red and yellow soils** develop on crystalline rocks; yellow tones appear where hydration of iron oxides is higher.
- **Laterite soils** form under intense leaching in hot wet climates and are rich in Fe/Al oxides.
- **Arid/desert soils** occupy western Rajasthan and similar dry tracts with low humus.
- **Saline and alkaline soils** are problem soils of arid tracts and poorly drained canal commands.
- **Peaty/marshy soils** occur in humid waterlogged pockets such as Kerala coast, Sundarbans fringes, and depressions.
- **Forest/mountain soils** vary with altitude and slope in the Himalaya and other hills.
- World analogy: Brazil's **Terra Roxa** is a fertile basaltic soil linked to coffee — useful for 2025 Q63 style questions.

> **Exam note:** Colour names are labels, not fertility guarantees — laterite is often nutrient-poor for field crops without management despite Fe/Al richness.

#### Master Classification Table

| Soil type | Parent / setting | Region (core) | Crop link | Trap |
|-----------|------------------|---------------|-----------|------|
| Alluvial | River alluvium | Indo-Gangetic, Brahmaputra, coasts | Wheat, rice, sugarcane | Khadar ≠ Bangar |
| Black (Regur) | Basalt (Deccan Trap) | MH–MP–GJ–KA–TG | Cotton | Not Himalayan |
| Red | Crystalline rocks | TN, KA, south MH, Odisha, Chota Nagpur | Millets, pulses | Not always infertile |
| Laterite | Hot wet leaching | Ghats, NE, plateau edges | Cashew, tea, coffee | Rich in Fe/Al |
| Desert/Arid | Arid climate | W Rajasthan | Millets if irrigated | Low humus |
| Mountain/Forest | Hills/Himalaya | Himalaya, NE hills | Orchards, plantations | Thin on steep slopes |
| Saline/Alkaline | Poor drainage / salts | Canal plains, arid | Needs reclamation | Usar/reh |
| Peaty/Marshy | Waterlogged organics | Kerala, depressions | Specialised | High organic, often acidic |

#### World Soil Link — Terra Roxa

- Brazil's equatorial rainforests are called **Selva**.
- **Terra Roxa** is a fertile soil associated with coffee regions on weathered basalt; the name means reddish/purple earth.
- UPPCS 2025 Q63 pairs Selva and Terra Roxa; majority keys treat **both** statements as correct.
- Use Terra Roxa as a world parallel to fertile basaltic soils, while remembering Indian black soils are the domestic Deccan analogue for cotton.

## 5.3.1 Peaty / Marshy Soil

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Peaty / marshy soil** | Soil with heavy accumulation of organic matter under waterlogged, humid conditions |
| **Peat** | Partially decomposed plant matter accumulated in anaerobic wetlands |

### Peaty / Marshy Soil — Overview

Listed in the ICAR school set but often skipped in revision. Peaty soils are **not** Deccan black regur — they are organic-rich wetland soils of humid pockets.

#### Peaty / Marshy Soil — How It Works

- Form where waterlogging slows decay, so plant residues pile up as dark organic matter.
- Common settings: humid Kerala coastal tracts, Sundarbans fringes, deltaic backwaters, and inland marshy depressions.
- Texture is heavy and poorly drained; soils are often **acidic** and need drainage before normal cropping.
- Organic content is high, but usable fertility for field crops is limited until drainage and management improve aeration.
- Colour can be blackish — do not confuse with **regur**; peaty black is organic/waterlogged, regur is basaltic clay of the Deccan.
- Crops need specialised management; with drainage, paddy and specialised horticulture may occupy reclaimed tracts.
- Lock: **humid + waterlogged + organic heavy + often acidic + Kerala/marsh coasts ≠ cotton regur**.

> **Exam note:** Black colour on a Kerala marsh stem → peaty/marshy, not Deccan cotton soil.

#### Peaty Snapshot

| Item | Fact |
|------|------|
| Setting | Humid waterlogged coasts/depressions |
| Chemistry | High OM; often acidic |
| Exam trap | Confuse with black regur |
| Need | Drainage before normal crops |

## 5.4 Alluvial Soil

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Alluvial soil** | Depositional soil transported and laid down by rivers and streams |
| **Khadar (newer alluvium)** | Floodplain alluvium renewed by floods; finer and usually more fertile |
| **Bhangar / Bangar (older alluvium)** | Older alluvium on higher ground; coarser; often with kankar |
| **Bhur** | Sandy alluvial mounds/ridges along river courses in the western Gangetic plain |
| **Kankar** | Calcareous nodules common in older alluvium |

### Alluvial Soil — Overview

Alluvial soils are India's **most extensive** soils — about **40%** of the country's area in NCERT framing — and the backbone of the wheat–rice–sugarcane economy. UPPCS tests **khadar vs bhangar**, fertility, and UP's alluvial identity. Never confuse the landform phrase "alluvial plain" with the soil-type question.

#### Alluvial Soil — How It Works

- Alluvial soils are **transported depositional soils** brought by Himalayan and peninsular rivers into plains, valleys, and deltas.
- They cover about **two-fifths** of India: Indo-Gangetic–Brahmaputra plains, peninsular river valleys, and east-coast deltas.
- Texture varies from sandy loam to clay loam with distance from the channel and flood energy.
- **Khadar** is new alluvium of the active floodplain; it is finer, less kankar-rich, and generally more fertile because floods renew fine silt.
- **Bhangar (Bangar)** is older alluvium above frequent flood level; it is coarser and often contains calcareous **kankar** nodules.
- **Bhur** soils are sandy ridges along western Gangetic river courses — a local alluvial variant students should recognise.
- Chemically, alluvial soils are generally **rich in potash, phosphoric acid, and lime**, but can be locally deficient in nitrogen and organic matter.
- They support intensive agriculture: wheat, rice, sugarcane, pulses, oilseeds, maize, and jute in fitting belts.
- **Deltaic alluvium** on the east coast is finer and often more clayey; **coastal alluvium** may carry salinity risk near tidal zones.
- In Uttar Pradesh, almost the entire plain is alluvial; canal irrigation raised yields but also created local **usar/reh** patches where drainage failed.
- Colour ranges from light grey to ash grey; fertility is high when structure and drainage are maintained.
- Do not mark UP as a black-soil state — UP is an **alluvial** state.

> **Exam note:** **Khadar = new/fine/fertile.** **Bhangar = old/coarser/kankar.** Spelling may appear as Bangar or Bhangar.

#### Khadar vs Bhangar — Master Comparison

| Feature | Khadar | Bhangar / Bangar |
|---------|--------|------------------|
| Age | Newer | Older |
| Location | Active floodplain | Higher terraces / older plain |
| Flooding | Frequent renewal | Rare / none |
| Texture | Finer silt-clay | Coarser |
| Kankar | Rare | Common |
| Fertility | Generally higher | Slightly lower |
| Local sandy form | — | **Bhur** ridges in west Gangetic belt |

#### Chemical Snapshot — Alluvial

| Rich / adequate | Often deficient / variable |
|-----------------|----------------------------|
| Potash, phosphoric acid, lime | Nitrogen, humus (locally) |

#### Distribution & Crops — Alluvial

| Belt | States / region | Star crops |
|------|-----------------|------------|

## 5.5 Black Soil (Regur)

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Black soil / Regur / Black cotton soil** | Dark clayey residual soil of Deccan Trap basalt |
| **Self-ploughing** | Shrink–swell cracking in dry season that works the soil naturally |
| **Montmorillonite clay** | Expanding clay mineral family explaining shrink–swell behaviour of black soils |

### Black Soil — Overview

Black soil is the Deccan cotton soil. UPPCS 2018 nails the Himalaya trap. Lock chemistry, moisture retention, and state belt.

#### Black Soil — How It Works

- Black soils develop mainly from weathering of **Deccan Trap basalt** and are residual soils of the lava plateau.
- Local names: **regur** and **black cotton soil**.
- They are generally **clayey, deep, and impermeable** when wet; sticky when wet and cracked when dry.
- Dry-season **wide cracks** produce a natural "self-ploughing" effect and help aeration at depth.
- Expanding clay behaviour is linked to **montmorillonitic** clay minerals — useful for deeper science awareness.
- They are **highly moisture-retentive**, so rainfed cotton can survive dry spells after monsoon soaking.
- Classical notes (including Krabs) treat regur as essentially a **mature** soil.
- Chemically they are rich in **iron, lime, calcium, magnesium, alumina, and potash**; they are generally deficient in **nitrogen, phosphorus, and organic matter**.
- Colour is dark from titaniferous magnetite and related compounds, not only from high humus.
- Core distribution: Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh/Telangana, northern Karnataka, parts of Tamil Nadu; very deep in upper Godavari–Krishna and NW Deccan.
- They are **not** characteristic of the Himalayan region (2018 Q31 false statement).
- Ideal crop is **cotton**; also jowar, wheat, linseed, castor, tobacco, and citrus in suitable tracts.
- In UP, true Deccan-style black soils appear mainly as **Mar/Kabar** in Bundelkhand — not across the Gangetic plain.

> **Exam note:** Black soil belongs to the Deccan Trap / regur belt. It is moisture-retentive, used for cotton, and treated as a mature soil — it is not a Himalayan soil.

## 5.6 Red and Yellow Soil

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Red soil** | Soil coloured by diffusion of iron oxides, usually on crystalline rocks |
| **Yellow soil** | Hydrated iron-oxide variant of the same family, yellowish in tint |

### Red and Yellow Soil — Overview

NCERT groups **red and yellow** together. They cover large crystalline tracts of the peninsula outside the black-soil core.

#### Red and Yellow Soil — How It Works

- These soils develop on ancient **crystalline igneous and metamorphic rocks** (granite, gneiss, and related rocks).
- Red colour comes from wide diffusion of **iron oxides** through the profile.
- Where iron oxides are more **hydrated**, the tint turns yellowish — hence red and yellow as one family.
- Texture is generally sandy to loamy and more **porous** than black clay soils; they drain faster.
- On uplands they are often thin, coarse, and poorer; in lowlands and valleys they become finer and more fertile.
- Chemically they are often deficient in **nitrogen, phosphorus, and humus**; potash may be variable; lime can be lacking on strongly leached sites.
- Distribution: Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, southern Maharashtra, Odisha, Chota Nagpur, parts of Madhya Pradesh, and parts of the Northeast.
- Crops: millets, pulses, tobacco, oilseeds, potatoes, fruits; cotton with irrigation and fertiliser support.
- They are not "barren by definition" — management and organic matter change outcomes.
- Do not call every red soil **laterite**; laterite needs intense hot-wet leaching and Fe/Al oxide dominance after base removal.
- Phosphorus fixation and low organic matter are common management headaches.
- Lock: **crystalline rock + Fe colour + porous + often low N/humus**.

> **Exam note:** Red ≠ laterite automatically. Check climate intensity and leaching.

## 5.7 Laterite Soil

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Laterite** | Soil from intense tropical leaching; rich in iron and aluminium oxides |
| **Laterisation** | Removal of silica and bases, leaving Fe/Al sesquioxides |
| **Later (Latin)** | Brick — laterite hardens on exposure and was used as building brick |

### Laterite Soil — Overview

Laterite is a UPPCS favourite because of the Fe/Al trap (2023 Q51). Leaching removes bases, but **concentrates** iron and aluminium oxides.

#### Laterite Soil — How It Works

- Laterite forms under **high temperature and heavy rainfall** with strong seasonal wetting.
- Intense leaching washes away **lime and silica**; soils rich in **iron oxide and aluminium compounds** remain.
- Statement "poor in iron oxide and aluminium" is **false** (2023 Q51).
- Humus is removed by rapid bacterial activity under tropical heat; organic matter is typically low.
- Chemically: **poor in organic matter, nitrogen, phosphate, and calcium**; **iron oxide and potash** are in excess relative to bases.
- Soft when wet and hardens on exposure — historically cut and used as **bricks**.
- Distribution: Western Ghats summits and edges, Eastern Ghats patches, NE hills, Odisha, West Bengal, Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra uplands, and similar hot-wet plateaus.
- Not suitable for ordinary field crops without manure and fertiliser; **cashew, tea, coffee, rubber, cinchona** thrive on suitable lateritic uplands.
- Red colour causes confusion with ordinary red soils — always check leaching intensity and climate.
- Forest clearing on laterite accelerates erosion because the profile is already base-poor.
- Lock: **hot + wet + Fe/Al rich + base-poor + brick**.

> **Exam note:** Laterite develops in hot wet climates and is **rich** in iron and aluminium oxides. The wrong trap is saying it is poor in Fe/Al.

#### Chemical Snapshot — Laterite

| Excess / rich | Poor / deficient |
|---------------|------------------|
| Iron oxide, aluminium compounds; potash relatively high vs bases | Organic matter, N, phosphate, calcium, lime, silica (leached) |

#### Laterite — Specs & Crops

| Item | Fact |
|------|------|
| Climate | High T + heavy rain |
| Process | Intense leaching |
| Building use | Bricks on hardening |
| Crops | Cashew, tea, coffee, rubber, cinchona |
| Field crops | Need heavy manuring |

### Exam Facts (raata — traps only) — §5.7

| # | Fact to remember |
|---|------------------|

## 5.8 Desert Soil (Arid Soil)

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Arid / desert soil** | Sandy, often saline soil of dry climates with low humus |
| **Kankar layer (arid)** | Calcium-rich nodule layer in lower horizons of arid soils |

### Arid Soil — Overview

NCERT arid soils are red to brown, sandy, and saline, with low moisture and humus. Western Rajasthan is the heartland.

#### Arid Soil — How It Works

- Arid soils form where rainfall is too low for dense vegetation and deep leaching of a humid type.
- Colour ranges from **red to brown**; texture is generally **sandy**.
- They are often **saline**; in some places salt content is so high that common salt is obtained by evaporating saline water.
- High temperature and evaporation leave them short of **moisture and humus**.
- **Nitrogen is insufficient**; **phosphate content is often normal**.
- Lower horizons may show **kankar layers** because calcium content increases downward.
- Core region: western Rajasthan (Thar) and arid fringes of Gujarat and Haryana.
- With irrigation (Indira Gandhi Canal and wells), millets, wheat, mustard, and fodder expand — if drainage prevents new salinity.
- Wind erosion and sand-dune encroachment are the dominant physical threats.
- Shelter belts and agro-forestry are NCERT-style answers for dune control in western Rajasthan.
- Lock: **sandy + saline tendency + low humus/N + Rajasthan + kankar below**.

> **Exam note:** Arid soil is not permanently useless; irrigation transforms it but salinity control is mandatory.

### Forest / Mountain Soil — Overview

NCERT forest soils change with altitude. Steep slopes stay thin; valleys hold loams. They are never the Deccan black-soil belt.

#### Forest / Mountain Soil — How It Works

- Forest soils form where sufficient rainfall supports forest vegetation in hills and mountains.
- Character changes with **altitude**: thin and immature on steep cold slopes; deeper and more fertile in valleys.
- In snow-bound areas of the Himalaya, soils may be acidic with low humus accumulation under harsh climate.

- Landslides reset young soils on steep faces after heavy rain.

## 5.10 Saline Soil (Usara / Reh)

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Saline / Usara soil** | Soil with excess soluble salts of Na, K, Mg; infertile for normal crops |
| **Reh** | White salt efflorescence on the surface |
| **Kallar** | Another regional name often used for alkali/saline infertile patches |

### Saline Soil — Overview

NCERT calls these **Usara** soils. They contain large proportions of sodium, potassium, and magnesium salts and do not support normal vegetative growth.

#### Saline Soil — How It Works

- Saline soils contain excess soluble salts — largely **sodium, potassium, and magnesium** compounds.
- They are infertile for ordinary crops and are known as **Usara** soils in NCERT language.
- Dry climate and **poor drainage** concentrate salts; swampy and waterlogged tracts also turn saline.
- Capillary rise in irrigated plains brings salts up; evaporation leaves **reh** white crusts.
- Structure ranges from sandy to loamy; they typically lack **nitrogen and calcium**.
- Occur in arid/semi-arid regions, waterlogged canal commands of the north, and parts of the **eastern coast** including deltaic tracts such as the Sundarbans fringe.
- Green Revolution intensive irrigation without drainage has expanded saline patches in Punjab, Haryana, and western UP.
- Reclamation needs **leaching of salts** plus **drainage** so washed salts leave the root zone.
- Farmers are advised to add gypsum in related sodic conditions and to avoid excessive irrigation without outlets.
- Lock: **Usara = salt excess + reh + poor drainage + leach & drain**.

> **Exam note:** More canal water without drainage **creates** usar; it does not cure it.

## 5.11 Alkaline Soil (Sodic / Kallar)

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Alkaline / sodic soil** | Soil with excess exchangeable sodium, high pH, dispersed structure |
| **Kallar / Usar** | Local names for infertile alkali–saline patches in northern plains |

### Alkaline Soil — Overview

Alkaline soils overlap with saline Usara tracts but the key chemistry is **exchangeable sodium** and high pH. Gypsum is the classic amendment.

#### Alkaline Soil — How It Works

- Alkaline (sodic) soils have excess exchangeable **sodium** and typically **high pH** (often >8.5).
- Sodium disperses clay, destroying crumb structure and blocking infiltration.
- They frequently coexist with saline soils in the same usar/kallar belts of UP, Punjab, and Haryana.
- Plants face nutrient imbalance, poor aeration, and toxicity.
- Standard chemical remedy is **gypsum (CaSO₄)** so calcium replaces sodium on the exchange complex.
- After gypsum, leaching and drainage remove displaced sodium salts.
- Green manures, salt-tolerant species, and bunding support reclamation.
- Distinguish: saline = soluble salts stress; alkaline = Na + high pH + structure collapse.
- Lime is for **acid** soils — never swap lime and gypsum in remedies.
- Lock: **Alkaline = Na + high pH + gypsum**.

> **Exam note:** Gypsum for alkali/sodic; lime for acid — the most tested remedy trap.

#### Saline vs Alkaline — Comparison

| Feature | Saline | Alkaline (sodic) |
|---------|--------|------------------|
| Main problem | Soluble salts | Exchangeable Na |
| pH | Near neutral to moderately high | Typically high |
| Structure | Variable | Dispersed, poor |
| Surface clue | White reh | Often darkish alkali stains |
| Key remedy | Leach + drain | Gypsum + leach |

## 5.12 Problem Soils (Acidic, Saline, Alkaline)

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Problem soil** | Soil whose chemistry or structure blocks normal cropping without reclamation |
| **Acid soil** | Low-pH soil from base leaching in high-rainfall regions |

### Problem Soils — Overview

Syllabus groups acidic, saline, and alkaline soils. Master **cause → effect → remedy** for each. UP usar is the state-paper bridge.

#### Problem Soils — How It Works

- **Acid soils** form where heavy rain leaches Ca and Mg; Fe/Al activity rises at low pH.
- Acid soils are common in lateritic belts, NE India, and high-rainfall eastern Himalayan foothills.
- Remedy for acid soils is **liming** (lime/limestone) to raise pH.
- **Saline soils** harm plants by osmotic salt stress; remedy is **leaching + drainage**.
- **Alkaline/sodic soils** harm plants by Na and high pH; remedy is **gypsum then leaching**.
- Usar lands of UP often mix saline and alkaline characters — integrated reclamation is needed.
- Waterlogging from canals is the shared trigger for salt problems in the northern plains.
- Afforestation, salt-tolerant crops, bunding, and drainage support chemical fixes.
- Mitti Bachao (Hoshangabad, MP) rose from waterlogging and soil-degradation fears in a dam command.
- Never swap remedies: gypsum ≠ lime.
- Lock: **Acid→lime; Alkaline→gypsum; Saline→leach+drain**.

> **Exam note:** Remedy swap is the #1 problem-soil trap.

## 5.13 Soil Texture and Structure

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Soil texture** | Relative % of sand, silt, and clay particles |
| **Soil structure** | Arrangement of particles into crumbs, blocks, or clods |
| **Loam** | Balanced sand–silt–clay mix favoured for farming |
| **pH** | Measure of acidity/alkalinity controlling nutrient availability |

### Soil Texture — Overview

Texture controls water, air, and tillage. Structure controls how those pores are organised. Both matter for fertility.

#### Soil Texture & Structure — How It Works

- **Sand** is coarsest: drains fast, warms quickly, holds little water or nutrients.
- **Silt** is medium: smooth feel, better water holding than sand.
- **Clay** is finest: holds water and nutrients but drains slowly and can waterlog or crack.
- **Loam** balances the three and suits most crops.
- Texture ≠ structure: a clay soil can be well-structured crumbs or a dispersed sodic mass.
- Black regur is clay-dominated; desert soils are sand-dominated; alluvium spans sandy to clayey loams.
- Fine textures usually have higher cation exchange capacity.
- Coarse textures need more frequent irrigation and organic matter.
- **pH scale** awareness: acid <7, neutral ~7, alkaline >7; nutrient availability peaks near slightly acid to neutral for many crops.
- Field feel tests (ribbon test) estimate clay content roughly.
- Lock: **Sand drains; clay holds; loam balances; structure organises pores**.

> **Exam note:** Clayey ≠ always fertile. Black clay can be productive; sodic clay can be sterile.

#### Texture Comparison

| Texture | Water | Drainage | Tillage | Example |
|---------|-------|----------|---------|---------|
| Sandy | Low | Fast | Easy | Desert soils |
| Silty | Medium-high | Medium | Moderate | Some alluvium |
| Clayey | High | Slow | Hard when wet | Black regur |
| Loam | Balanced | Balanced | Favourable | Good farm soils |

## 5.14 Soil Fertility

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Soil fertility** | Ability to supply nutrients and physical conditions for plant growth |
| **Humus** | Stable dark organic matter improving structure and nutrient holding |
| **NPK** | Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium — primary macronutrients |
| **Soil Health Card** | Scheme giving farmers soil nutrient status and fertiliser advice (awareness) |

### Soil Fertility — Overview

Fertility is chemical + physical + biological. Colour alone never proves fertility.

#### Soil Fertility — How It Works

- Plants need **N, P, K** plus secondary and micronutrients from the soil solution.
- **Humus** improves crumb structure, water holding, and cation exchange.
- Microbes and earthworms recycle nutrients and build porosity; earthworms are called friends of farmers (2025 fertility overlap).
- Nitrogen is often limiting in Indian soils; legumes and fertilisers address gaps.
- Phosphorus availability falls in strongly acid or alkaline soils.
- Physical fertility needs aeration, drainage, and rootable depth.
- Continuous cropping without organic returns mines nutrients and collapses structure.
- Limits by type: laterite lacks bases; desert lacks humus; usar has salts; red uplands lack N/humus.
- **Soil Health Card** awareness links fertility testing to balanced fertiliser use (scheme detail can sit with Agriculture Topic 6).
- Edaphic fertility constraints are soil-factor limits on vegetation (2018 Q51 edaphic).
- Lock: **Fertility = nutrients + OM + structure + biology + moisture + suitable pH**.

> **Exam note:** Black colour ≠ automatic high fertility if salts or sodicity dominate.

| Organisms | Cycling and porosity |

## 5.15 Soil Degradation and Soil Erosion

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Soil degradation** | Decline in soil quality from erosion, exhaustion, salinity, pollution, or misuse |
| **Soil erosion** | Physical removal of topsoil by water, wind, or human disturbance |
| **Soil exhaustion** | Nutrient depletion from continuous cropping without replenishment |
| **Sheet / Rill / Gully / Ravine** | Increasing severity of water-erosion forms |

### Degradation & Erosion — Overview

NCERT treats **soil degradation** and **soil erosion** together. Degradation includes chemical and biological decline; erosion is the physical stripping of topsoil.

### 5.15.1 Soil Degradation

#### Soil Degradation — How It Works

- Degradation means the soil loses fertility, structure, or depth so that productivity falls.
- Causes include deforestation, overgrazing, faulty ploughing, over-irrigation, mining, and continuous cropping without organics.
- Chemical degradation includes salinisation, alkalisation, acidification, and nutrient mining.
- Physical degradation includes compaction, crusting, waterlogging, and topsoil loss.
- Biological degradation includes loss of organic matter and soil organisms.
- Green Revolution zones gained yield but locally gained salinity and waterlogging where drainage lagged.
- Ravine lands and usar patches are extreme degraded end-states in north India.
- Preventing degradation is cheaper than reclaiming after collapse.
- Lock: **degradation = quality decline; erosion = particle removal**.

> **Exam note:** Erosion is one path of degradation, not the only path — salts can degrade soil with little visible sheet wash.

#### Degradation Causes — Table

| Cause | Pathway |
|-------|---------|
| Deforestation | Splash + runoff on bare slopes |
| Overgrazing | Surface exposure + trampling |
| Faulty agriculture | Up-down ploughing; mono-cropping |
| Over-irrigation | Capillary salts; waterlogging |
| Mining / construction | Direct removal of soil mantle |

### 5.15.2 Soil Erosion Types

#### Soil Erosion — How It Works

- Water erosion dominates monsoon India; wind erosion dominates arid western tracts.
- **Sheet erosion** removes a thin film of topsoil and is the most widespread, often unnoticed.
- **Rill erosion** cuts small finger channels on slopes after heavy rain.
- **Gully erosion** cuts deep channels that ordinary tillage cannot erase.
- **Ravines** are extreme gully badlands; **Chambal ravines** are the classic Indian example.
- Wind erosion moves dry sand by **deflation** (lifting/removal) and abrades surfaces; dunes encroach when vegetation fails.
- Deforestation, overgrazing, and clean weeding raise runoff and wind attack.
- Loss of A-horizon humus collapses fertility even if subsoil remains.
- Severity ladder: **Sheet → Rill → Gully → Ravine**.
- Lock the ladder and the Chambal example.

> **Exam note:** Sheet erosion is underestimated because the field still "looks flat."

#### Erosion Types — Table

| Type | Agent | Feature | Example |
|------|-------|---------|---------|
| Sheet | Water | Thin uniform loss | Cultivated slopes |
| Rill | Water | Small channels | Farm slopes |
| Gully | Water | Deep channels | Plateau edges |
| Ravine | Water | Badland maze | Chambal; Agra–Etawah fringe |
| Wind / deflation | Wind | Sand removal, dunes | Thar |

### Soil Conservation — Overview

NCERT defines conservation as maintaining fertility, preventing erosion/exhaustion, and improving degraded soils. Movements and calendar days also appear in Prelims.

#### Soil Conservation — How It Works

- Conservation keeps soil in place, keeps organic matter cycling, and fixes chemical problems.
- Water conservation and soil conservation travel together because runoff drives erosion.
- Cover is the first defence: forests, cover crops, mulches, and regulated grazing.

## 5.17 Soil Conservation Methods

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Contour ploughing** | Ploughing along contours so furrows intercept runoff |
| **Contour / graded bunding** | Earthen bunds on contours (or slight grade) to hold soil and water |
| **Terracing** | Level steps on steep slopes for farming and erosion control |
| **Contour trenching** | Trenches on contours to catch runoff on hills |
| **Shelter belt / wind break** | Tree rows that cut wind speed and stop dune advance |
| **Strip cropping** | Alternating crop strips to break runoff or wind |
| **Check dam / gully plug** | Small barrier trapping silt and water in gullies |
| **Agro-forestry** | Trees with crops/livestock for cover and income |
| **Regulated grazing** | Controlled stocking to protect surface cover |

### Conservation Methods — Overview

Syllabus separates principles (§5.16) from **methods**. Match each method to the erosion agent and landscape.

#### Soil Conservation Methods — How It Works

- **Contour ploughing** follows elevation lines so furrows act as mini barriers to runoff.
- **Contour bunding** builds earthen bunds on contours for gentle agricultural slopes; **graded bunding** allows safe disposal of excess water.
- **Contour trenching** digs trenches on hill contours to catch water and encourage recharge/vegetation.
- **Terracing** creates benches on steep hills for cultivation and slope stability.
- **Strip cropping** alternates close-growing and open-row crops across slope or wind path.
- **Shelter belts / wind breaks** and **agro-forestry** cut wind speed and stop sand-dune encroachment in western Rajasthan (NCERT emphasis).
- **Cover crops, mulching, crop rotation, mixed cropping** keep surface covered and replenish organic matter.
- **Check dams and gully plugging** stop gully heads and trap sediment in ravine belts like Chambal.
- **Afforestation / reforestation** and **regulated forestry** armour watersheds long term.
- **Regulated / controlled grazing** prevents surface exposure from overstocking.
- Chemical methods: **lime** for acid soils; **gypsum** for sodic soils; leaching drains for saline soils.
- Choose by agent: water → contour/terrace/check dam; wind → shelter belt/agro-forestry; chemistry → lime/gypsum.

> **Exam note:** Shelter belts fight **wind**; terraces fight **steep-slope water**. Do not swap.

## 5.18 Soils of Uttar Pradesh

> **Syllabus lock:** `00_Syllabus.md` → UP Geography → **Soils of UP**. This is the state GK layer on top of ICAR types in §5.3–5.11.

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Soils of UP** | Regional soil groups of the state: Bhabar, Tarai, Gangetic alluvium, Bundelkhand, Vindhyan, plus usar/reh patches |
| **Mar / Kabar** | Local black soils of Bundelkhand |
| **Parua / Rakar** | Local red soils of Bundelkhand (Rakar also called pathari) |
| **Bhonta / Monta** | Local Vindhyan red–yellow soils |

### Soils of UP — Overview

UP is **overwhelmingly alluvial**, but exam setters love **Bundelkhand local names**, **Bhabar vs Tarai**, **usar/reh districts**, and **Chambal ravines**. Do not answer every UP soil stem with "alluvial" when the stem says Mar, Kabar, Parua, or Bhonta.

#### Soils of UP — How It Works

- Roughly **nine-tenths** of the state is Gangetic **alluvium** (khadar, bangar, bhur variants).
- Northern fringe: **Bhabar** (pebbly foothill strip) then **Tarai** (moist, nitrogen-rich, crop-friendly) south of it.
- Southern plateau fringe: **Bundelkhand** holds black **Mar/Kabar** and red **Parua/Rakar**; **Vindhyan** belt holds **Bhonta/Monta** red–yellow soils (Mirzapur–Sonbhadra–Prayagraj fringe).
- **Usar/Reh/Kallar/Thur** saline–alkaline patches concentrate in western–central canal tracts (Aligarh, Mainpuri, Etawah, Kanpur, Unnao, Raebareli, Lucknow belt in standard GK lists).
- **Ravine badlands** of the Chambal–Yamuna system hit Agra–Etawah–Jalaun hardest; wind erosion also hits Agra–Mathura–Etawah in dry summers.
- Aravali-fringe reddish soils appear in parts of **Agra** brought by Chambal and tributaries.
- Conservation in UP = drainage + gypsum for usar, check dams/afforestation on ravines, canal lining against seepage, and organic returns against fertility decline.
- Lock: **Alluvial plains + Bundelkhand names + Tarai crops + usar districts + ravine fringe**.

> **Exam note:** UP ≠ Deccan black-soil state. Black soils in UP are mainly **Bundelkhand Mar/Kabar**, not statewide regur.
