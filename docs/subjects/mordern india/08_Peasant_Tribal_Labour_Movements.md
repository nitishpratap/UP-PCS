1. Three streams with distinct targets: **peasant** (rent, indigo, sharecrop), **tribal** (land–forest–*diku*), and **labour** (unions and wages). Method can overlap; the grievance target does not.

2. The **Sanyasi–Fakir** rising (~**1763–1800**) [संन्यासी-फ़क़ीर विद्रोह] in the Bengal–Bihar belt is the early chronology opener. Chronology order often starts here before Indigo and Pabna.

3. **Paika Rebellion (1817)** [पाइक विद्रोह] in Odisha was led by **Jagabandhu Bidyadhar**. **Cause:** loss of paika privileges under Company (कंपनी) land-revenue changes. **Course/Result:** armed rising suppressed; it remains a major pre-1857 tribal/peasant fact.

4. **Ahom rising** is ~**1828** under **Gomdhar Konwar** — **not 1815** (a repeated false year). **Khasi (खासी) Revolt (1829)** was led by **Tirut Singh**.

5. **Kol Rebellion (1831–32)** [कोल विद्रोह] in Chotanagpur (छोटानागपुर) involved leaders such as **Buddhu Bhagat**. **Santhal Hul (हूल) (1855–56)** [संथाल हूल / विद्रोह] was led by **Sidhu and Kanhu** in the Rajmahal / Santhal Parganas belt.

6. **Indigo Revolt (1859–60)** [नील विद्रोह]. **Cause** — European planters' forced indigo and *dadan* (दादनी प्रथा - पेशगी). **Course** — Biswas brothers in Nadia and wider Bengal resistance. **Result** — **Indigo Commission 1860** and a check on planter power.

7. **Deccan Riots (1875)** [दक्कन दंगे]. **Cause** — moneylender (*sahukar* / साहूकार) grip in Poona–Ahmednagar. **Course** — crowds attacked creditor power. **Result** — **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act, 1879**.

8. **Pabna agrarian leagues (1873–85)** fought **zamindar (ज़मींदार) rent-hikes** in Bengal — not the same grievance as indigo planters in 1859–60.

9. **Munda Ulgulan (1899–1900)** [मुंडा उलगुलान / महान हलचल] was led by **Birsa Munda** against *diku* (दिकू) landlords and colonial intrusion in Chotanagpur. **Tana Bhagat (1914)** [ताना भगत आंदोलन] under **Jatra (यात्रा) Oraon** is a later Oraon faith-and-protest stream.

10. **Kuka (Namdhari) rising (~1872)** [कूका आंदोलन] under **Baba Ram Singh** belongs to Punjab — keep it distinct from Bengal peasant leagues.

11. **Champaran (चंपारण) (1917)** attacked the **tinkathia** (तीनकठिया प्रथा - 3/20 भाग) indigo system with Gandhi (गांधी)'s satyagraha method. **Kheda (खेड़ा) (1918)** was a Gujarat no-revenue satyagraha in crop-failure conditions.

12. **UP Kisan Sabha (1918)** is keyed to **Indra (इन्द्र) Narayan Dwivedi (द्विवेदी)** (with Gauri Shankar Mishra) — **not** Baba Ramchandra (बाबा रामचंद्र), Sahajanand or Nehru (नेहरू).

13. **Oudh Kisan Sabha (October 1920, Pratapgarh)** was mobilised by **Baba Ramchandra**. **Eka (एका) (1921–22)** [एका आंदोलन] under **Madari Pasi** stressed rent receipts and oath-bound unity in Hardoi–Bahraich–Sitapur.

14. **Moplah / Malabar (1921)** [मोपला विद्रोह] mixed tenancy grievance with a violent phase under leaders such as **Ali Musliyar** and **Variyamkunnath**. **Bardoli (1928)** was Vallabhbhai Patel's Gujarat no-tax satyagraha — different method and region.

15. **Tebhaga (1946–47)** [तेभागा आंदोलन] in Bengal demanded a **two-thirds** (ते-भागा: 2/3) share for sharecroppers. **Telangana (1946–51)** [तेलंगाना किसान आंदोलन] was the CPI-led armed anti-*jagirdar* struggle in Hyderabad (हैदराबाद) State — if the stem says Nizam (निजाम) / Razakars / *dalams*, fact Telangana.

16. **Rampa / Manyam (1922–24)** was led by **Alluri Sitarama Raju** in the East Godavari (गोदावरी) agency tract. **All India Kisan Sabha (1936)** [अखिल भारतीय किसान सभा] is keyed to **Swami (स्वामी) Sahajanand Saraswati (सरस्वती)** (Lucknow (लखनऊ) session (पंगु सत्र)).

17. **AITUC** (एटक) was founded in **1920** with **Lala Lajpat Rai** (लाला लाजपत राय) as first president. **INTUC (इंटक) (1947)** is the Congress labour wing. **BMS (बीएमएस) (1955)** was founded by **Dattopant Thengadi** (BJP/RSS stream).

18. **N.M. Joshi** (जोशी) represented Indian labour at the **ILO, Washington, 1919**. **Madras Labour Union (1918)** is keyed to **V.P. Wadia**.

19. UPPCS often maps **UTUC (यूटीयूसी) → CPI (सीपीआई)(M)** in match keys even though **CITU (सीटू) (1970)** is the real CPI(M) trade-union centre — learn the map and the real-world correction together.

20. **Bareilly 1816** (Mufti Muhammad Aiwaz) is not **Bareilly 1857** (Khan Bahadur Khan (खान बहादुर खान)). Same city, different revolts and centuries.

21. Match extras often recycled: **Paika–Jagabandhu**, **Bareilly–Mufti Muhammad Aiwaz**, **Malabar–Edachena Kungan**, **Sylhet–Radharam**.

22. Bonded labour is **debt-tied unfree work**; factory (फैक्टरी) wage labour (मजदूरी) is paid and free in law. The **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976** is the modern legal fact.

23. Chronology killer set: **Sanyasi → Indigo → Kuka → Pabna**. Another set: **Pabna 1873–85 (पाबना) | Eka 1921–22 (एका) | Santhal 1855 (संथाल)–56 | Tana Bhagat 1914**.

24. Peasant vs tribal: rent / indigo / zamindar among settled cultivators versus land–forest–*diku* in tribal belts — first filter before picking the leader.

25. Telangana closed when the CPI withdrew around **1951**; do not reduce the whole movement to the **1948** Police Action alone.

26. UP Focus triple: **Dwivedi–UP Kisan Sabha 1918**, **Ramchandra–Oudh Kisan Sabha 1920**, **Madari Pasi–Eka 1921–22**. Mixing any two names fails the stem.

27. **Wahabi** (वहाबी) (Sayyid (सैयद) Ahmad of Rae Bareli; died **Balakot 1831**; later centre **Patna** (पटना)) and **Faraizi** (फ़राज़ी) (Haji Shariatullah / **Dudu Miyan**, Faridpur) are socio-religious streams with an anti-British edge — full orientation card in Topic 6.

28. Pre-1857 match extras: **Velu Thampi** (Travancore), **Vasudev Balwant Phadke** (Ramosi Krishak Jatha), **Gadakari** (**Kolhapur, 1844**), **Waghera** (**Baroda, 1818–19**), **Ho** (**1820–21**), **Khairwar** (**Bhagirath Manjhi, 1874**), **Surendra Sai** (Sambalpur).

29. **Thakkar Bapa** popularised the word **Adivasi** (आदिवासी); **Jadonang** is tagged as the first Gandhi-inspired Naga (नागा) tribal leader; **N.M. Lokhande** pioneered organised mill labour.

30. After Santhal Hul: **Santhal Parganas** + ban on transfer of Santhal land to non-Santhals. Indigo faded in the early twentieth century mainly because of **synthetic dyes**.

## 8.0 One clear picture — three streams of popular protest

This chapter is **not** one long list. Hold three separate streams; every card below plugs into one of them.

| Stream | Core grievance | Typical method | Arc across the chapter |
|--------|----------------|-----------------|-------------------------|
| **Peasant** | Rent, revenue, eviction, indigo/tinkathia, sharecrop share | Legal league → satyagraha → sharecropper strike → armed struggle | Pabna (1873) → Champaran/Kheda/Eka (1917–22) → Bardoli (1928) → Tebhaga / **Telangana** (1946–51) |
| **Tribal** | Land, forest rights, *diku* moneylenders, *begar* (बेगार) | Millenarian faith + armed rising | Kol (1831) → Santhal (1855) → Munda (1899) → Tana Bhagat / Rampa (1914–24) |
| **Labour** | Wages, hours, union recognition (मान्यता) | Strike → union → legislation → party-linked federation | Madras Labour Union (1918) → AITUC (1920) → post-1947 party-wise split (INTUC/UTUC/BMS/CITU) |

- Peasant and tribal streams **overlap in method** (both used armed resistance early, satyagraha later) but differ in the **target**: rent-collecting landlords/zamindars for peasants, *diku* outsiders and forest law for tribals.

- The peasant stream has a **long tail into Independence**: Tebhaga (1946–47) and **Telangana (1946–51)** are the most radical closing chapters, both fought by Communist-led Kisan Sabhas rather than Congress.

- The labour stream is **organisational, not agrarian** — unions, not land. Keep AITUC here as a **founding-fact fact only** (year, place, first president); the deeper left-party politics around labour (Workers and Peasants Party, Congress Socialist Party, later CITU-era realignments) has its **one home in Topic 12**.

> **Logic:** If a question names a **Nizam's State / Andhra Mahasabha / Razakars**, it is **Telangana**, not Tebhaga. If it names a **trade union federation's party tag**, check Topic 12 first for the fuller politics; this topic keeps only the union's birth facts.

### Sanyasi–Fakir Revolt (~1763–1800)

**Period:** ~1763–1800 (peak after the **Bengal famine of 1770**)

**Region:** Bengal and Bihar

**Leaders:** **Majnu Shah** (Fakir), **Musa Shah**, **Bhawani Pathak**, **Devi Chaudhurani**

### Causes

- Hindu sanyasis and Muslim fakirs traditionally moved in armed bands, collecting alms and customary contributions from villages.

- After Plassey (प्लासी) the Company treated these bands as a threat to order and revenue.

- Company rules restricted their movement, taxed pilgrim routes, and cut their right to collect village contributions.

- The **Bengal famine of 1770** left peasants desperate. Many uprooted cultivators joined the bands for survival.

### Course

- Rebels attacked Company *kuthis* (trading posts), government treasuries and loyal zamindars across Bengal and Bihar.

- The rising ran through **Warren Hastings (वारेन)'** years and continued in waves into the 1780s–90s.

- Company troops fought a long, scattered campaign of hit-and-run raids rather than one decisive battle.

### Results

- The rising was gradually crushed only towards the end of the eighteenth century as Company control tightened.

- **Bankim Chandra Chatterjee's** novel *Anandamath* (आनंदमठ) (1882) is set against this background.

- The song (सोंग) **Vande Mataram** comes from that novel.

- In the four-revolt chronology set this is the **earliest** revolt.

> **Logic:** Sanyasi is **late eighteenth century**, not 1859. It comes **before** Indigo, Kuka and Pabna.

### Vellore Mutiny (1806)

**Year:** **1806**

**Region:** Vellore (Madras Presidency (प्रेसीडेंसी))

**Leaders:** Sepoy garrison; Governor-General (गवर्नर-जनरल) **Sir George Barlow** was in office

### Causes

- New dress and appearance rules offended religious custom among sepoys at Vellore.

- The fort also held Tipu Sultan (टीपू सुल्तान)'s family, which added political tension to the garrison.

### Course

- On **10 July 1806** sepoys rose, killed British officers, and briefly seized the fort.

- British troops from Arcot under **Colonel Gillespie** crushed the rising the same day.

### Results

- The mutiny was suppressed quickly and remained local.

- It is an early sepoy-custom rising — a preview theme for **1857**, not part of the Revolt of 1857 itself.

> **Logic:** Vellore = **1806, Barlow**. It is **not** Meerut (मेरठ) 1857 and **not** a peasant league.

### Kuka (Namdhari) Revolt (~1872)

**Year:** ~**1872** (Malerkotla action)

**Region:** Punjab (Bhaini Sahib, Ludhiana; Malerkotla)

**Leaders:** Baba Ram Singh; the sect was earlier shaped by **Balak Singh** and **Bhagat Jawaharmal (Sian Sahab)** in the 1840s; **Ram Singh** was later exiled to **Rangoon (1872)** and died there (**1885**)

### Causes

- The **Namdhari / Kuka** movement began as a Sikh socio-religious reform preaching simplicity, cow protection, boycott (बहिष्कार) of British goods, and opposition to caste display.

- Baba Ram Singh built a strong following around his centre at **Bhaini Sahib**; followers were called Kukas from their cry in kirtan.

- Cow-slaughter by butchers, seen as an attack on Sikh-Hindu sentiment, turned the sect's reform zeal into direct action.

### Course

- In **1872** a Kuka band attacked butchers at **Malerkotla** after cow-slaughter incidents.

- British officer **Cowan** responded by having dozens of Kukas **blown from guns** without a proper trial.

### Results

- Baba Ram Singh was deported to **Rangoon**, ending the movement's organised leadership.

- Place Kuka **after Indigo (1859–60)** and **before Pabna (1873)** — the standard four-revolt chronology.

> **Logic:** Kuka ≠ 1857. Kuka ≠ Pabna. Year tag for chronology is **1872**.

> [!TIP] **Exam Anchor (UPPCS 2025 Q25):** Chronological order of key revolts: **Sanyasi Revolt (1763–1800)** → **Indigo Revolt (1859–60)** → **Kuka Movement (1840s–1872)** → **Pabna Peasant Uprising (1873–76)**.

## 8.2 Peasant Movements and Revolts

- Peasant movements grew from **rent hikes**, eviction (*bedakhli*), **indigo contracts**, and unpaid labour (*begar*).

- Later they also grew from Kisan Sabha, Congress and left politics.

- Early cases often used **leagues and courts**, as at Pabna.

- Later cases used **satyagraha** (सत्याग्रह), as at Champaran, Kheda and Bardoli, or **sharecropper strikes**, as at Tebhaga.

### Pabna Agrarian League (1873–85)

**Year:** **1873–85**

**Region:** Yusufshahi pargana (परगना), **Pabna** district, Bengal

**Leaders:** Ishan Chandra Roy, **Khoodi Mollah**, **Shambhu Nath Pal**

### Causes

- Zamindars raised rents illegally above the recorded rate, forged occupancy papers, and threatened eviction of tenants who resisted.

### Course

- Peasants formed an **agrarian league** in **1873**. They refused illegal cesses, went to court, and used village solidarity rather than arms. Their famous legal declaration was that they wished **"to be the ryots of Her Majesty the Queen and of Her only"** (demanding royal protection under the British crown against zamindari extortion).

- The movement stayed largely **legal and organised** — not a tribal war and not an indigo-factory riot.

- Indian publicists such as **R.C. Dutt** and sections of the Bengal press backed the ryots' cause.

### Results

- Pressure from this long struggle fed into the **Bengal Tenancy Act, 1885**, which gave occupancy tenants some legal protection.

- Pabna ran **1873–85** and comes **last** among Sanyasi–Indigo–Kuka–Pabna.

> **Logic:** Pabna = **zamindar rent**, Bengal, **1873–85**. Deccan 1875 = **moneylenders**. Indigo 1859 = **European planters**.

### Eka Movement (1921–22)

**Year:** **1921–22** (2019 list uses **1922**)

**Region:** Northern Awadh — **Hardoi, Barabanki, Bahraich, Sitapur** (also Unnao / Kheri belt)

**Leader:** Madari Pasi

### Causes

- Awadh tenants faced high rents, illegal **nazrana** (premium), unpaid **begar**, and **bedakhli** (eviction) at the hands of taluqdars.

### Course

- *Eka* means **unity**. Peasants swore — often on Ganga (गंगा) water — to pay **only the recorded rent**, to take a **receipt**, and not to leave the holding.

- They did **not** refuse rent outright. Under high inflation they pressed for **cash** collection of the recorded demand rather than oppressive produce-share rents (often ~50% above recorded rates).

- Congress and Khilafat (खिलाफत) volunteers helped launch the movement. When it turned more militant, Congress pulled back.

- **Madari Pasi**, a **Pasi** (depressed-caste) leader, kept a strong local command after Congress withdrew support.

### Results

- The rising was suppressed by **1922**. It is **not** the 1918 UP Kisan Sabha and **not** Tebhaga.

> **Logic:** Eka = **Awadh 1921–22 + Madari Pasi**. Dwivedi is **1918 UP Kisan Sabha**, not Eka.

### Champaran Satyagraha (1917)

**Year:** **1917**

**Region:** Champaran, Bihar

**Issue:Tinkathia** indigo (peasants had to plant indigo on **3/20** of the holding)

### Causes

- European planters still forced peasants into the **tinkathia** indigo contract long after the 1859 Bengal Indigo Revolt.

### Course

- **Rajkumar Shukla** brought **Gandhi** (गांधी) to Champaran. This became Gandhi's **first satyagraha in India**.

- Gandhi held a public inquiry and recorded peasant statements documenting planter abuses.

### Results

- The inquiry broke the tinkathia system.

- The **Champaran Agrarian Act, 1918** ended the old planter compulsion.

- Do **not** merge this with the **Indigo Revolt of 1859–60** (Bengal, Biswas brothers).

> **Logic:** Champaran **1917** = Gandhi + tinkathia + Bihar. Indigo Revolt **1859–60** = Bengal + Biswas brothers.

### Kheda Satyagraha (1918)

**Year:** **1918**

**Region:** Kheda, Gujarat

**Leaders:** Gandhi, **Vallabhbhai Patel**, Indulal Yagnik

### Causes

- Crop failure and plague made the fixed revenue demand unjust; peasants asked for **remission**.

### Course

- It ran as a **no-revenue** satyagraha — a dress rehearsal in method for **Bardoli 1928** (बारडोली).

- Patel's rural organisational skill was first seen here, building village-level discipline for withholding revenue.

### Results

- The government granted partial relief on the revenue demand.

> **Logic:** Kheda **1918** ≠ Bardoli **1928**. Both are Gujarat revenue satyagrahas; Bardoli is the bigger Patel card.

### Oudh (Awadh) Kisan Sabha (October 1920)

**Year:** **October 1920**, Pratapgarh

**Leader:** Baba Ramchandra (mass mobiliser); Congress and Home Rule (होम रूल) volunteers helped organise

**Demands:** end to *nazrana* (illegal premium), reduced *begar*, security of tenure against *bedakhli*

### Causes

- Awadh tenants faced illegal *nazrana* premiums, unpaid *begar*, and insecurity of tenure through *bedakhli* eviction by taluqdars.

- This is a **separate body** from the **1918 United Provinces Kisan Sabha** of Dwivedi and Gauri Shankar Mishra. Do not merge the two founding dates.

### Course

- Late **1919** saw **Nai-Dhobi Band** social boycott of exploiting landlords in **Pratapgarh** estates; **Jhinguri Singh** and **Durgapal** were early village organisers before Baba Ramchandra dominated the stage.

- **Baba Ramchandra**, a former indentured labourer returned from Fiji, held huge Awadh village meetings using Ramayana-style oral recitation to draw crowds.

- The **UP Provincial Congress Committee** took formal notice and linked the Sabha to the wider Non-Cooperation (असहयोग) mobilisation of 1920–21.

- A young **Jawaharlal Nehru** (जवाहरलाल नेहरू) toured Pratapgarh, Rae Bareli and Faizabad villages in these months and later called the experience his political education, but he did **not found** the Sabha.

- Militancy grew faster than Congress control; taluqdar houses were looted in places, and Congress leaders tried to keep the movement non-violent and constitutional.

### Results

- The more radical **Eka Movement (1921–22)** under **Madari Pasi** grew partly out of this same Awadh discontent when Congress pulled back from the sharper edge of tenant anger.

> **Logic:** **1918 UP Kisan Sabha** (Dwivedi) → **October 1920 Oudh Kisan Sabha** (Ramchandra) → **1921–22 Eka** (Madari Pasi). Three different Awadh-belt bodies, three different years.

### All India Kisan Sabha (1936)

- Founded as **All India Kisan Congress** at **Lucknow (April 1936)** and soon renamed **All India Kisan Sabha**.

- **Swami Sahajanand Saraswati** was President; **N.G. Ranga** was General Secretary. Indulal Yagnik helped publicity.

- Sahajanand founded the **Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha** earlier and is tagged **Kisan-Pran** for devoted peasant work; papers linked to him include ***Hunkar*** and ***Lok Sangraha***.

- A second AIKS session ran with the Congress **Faizpur** session (**1936**), chaired for AIKS by **N.G. Ranga**. The Congress agrarian manifesto for **1937** elections drew heavily on the Kisan Sabha agenda.

- Demands included zamindari abolition, occupancy rights, and an end to illegal cesses.

- Sahajanand is a **1936** figure. He did **not** found the **UP Kisan Sabha in 1918**.

> **Logic:** AIKS **1936** = Sahajanand. UP Kisan Sabha **1918** = **Indra Narayan Dwivedi**.

## 8.4 Indigo Revolt (1859–60)

**Year:** 1859–60

**Region:** Bengal — **Nadia**, Jessore, Pabna, Khulna, Murshidabad

**Leaders:** Digambar Biswas and **Bishnu Biswas** of Chaugacha, Nadia

**Viceroy (वायसराय):** Canning

### Causes

- European planters forced ryots to grow indigo on their **best plots** at a price that did not cover cost.

- Planters gave cash advances (*dadon*) that trapped ryots in endless debt. Refusal to sow brought beatings, cattle seizure, and eviction.

### Course

- The spark is often placed at **Govindpur** in **Nadia**. In **1859** the Biswas brothers of Nadia refused to sow indigo. Village after village followed their lead.

- Ryots stopped planting, socially boycotted planter agents, and in places attacked indigo factories.

- The **Hindu Patriot** under **Harish Chandra Mukherjee** publicised planter crimes; missionaries also wrote against the system.

- **Dinabandhu Mitra (मित्र)** wrote the play ***Nil Darpan*** (1860) dramatising the ryots' suffering.

- **Rev. James Long** was fined and jailed for publishing the English translation of the play.

### Results

- The government appointed the **Indigo Commission, 1860** (Seton-Karr), which held that indigo was grown **only under force**.

- Forced indigo collapsed in large parts of Bengal — this is treated as a **successful peasant revolt (किसान)**. Separately, by the early **twentieth century** indigo also lost world-market value because of **synthetic dyes**.

- It remained a **ryot (रैयत) versus European planter** fight, distinct from a tribal forest war and from Champaran 1917.

> **Logic:** Indigo = **1859–60 Bengal + Biswas brothers + Commission 1860**. 2025 Q25: between Sanyasi and Kuka. 2025 Q127: after Awadh 1856 (अवध), before Second Afghan War 1878.

## 8.5 Deccan Riots (1875)

**Year:** **1875Region:Poona** and **Ahmednagar** districts (Maharashtra Deccan)

**Target:** Moneylenders (*sahukars* / Marwari and Gujarati creditors) — **not** indigo factories

### Causes

- Ryotwari (रैयतवाड़ी) revenue was high and had to be paid in cash even after a bad harvest.

- The **American Civil War** created a **cotton boom**; when it ended, prices crashed and peasants could not repay loans taken during the boom.

- Moneylenders took land, ornaments and enforced **debt bonds**, and courts usually sided with creditors.

### Course

- Riots began in **May 1875** at **Supa** and spread through the Poona–Ahmednagar belt (Koregaon and nearby villages).

- Crowds attacked *sahukar* houses and **burnt bond papers**. The aim was to wipe out the written debt, not random massacre.

### Results

- The **Deccan Riots Commission** inquired into the causes.

- The **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act, 1879** tried to check usury and ease court procedure (प्रक्रिया) for ryots.

- Deccan 1875 stayed a short **debt riot**, unlike Pabna's long **rent-league** struggle or Bardoli's **1928 no-tax satyagraha**.

> **Logic:** Deccan **1875** = moneylenders + Relief Act **1879**. Not 1857, not Birsa, not Bardoli.

## 8.6 Santhal Rebellion (1855–56)

**Year:** **1855–56** | **Region:** **Damin-i-Koh** (दामिन-ए-कोह) / Rajmahal hills (Bhagalpur–Rajmahal belt; later **Santhal Parganas** / संथाल परगना)

**Leaders:** brothers **Sidhu, Kanhu, Chand and Bhairav** Murmu (tag: **Sidhu and Kanhu**)

### Causes

- The Company had settled Santhals in **Damin-i-Koh (1832)** [दामिन-ए-कोह: राजमहल पहाड़ियों की तलहटी] to clear forest and pay rent.

- Outsiders called ***dikus** (दिकू)* (दिकू - बाहरी शोषक, साहूकार व ज़मींदार) — zamindars, *mahajans* (महाजन), traders, police and railway contractors — seized Santhal land and trapped them in debt.

- Courts and police backed the exploiters, and traditional village headmen lost their customary authority.

### Course

- On **30 June 1855** at **Bhognadih**, Sidhu and Kanhu declared the **Hul** (हूल - सशस्त्र क्रांति / विद्रोह), claiming a divine command to end *diku* rule.

- Rebel bands attacked moneylenders, zamindars and symbols of Company power. The rising spread fast across the Santhal belt.

- The British declared martial law and crushed the Hul by **1856**; Sidhu was killed and Kanhu was captured.

### Results

- Act XXXVII of **1855** created a separate **Santhal Parganas** district with a simpler administration. Early in the Hul, Santhal bands defeated **Major Burrough**. After suppression the government also made it illegal for a Santhal to transfer land to a **non-Santhal**.

- Place Santhal **before** Indigo (1859) and **long before** Munda Ulgulan (~1900).

> **Logic:** Santhal = **1855–56 + Sidhu–Kanhu + *diku***. 2018 pair is correct. 2019 code = **1 (1855–56)**. Not Birsa. Not 1857.

> [!TIP] **Exam Anchor (UPPCS 2019 Q18):** Chronology & years match: **Santhal Rebellion** (1855–56) → **Pabna Peasant Uprising** (1873–85) → **Tana Bhagat Movement** (1914) → **Eka Movement** (1921–22).

## 8.7 Kol Rebellion (1831–32)

**Year:** **1831–32Region:Chotanagpur** — Ranchi, Bundu, Tamar, Palamau, Singhbhum

**Leaders:** Buddhu Bhagat, Joa Bhagat, Bindrai Manki, Surga Munda and other Kol chiefs

### Causes

- Company records used "Kol" for Hos, Mundas, Oraons and related groups of the Chotanagpur plateau.

- Tribal land was given to outsider farmers and ***thikedars***. Moneylenders and new revenue rules broke customary rights.

### Course

- Armed resistance exploded in **1831**. Rebels attacked outsider settlements, moneylenders and police posts.

- The rising spread through the Ranchi–Singhbhum tract and was put down by **1832** with Company troops.

### Results

- The same belt later produced **Birsa's Ulgulan (1899–1900)**. Kol is **two generations earlier**.

- Kol revolt is correctly tagged **1831**.

> **Logic:** Kol = **1831 Chotanagpur**. Not Santhal 1855. Not Khasi 1829 (खासी) (Tirut Singh). Not Moplah.

## 8.8 Munda Ulgulan (1899–1900)

**Year:** **1899–1900**

**Region:** Chotanagpur — Ranchi / Khunti belt

**Leader:** Birsa Munda (born **15 November 1875**, Ulihatu; died **9 June 1900**, Ranchi jail)

### Causes

- *Ulgulan* (उलगुलान) means **Great Tumult** (महान हलचल / महाविद्रोह). Mundas held land under ***khuntkatti** (खूँटकट्टी प्रथा)* (खूँटकट्टी - सामूहिक/संयुक्त भूमि व्यवस्था); landlords, contractors, *jagirdars* and colonial law ate into that right.

- Forced labour, forest rules and moneylender debt added to the anger. Missionary activity created religious ferment on top of the agrarian grievance.

- Birsa first came under Christian influence, then left it, and preached a new **Birsait** (बिरसाइत) faith of one God and a golden age without *dikus* (दिकू).

### Course

- Followers are called **Birsaites**. Traditional joint-clearing tenure is **Khuntkatti** (*Mundari* / खूँटकट्टी प्रथा). Birsa is remembered as **Dharti Aaba** (धरती आबा) / **Jagat Pita** (जगत पिता) and preached one God (**Sing Bonga** / सिंगबोंगा). The long prior struggle is also called the **Sardari** fight. The slogan memory is *Abua raj etejana* (अबुआ राज एतेजाना — महारानी राज तुंदु जाना: हमारा राज स्थापित हो).

- On **Christmas 1899** Birsaites attacked churches, police stations and outsider property. Fighting followed at **Sail Rakab** and nearby hills.

- British troops suppressed the rising. Birsa was captured around **early February 1900** (often keyed **3 February**) and died in jail in **June 1900**.

### Results

- The revolt pushed the colonial state towards tribal land protection.

- The **Chotanagpur Tenancy Act, 1908** later recognised *khuntkatti* in law.

- **Janjatiya Gaurav Diwas** is observed on **15 November** (Birsa's birth anniversary).

> **Logic:** Birsa = **Munda, 1899–1900**. Sidhu–Kanhu = **Santhal, 1855**. Do not swap leaders or years.

## 8.9 Tribal Revolts and Tribal Leaders

### Paika Rebellion (1817)

**Year:** **1817**

**Region:** Khurda / Puri (पुरी) / Cuttack, Odisha

**Leader:** Buxi Jagabandhu Bidyadhar Mahapatra (military chief of the Raja (राजा) of Khurda)

### Causes

- Paikas were Odisha's traditional militia holding service tenures (*nish-kar* jagirs) under the Khurda raja.

- After the British took Odisha (**1803**), a new land settlement stripped Paika privileges; salt (लवणाध्यक्ष) tax and revenue pressure added to the anger.

- Jagabandhu's own estate at Rodanga was taken by the Company, giving him a personal grievance alongside the wider Paika discontent.

### Course

- In **March 1817** Jagabandhu led Paikas and peasants against Company posts. The rising spread through Khurda, Puri and nearby tracts.

### Results

- The rising was suppressed by **1818**. Jagabandhu remained in hiding and surrendered later (**1825**).

- Paika Rebellion leader: **Jagabandhu Bidyadhar Mahapatra**.

> **Logic:** Paika = **1817 Odisha + Jagabandhu**. Not Birsa. Not Edachena Kungan.

### Bareilly Revolt (1816)

**Year:** **1816**

**Region:** Bareilly / Rohilkhand

**Leader:** Mufti Muhammad Aiwaz (Iwaz)

### Causes

- The Company tightened police control and imposed a new **house / police tax** in Rohilkhand, sparking protest in **1816**.

### Course

- Mufti Muhammad Aiwaz led the opposition. Crowds clashed with the British at Bareilly.

### Results

- **Mufti Muhammad Aiwaz** led the **1816** Bareilly revolt.

- It is **not** the 1857 Bareilly of **Khan Bahadur Khan**.

> **Logic:** 2024 Bareilly = **Aiwaz, 1816**. 1857 Bareilly = **Khan Bahadur Khan**.

### Sylhet Revolt

**Region:** Sylhet

**Leader (2024 tag):Radharam**

### Causes

- Company revenue demands and tightening control over the Sylhet frontier provoked local resistance in the late eighteenth century.

### Course

- **Radharam**, the zamindar/chief name used in the UPPCS match list, led the local rising against Company authority.

### Results

- Sylhet revolt leader: **Radharam**, not Edachena Kungan.

> **Logic:** Sylhet = **Radharam**. Malabar = **Edachena Kungan**.

### Malabar (Edachena Kungan) and Pazhassi stream

**Leader (2024 tag):Edachena Kungan** (Kungan Nair)

**Region:** Malabar / Wayanad

### Causes

- Malabar passed to the British, who imposed new revenue and forest control that clashed with local chiefs' customary authority.

### Course

- **Edachena Kungan** was a commander of **Pazhassi Raja** (Kerala Varma) in the guerrilla war against the British in Malabar–Wayanad (**c. 1800–05**).

- Pazhassi resisted Company revenue and forest control after Malabar passed to the British.

### Results

- Pazhassi Raja died in **1805**, ending the guerrilla phase.

- Malabar revolt leader in match lists: **Edachena Kungan**.

- The **1921 Moplah** mass rising is a **different** event (tenants + Khilafat), with mass leaders **Ali Musliyar** and **Variyamkunnath Kunjahammed Haji**.

- When the list is Paika / Bareilly / Malabar / Sylhet, mark Malabar = **Edachena**. When the question is 1921 chronology, mark **Moplah 1921** (मोपला).

> **Logic:** Two Malabar tags — **Edachena** for the 2024 match; **1921 Moplah** for year/last-event questions.

### Ahom Rising (1828)

**Year:** **1828** (not **1815**)

**Region:** Assam

**Leader:** Gomdhar Konwar (Ahom prince); **Dhananjay Borgohain** associated

### Causes

- After the **First Anglo-Burmese War** and the **Treaty of Yandabo (1826)**, the British stayed in Assam instead of restoring Ahom rule, angering the Ahom nobility.

### Course

- In **1828** Gomdhar Konwar was proclaimed king with the aim of reviving the Ahom monarchy (राजतंत्र).

### Results

- The rising was crushed. Later plots (around 1830, Piyoli Phukan / Dhananjay line) also failed.

- **Ahom 1815** is the **wrong** pair; **1828** is correct. **1815** is a trap (do not confuse with the Nepal War of 1814–16).

> **Logic:** Ahom = **~1828**. **1815 is incorrect.**

### Khasi Revolt (1829)

**Year:** **1829**

**Region:** Khasi hills (Meghalaya); Nongkhlaw

**Leader:** Tirot Sing / Tirut Singh

### Causes

- The British wanted a road from **Guwahati to Sylhet** through the Khasi hills. An agreement with Tirot Sing of Nongkhlaw broke down.

### Course

- In **1829** Tirot Sing attacked British parties. Fighting continued into the early 1830s.

### Results

- Tirot Sing was captured (**1833**) and died in **Dhaka jail (1835)**.

- Khasi revolt is correctly tagged **1829**.

> **Logic:** Khasi **1829** = Tirut Singh. Kol **1831**. Santhal **1855**.

### Tana Bhagat Movement (1914)

**Year:** **1914**

**Region:** Chotanagpur (Oraon country)

**Leader:** Jatra Oraon (Jatra Bhagat) of Chingri village

### Causes

- *Tana* means to **pull** (pulling out evil spirits). It began as an Oraon socio-religious purification movement: no liquor, no meat, no ghosts.

### Course

- The movement then turned against **landlords, begar and colonial rent**. Tana Bhagats refused illegal cesses.

- Later some Tana Bhagats joined the Non-Cooperation climate (khadi (खादी), no-rent).

### Results

- The ** year remains 1914**, the founding date of the movement.

- Tana Bhagat movement is correctly tagged **1914**.

> **Logic:** Tana Bhagat **1914** ≠ Eka **1922** ≠ Pabna **1873**. Oraon, not Munda/Birsa.

### Rampa / Manyam Rebellion (1922–24)

**Year:** **1922–24**

**Region:** Agency tracts of East Godavari / Visakhapatnam (Andhra)

**Leader:** Alluri Sitarama Raju

### Causes

- Forest laws, *muthadar* exploitation and toddy/tax pressure hit the hill people of the Rampa country.

### Course

- Alluri Sitarama Raju led a **guerrilla** war. The Non-Cooperation years supplied the political climate; the fight itself was armed.

### Results

- He was killed in **1924**. Do not give him the Paika 1817 slot.

> **Logic:** Alluri = **Rampa 1922–24**. Jagabandhu = **Paika 1817**.

### Other tribal / frontier risings (match extras)

**Pagal Panthi (1820s, Sherpur / Mymensingh)**

- *Cause:* Zamindars pressed rent and dues on Garo (गारो)–Hajong peasants in the Sherpur–Mymensingh tract.

- *Course:* A religious-agrarian sect under **Karam Shah**, then **Tipu Shah**, organised peasant resistance against the zamindars.

- *Result:* The rising stayed a local agrarian-religious protest, remembered mainly as a match-list leader tag.

**Chuar rising (late eighteenth century, peaked 1798–99)**

- *Cause:* Dispossessed jungle zamindars and tribal paiks lost land and livelihood as the Company tightened revenue in the Jungle Mahals.

- *Course:* Armed bands under leaders such as **Durjan Singh** raided the Midnapore–Bankura belt.

- *Result:* Company forces suppressed the rising by the turn of the century.

**Bhil rising, Khandesh (~1818–31)**

- *Cause:* The Maratha (मराठा) collapse and Company pacification measures disrupted Bhil autonomy in Khandesh.

- *Course:* Bhil chiefs, including **Sewaram**, resisted Company control through the 1820s.

- *Result:* Company control was gradually established. A **later, separate** Bhil satyagraha followed under **Govind Guru (गुरु)** at **Mangarh, 1913**, in the Banswara–Sunth belt.

**Khond rising, Ghumsar (mid-nineteenth century)**

- *Cause:* Colonial interference with Khond land rights and suppression of *mariah* (human sacrifice) rites angered the community.

- *Course:* **Chakra Bisoi** led resistance in the Odisha hills / Ghumsar tract.

- *Result:* The rising was contained by colonial forces; it remains a leader-tag fact for the Khond hills.

**Ramosi rising (1820s, Western Ghats (पश्चिमी घाट))**

- *Cause:* The fall of the Peshwa (पेशवा) left Ramosi hill-watch communities without their traditional livelihood and status.

- *Course:* **Umaji Naik** led Ramosi unrest against the new British administration.

- *Result:* The rising was suppressed; Umaji Naik was later captured and executed.

**Wahabi (anti-British edge — full stream in Topic 6)**

- *Cause/Course:* **Sayyid Ahmad of Rae Bareli** built a militant revivalist network (inspired in India by **Shah Waliullah's** lineage of reform). He briefly held **Peshawar (~1830)** and died at **Balakot (1831)**. After his death **Patna** became the main Indian centre (Inayat Ali, Wilayat Ali and others).

- *Result:* A long underground challenge to Company power; do not confuse with Aligarh (अलीगढ़) loyalism.

**Faraizi (Bengal)**

- *Cause:* **Haji Shariatullah** of **Faridpur** founded the Faraizi sect for radical religious–social change among Bengal Muslims.

- *Course:* Son **Dudu Miyan** organised followers against English and zamindar pressure (**~1818–1862**).

- *Result:* Many followers later merged into the Wahabi stream.

**Velu Thampi (Travancore)**

- *Cause:* Wellesley (वेलेज़ली) forced Travancore into **subsidiary alliance (सहायक संधि) (1805)**; subsidy arrears and a harsh Resident angered the court.

- *Course:* Diwan **Velu Thampi** rose with **Nair** support against the British.

- *Result:* The rising was crushed; it remains the Kerala pre-1857 Diwan-revolt tag.

**Vasudev Balwant Phadke / Ramosi Krishak Jatha**

- *Cause:* Early revolutionary (क्रांतिकारी) anger at colonial rule in western India.

- *Course:* **Phadke (1845–83)** organised Ramosi hill-people into a fighting force and founded a **Ramosi farmers' group**.

- *Result:* He was captured and died in custody (**1883**). Keep him distinct from Umaji Naik's earlier Ramosi unrest.

**Gadakari rising (Kolhapur, 1844)**

- *Cause:* Hereditary Maratha fort servants (*gadakaris*) faced harsh revenue, loss of army (सेना) berths and *mamlatdar* control of land.

- *Course:* They rose at **Kolhapur in 1844**.

- *Result:* British troops suppressed the rising after hard fighting.

**Waghera (Baroda, 1818–19)**

- *Cause:* Pressure from the Gaekwad backed by the Company.

- *Course:* Waghera chiefs attacked British territory in **1818–19**.

- *Result:* Peace treaty **~1820**.

**Ho rising (1820–21)**

- *Cause:* Colonial intrusion in the Singhbhum / Chotanagpur Ho belt.

- *Course:* Ho tribals rose in **1820–21**.

- *Result:* Order was restored only after prolonged military pressure; the belt stayed disturbed into the 1830s.

**Khairwar (1874)**

- *Cause:* Colonial and moneylender pressure on Khairwar tribal cultivators in the Chotanagpur fringe.

- *Course:* **Bhagirath Manjhi** led the **1874** rising.

- *Cause:* Dynastic claim to Sambalpur against British settlement.

- *Course:* Long anti-British guerrilla struggle; he **surrendered in 1862**.

- *Leader:* **Ganga Narain**.

- *Cause:* Social reform among Bhils of Mewar (मेवाड़)–Bagar (बागड़)–nearby states.

- *Course:* **Govind Guru** (Lasodhiya) founded **Samp Sabha (1883)** to organise Bhils; later remembered with the **Mangarh** sacrifice line.

## 8.10 Moplah Rebellion (1921)

**Year:** **1921** (outbreak **20 August 1921**)

**Region:** Malabar — especially **Ernad** and **Valluvanad** taluks (present Kerala)

**Leaders:** Ali Musliyar; **Variyamkunnath Kunjahammed Haji**

- The **2024** Malabar match-tag is a different stream. That list wants **Edachena Kungan**, not the 1921 Moplah leaders.

### Causes

- Moplah (Mapilla) tenants held land under the ***jenmi*** (landlord) system. Tenure was insecure; rents, renewal fees and eviction were common.

- Agrarian tension in Malabar was older than 1921. **Khilafat** and **Non-Cooperation** (असहयोग) gave it a political spark.

### Course

- The large outbreak followed a British search at the **Tirurangadi** mosque area on **20 August 1921**.

- Rebels attacked unpopular landlords, police stations, courts and colonial offices. A parallel Khilafat administration was attempted in places.

- The government imposed **martial law**. Later phases saw communal killings; the **root remains tenancy**, not "only a riot".

### Results

- The **Wagon Tragedy (November 1921)**: about **70** Moplah prisoners suffocated in a closed railway wagon.

- By late 1921 the revolt was crushed. Ali Musliyar was captured and hanged; Variyamkunnath was shot.

- Chronology: Home Rule (**1916**) → Jallianwala (**1919**) → Khilafat (**1919**) → Moplah (**1921**, last).

> **Logic:** Moplah = **1921 Malabar tenants**. Not Bardoli 1928. Not 1919. Patel did not lead it.

> [!TIP] **Exam Anchor (UPPCS 2023 Q47):** Chronological sequence: Home Rule (1916) → Rowlatt / Jallianwala Bagh (April 1919) → Khilafat (1919–20) → **Moplah Rebellion (August 1921)** — Moplah was the latest event.

## 8.11 Tebhaga Movement (1946–47)

**Year:** **1946–47**

**Region:** Bengal — especially **Dinajpur, Rangpur, Jalpaiguri, 24 Parganas**, Mymensingh

**Demand:** sharecroppers (*bargadars*) keep **two-thirds (te-bhaga)** of the crop; landlord gets one-third

**Against:** ***jotedars*** (rich tenants / landlords), not European indigo planters

### Causes

- Custom was **half-and-half**. The tiller wanted **2/3** because he supplied labour, cattle and seed.

- The **Floud Commission (1938–40)** had already recommended a two-thirds share for the bargadar, but the government did not enforce it.

### Course

- The **Bengal Provincial Kisan Sabha** and **CPI** cadres organised harvest-time action: peasants stacked paddy in their own khamar (yard) instead of the jotedar's.

- Women played a visible role in guarding the crop. The red flag of the Kisan Sabha was common in the north Bengal belt.

- Jotedars and police hit back. The movement was strongest in 1946 and faded in 1947 amid communal partition violence.

### Results

- Later bargadar laws in independent Bengal partly answered the same demand.

- Place Tebhaga **after Bardoli (1928)** and at the **end** of the colonial peasant timeline.

> **Logic:** Tebhaga = **Bengal 1946–47 + 2/3 share**. Eka = Awadh **1921–22**. Bardoli = Gujarat **1928** no-tax.

## 8.12 Telangana Peasant Movement (1946–51)

**Region:** Nalgonda, Warangal (वारंगल), Khammam belt of the **Hyderabad State** (Telangana country, not British India directly)

**Against:*deshmukhs*** and ***jagirdars*** (big landlords), the Nizam's illegal grain levy, and ***vetti*** (unpaid forced labour)

**Organisers:** **Andhra Mahasabha**, then the **Communist Party of India (CPI)**-led Andhra Mahasabha; armed squads called ***dalams***

**Key names:** **Puchalapalli Sundarayya**, **Ravi Narayan Reddy**, **Chandra Rajeswara Rao**, **Devulapalli Venkateswara Rao**, **Makhdoom Mohiuddin** (poet-organiser)

### Causes

- Peasants in the Nizam's Telangana country faced *vetti* begar, illegal cesses, and eviction by *deshmukhs* who doubled as revenue farmers and moneylenders.

- The **Andhra Mahasabha**, originally a moderate (नरम दल) cultural-political body, radicalised through the 1940s as Communists took its leadership.

### Course

- The spark in **1946** was resistance to grain requisitioning and to a *deshmukh* (देशमुख)'s attempt to grab peasant land at **Kadavendi** (Warangal). Armed *dalams* began seizing landlord land and redistributing it to the tiller.

- The Nizam's private militia, the ***Razakars*** under **Kasim Rizvi**, was used to crush the peasants and terrorise Hindus in the state; this connects Telangana to the wider Hyderabad-accession crisis.

- India's **Police Action ("Operation Polo," September 1948)** ended the Nizam's independent rule and merged Hyderabad into the Indian Union. The **peasant struggle continued** after Police Action, now against the same landlords under the Indian administration — this is the trap: **1948 ends the Nizam, not the movement**.

- At its peak the movement's *dalams* controlled villages across roughly **3,000 villages**, redistributed close to **a million acres**, and ran village communes with their own courts (*gram raj*).

### Results

- The **CPI withdrew the armed struggle in October 1951**, ahead of the first general elections, on a changed party line. That withdrawal — not the Nizam's defeat — is the correct **end date, 1951**.

- Telangana is the **largest and only sustained armed peasant insurrection** in the freedom-and-early-Republic period, distinct from Tebhaga's disciplined harvest-time civil action.

- It fed directly into the post-Independence push for **land-ceiling and tenancy-reform legislation** in Andhra/Hyderabad and beyond.

> **Logic:** Telangana = **1946–51, Nizam's Hyderabad, CPI-led *dalams***. Do **not** stop the movement at Police Action **1948**; the correct closing year is **1951**. Do not merge with **Tebhaga (Bengal, 1946–47, sharecrop share)**.

## 8.13 Bardoli Satyagraha (1928)

**Year:** **1928**

**Region:** **Bardoli** (बारडोली) taluka, Surat district, Gujarat (Bombay Presidency)

**Leader:** **Vallabhbhai Patel**

### Causes

- The Bombay government raised land revenue in Bardoli by about **22 per cent** (a 30 per cent hike had been discussed) despite falling prices and peasant distress.

### Course

- Peasants, including many Patidar cultivators, refused to pay the enhanced demand. This was a **no-tax satyagraha**, not an armed tribal revolt.

- Patel organised volunteers, village committees, and **social boycott** of those who paid or helped attachment of property.

- The government attached land, cattle and houses, but the satyagraha held firm.

- Women (Mithuben Petit and local cadres) ran pickets and relief. Gandhi blessed the struggle from a distance; the field commander was Patel.

### Results

- An inquiry (**Maxwell–Broomfield**) found the enhancement unjust, and the hike was cut. The campaign is remembered as a **success**.

- Women of Bardoli hailed Patel as **Sardar**; Gandhi's recognition of that title stuck from this victory.

- Compare: **Kheda 1918** is an earlier Gujarat revenue satyagraha; **Moplah 1921** is Malabar violence; **Tebhaga 1946** is a Bengal produce-share movement.

> **Logic:** Bardoli **1928** = Patel + revenue enhancement. Not 1921. Not Birsa. Not tebhaga.

## 8.15 Bonded Labour Practices

- **Bonded labour** (बंधुआ मज़दूरी - जैसे बिहार-झारखंड में कमिया/कमियौती प्रथा [Kamiuti / Kamia], हरवाही प्रथा) is work tied by **debt or custom** so the worker cannot leave freely. The loan never clears; the family often inherits the debt.

- ***Begar*** (बेगार - निःशुल्क/बलपूर्वक श्रम) is unpaid labour taken by landlords, taluqdars or officials. It was a core Awadh and tribal grievance (Eka, Tana Bhagat, Kol, Munda).

- Indigo ***dadon*** (दादनी प्रथा - अग्रिम पेशगी) tied Bengal ryots into unfree crop contracts. That is crop bondage, not a mill wage.

- Deccan *sahukars* (साहूकार) and Santhal *mahajans* (महाजन) used **bond papers** as weapons. Burning those papers in 1875 was an attack on bondage, not random loot.

- Plantation and *arkatti* (अरकाठी प्रथा) recruitment in Assam also used advances and penal contracts (a related unfree-labour story).

- **Article 23** of the Constitution prohibits traffic in human beings and *begar* (बेगार) / similar forms of forced labour (बलात् श्रम).

- The **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976** [बंधुआ श्रम प्रणाली (उन्मूलन) अधिनियम, 1976] is the main statute after independence. It frees bonded labourers and extinguishes the debt.

- **AITUC (1920)** organised factory wage workers. It did **not** abolish village bondage.

> **Logic:** Bondage here = **debt, indigo advances, *diku* moneylenders, begar**. Not the same as INTUC/AITUC mill unions. Act = **1976**, not 1920.

**Answer: (c)**

**Explanation:**
The Indigo Revolt (1859-60 A.D.) took place in Bengal

immediately after the revolt of 1857. Sanyasi Rebellion

in 1855-56 A.D., and Pabna uprising took place in 1873-76

Movement through their articles and rallies. The editor

portrayed by Dinbandhu Mitra in his play Neel Darpan.

Ulgulan Revolt was related to –

- (a) Santhal

- (b) Kachha Nagh

- (c) Kol

- (d) Birsa Munda

<details><summary>Show answer</summary>

**Answer: (d)**

**Explanation:**
Munda Rebellion was one of the most prominent 19th-Century

tribal rebellions in the subcontinent. Birsa Munda led this

movement in the region in 1899-1900 A.D. The Ulgulan,

meaning ‘Great Tumult,’ sought to establish an independent

Munda Raj. The Mundas traditionally enjoyed a preferential

rent rate as the Khuntkatti or the original clearer of the forest.

But in the course of the 19th century, they had seen this

Khuntkatti land system being eroded by the jaghirdars and

thikedars coming as merchants and moneylenders. In 1895

A.D., Birsa claiming to have seen a vision of God, proclaimed

himself a prophet with miraculous healing powers. Thousands

flocked to hear the ‘new word’ of Birsa with its prophecy of

an imminent deluge. The new prophet became a critic of the

traditional tribal customs, religious beliefs, and practices. He

called upon the Mundas to fight against superstition, give

up animal sacrifice, stop taking intoxicants, wear the sacred

thread and retain the tribal tradition of worship in the Sarna

or the sacred grove. It was essentially a revivalist movement,

which sought to purge Munda society of all foreign elements

and restore its pristine character. Christianity influenced the

movement as well and it used both Hindu-Christian idioms

to create the Munda ideology and worldview. On Christmas

Eve 1899 A.D., the Mundas shot arrows and tried to burn

down churches over an area covering six police stations in the

districts of Ranchi and Singhbhum. Next, in January 1900,

the police stations were targeted and there were rumours

that Birsa’s followers would attack Ranchi on 8th January,

leading to panic there. However, on 9th January, the rebels

were defeated. Birsa was captured and died in jail. Nearly

350 Mundas were put on trial, and three of them were hanged

and 44 transported for life.

</details>
