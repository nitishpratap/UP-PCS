## 11.1 Overview — From Swadeshi to Revolutionary Politics

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Swadeshi Movement** | Mass campaign (esp. after 1905) for Indian goods, education, and boycott of British products |
| **Revolutionary Movement** | Secret societies and armed actions aiming to overthrow British rule by force |
| **Delhi Durbar (1911)** | Imperial assembly where Partition was annulled and capital shift announced |

### Causes — Why This Phase Matters

1. Curzon’s Partition of Bengal radicalised Bengal politics and nationalised protest.
2. Moderate petition methods looked weak against a settled imperial decision.
3. Swadeshi created a mass repertoire: boycott, national schools, swadeshi shops.
4. Repression, arrests, and Surat Split pushed some youth toward secret societies.
5. World War I diaspora plots (Ghadar/Berlin) and 1920s UP–Punjab–Bengal actions formed a second revolutionary wave.

### Overview — How It Works

- Topic 11 covers two linked stories: the **open Swadeshi–Boycott phase (1905–11)** and the **secret revolutionary phase** that grew beside and after it.
- Exam questions usually mix three layers: **1905–11 chronology**, **organisation↔event matching**, and **UP Kakori geography** (Shahjahanpur–Kakori–Gorakhpur–Allahabad).
- Do not treat Extremists and revolutionaries as identical. **Lal–Bal–Pal** led assertive public politics; **Anushilan / HRA / HSRA** ran underground armed work.
- **Partition of Bengal (1905)** is both a **cause** of Swadeshi and a **matching fact** for Curzon (**2025 Q40**). The new province was **East Bengal and Assam**.
- **1911** is a year trap: Partition was annulled and the capital moved from Calcutta to Delhi; the **Lucknow Pact (1916)** does **not** belong in that year (**2025 Q6**).
- Revolutionary centres shift over time: Bengal (Anushilan/Jugantar) → Maharashtra/London (Abhinav/India House) → North America (Ghadar 1913) → UP (HRA/Kakori 1925) → Punjab–Delhi (HSRA 1928–31) → Bengal again (Chittagong 1930).
- Chronology drills reward order memory: Muzaffarpur/Alipore **1908** → Hardinge **1912** → Ghadar **1913** → Kakori **1925** → Assembly Bomb **1929** → Chittagong **1930**.
- UPPCS repeatedly tests founder/org pairs: **Abhinav Bharat = Savarkar**, **India House = Shyamji Krishna Varma**, **Hardinge bomb = Anushilan**, **Kakori = HRA**.
- Trap: "Swadeshi began only after Gandhi returned in 1915." Swadeshi’s first great mass phase is **1905**.
- Trap: "All Swadeshi leaders threw bombs." Most Swadeshi work was public economic-cultural protest.
- Trap: "Partition of Bengal = 1911." Partition is **1905**; annulment is **1911**.
- Trap: "Delhi became capital in 1905." Capital shift was announced at the **1911** Durbar.

> **Exam note:** First lock **1905 partition / 1911 annulment+capital**, then org–event pairs.

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Partition 1905; annulment + capital 1911
- Swadeshi = boycott + Indian goods + national education
- Extremists ≠ all revolutionaries
- Kakori = HRA, UP
- HSRA = 1928 socialist turn

2. **(UPPCS Prelims 2025, Q40)** Curzon matched with → **Partition of Bengal**.

## 11.2 Partition of Bengal (1905)

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Partition of Bengal (1905)** | Curzon’s division of Bengal Presidency into Bengal and **East Bengal & Assam** |
| **Administrative excuse** | Official claim: Bengal was too large to govern efficiently |
| **Political reading** | Nationalists saw divide-and-rule — splitting Hindu-majority west and Muslim-majority east |

### Causes

1. Bengal Presidency was huge; Curzon claimed administrative convenience.
2. Colonial desire to weaken Calcutta’s nationalist intelligentsia.
3. Hope that a Muslim-majority eastern province would blunt anti-British unity.
4. Wider Curzonian assertiveness in frontier, education, and police policy.
5. Rising nationalist press and associations made Bengal the political heartland.

### Course of Partition — Step by Step

1. Curzon’s government prepares territorial rearrangement (official “administrative” case).
2. **July 1905:** Partition announcement becomes public; anti-Partition meetings surge.
3. **7 Aug 1905:** Calcutta Town Hall **Boycott Resolution** formally launches Swadeshi–Boycott.
4. New province **East Bengal and Assam** is constituted (capital **Dacca**); western Bengal kept with Bihar–Orissa linkage in the settlement design.
5. **16 Oct 1905:** Partition comes into force — mourning day, Raksha Bandhan solidarity, Tagore’s *Amar Sonar Bangla* protest culture.
6. Anti-Partition press campaigns intensify (*Hitabadi*, *Sanjibani*, *Bengalee*).
7. Movement spreads beyond Bengal under Extremist leadership; secret societies also recruit.
8. British reverse course at **1911 Delhi Durbar** (annulment + capital shift).

### Partition of Bengal — How It Works

- **Lord Curzon** announced the Partition of Bengal in **July 1905**. It took effect on **16 October 1905**, remembered in Bengal as a day of mourning and Rakhi protest.
- The British created a new province of **East Bengal and Assam** with capital at **Dacca (Dhaka)**. Western Bengal remained with Bihar and Orissa under Calcutta.
- Official justification was administrative convenience: Bengal was said to be too large to govern. Nationalists rejected this as political **vivisection** meant to split Bengali unity and weaken Hindu–Muslim anti-colonial solidarity.
- Two statements often appear together in Prelims: Curzon rearranged boundaries **and** East Bengal & Assam was created — both are true (**2019 Q95**).
- Partition is the classic **Curzon matching** contribution (**2025 Q40**). If an option names Curzon’s major act, choose Partition of Bengal.
- Anti-Partition protest fused immediately with **Swadeshi economics**, boycott of British goods, and national education experiments. That fusion is why 1905 opens both the Partition and Swadeshi chapters.
- The Partition was **annulled in 1911** at the Delhi Durbar, together with the decision to move the imperial capital from Calcutta to Delhi. Annulment changed the map but did not erase communal politics.
- Partition also accelerated Moderate–Extremist tension inside Congress and pushed some youth toward secret societies, so the 1905 map decision is a cause of both Swadeshi politics and revolutionary recruitment.
- Trap: "Partition created Pakistan in 1905." Wrong century outcome; this is a provincial rearrangement inside British India.
- Trap: "Partition annulled in 1905 same year." Annulment is **1911**.
- Trap: "East Bengal and Assam never existed." It did — that is the 1905 new province.
- Trap: "Partition was Wellesley’s idea." It is **Curzon**.

> **Exam note:** **2019 Q95 = Both statements**; **Curzon ↔ Partition**.

|--------|----------------|

## 11.3 Swadeshi Movement

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Swadeshi Movement** | Mass campaign (esp. 1905–08) for Indian goods, national education, self-reliance (**atmashakti**), and boycott of British goods/institutions |
| **Boycott Resolution (7 Aug 1905)** | Formal Swadeshi–Boycott proclamation at Calcutta Town Hall |
| **Passive resistance** | Extremist programme to make British administration/commerce unworkable by organised refusal (Aurobindo) |
| **National Council of Education (Aug 1906)** | Body to organise literary, scientific, technical education on national lines under national control |
| **Samitis** | Volunteer corps for mobilisation, social work, swadeshi propaganda (e.g. Swadesh Bandhab Samiti) |

### Causes

1. Partition of Bengal (announced July 1905; effective 16 Oct 1905) insulted Bengal unity and nationalist pride.
2. Moderate “prayer–petition–protest” methods failed to stop a settled imperial decision.
3. Curzon-era repression (Universities Act, Official Secrets Act, Calcutta Corporation cuts) hardened opinion.
4. International examples (Japan’s rise; Ethiopia/Boer shocks to European invincibility) raised Asian self-confidence.
5. Drain/economic critique made boycott of British goods feel like practical nationalism.
6. A trained Extremist leadership (Lal–Bal–Pal, Aurobindo) offered assertive methods and Swaraj language.

### Course of Swadeshi — Step by Step

1. **July 1905:** Partition announcement; anti-Partition meetings intensify.
2. **7 Aug 1905:** Calcutta Town Hall passes **Boycott Resolution** — formal Swadeshi–Boycott launch.
3. **16 Oct 1905:** Partition day observed as mourning; Raksha Bandhan solidarity; Tagore’s *Amar Sonar Bangla* enters protest culture.
4. Moderate phase tools dominate early: petitions, meetings, pamphlets, papers (*Hitabadi*, *Sanjibani*, *Bengalee*).
5. Extremist leadership expands methods: boycott of foreign cloth, schools, courts, titles, councils; passive resistance doctrine.
6. **National education:** Bengal National College (Aurobindo as principal); **National Council of Education (15/Aug 1906)**; Bengal Technical Institute stream.
7. **Samitis** organise magic-lantern lectures, swadeshi songs, craft training, social work (Barisal’s Swadesh Bandhab Samiti — Ashwini Kumar Dutt).
8. Movement spreads: Maharashtra (Tilak), Punjab (Lajpat Rai), Madras/Tuticorin (V.O. Chidambaram Pillai / Swadeshi Steam Navigation).
9. **Calcutta Congress 1906** (President Dadabhai Naoroji): Congress goal declared as **Swaraj / self-government**; supports anti-Partition–Swadeshi.
10. Repression, arrests, and Moderate–Extremist clash culminate in **Surat Split 1907**; open Swadeshi intensity declines after 1908, while underground revolution grows.

### Methods Table (must raata)

| Method | What it meant in practice |
|--------|---------------------------|
| **Boycott of foreign goods** | Bonfires of Manchester cloth; swadeshi shops |
| **Swadeshi enterprise** | Indian mills, soap, matches; P.C. Ray’s Bengal Chemical examples |
| **National education** | Parallel schools/colleges; vernacular medium; technical education |
| **Passive resistance** | Boycott titles, councils, courts, govt schools/jobs |
| **Samitis / volunteers** | Grassroots mobilisation + social service |
| **Festivals & culture** | Ganapati/Shivaji-type political use; swadeshi songs/theatre |
| **Press** | *Bande Mataram*, *Yugantar*, *Kesari*, regional nationalist papers |

### Moderate vs Extremist control of Swadeshi

| Aspect | Moderates | Extremists |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| Early anti-Partition | Petitions, meetings, UK opinion | Want mass pressure |
| Boycott scope | Often limited to goods | Extend to schools, courts, titles, services |
| Goal language | Reform + protest | Swaraj / independence emphasis |
| After 1905 failure | Still constitutional faith | Passive Swadeshi into passive resistance |

### Spread beyond Bengal

| Region | High-yield tag |
|--------|----------------|
| **Bengal** | Epicentre; samitis; national education |
| **Maharashtra** | Tilak; swadeshi–boycott propaganda |
| **Punjab** | Lala Lajpat Rai mobilisation |
| **Madras / Tuticorin** | V.O. Chidambaram Pillai; Swadeshi Steam Navigation Co. vs British shipping |
| **Delhi / UP towns** | Meetings, boycott shops, student activism |

### Swadeshi Movement — How It Works

- Swadeshi is a **political-economic method**, not only “buy Indian cloth.” It meant promoting Indian goods and industries while boycotting British products and, in the Extremist programme, boycotting colonial institutions.
- Lock the date triangle: Partition announced **July 1905** → **Boycott Resolution at Calcutta Town Hall on 7 August 1905** → Partition effective **16 October 1905**.
- Extremists added **passive resistance** to swadeshi and boycott. Aurobindo’s formula was to make colonial administration impossible by withdrawing cooperation from schools, courts, titles, and jobs.
- National education was core Swadeshi culture. The **National Council of Education (August 1906)** and the **Bengal National College** (Aurobindo as principal) belong here; keep BHU detail for Topic 12.
- Samitis were the organisational muscle of local mobilisation. **Ashwini Kumar Dutt’s Swadesh Bandhab Samiti (Barisal)** is the classic district example.
- Outside Bengal, Swadeshi enterprise also appeared in shipping: **V.O. Chidambaram Pillai** founded the **Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company** at Tuticorin to challenge British coastal monopoly.
- At the **Calcutta Congress (1906)**, under president **Dadabhai Naoroji**, Congress declared **Swaraj** as its goal. Do not confuse this with the later **Purna Swaraj** resolution of **Lahore 1929**.
- Decline came from severe repression, the **Surat Split (1907)**, uneven all-India organisation, British communal counter-tactics, and the diversion of some youth into secret revolutionary societies.
- Trap: "Swadeshi began only after Gandhi 1915." Mass Swadeshi starts **1905**.
- Trap: "National Council of Education = BHU." NCE is the **1906** Bengal national-education body; BHU is a later/different stream (Topic 12).
- Trap: "Boycott meant only cloth." Extended boycott also hit schools, courts, titles, and jobs.
- Trap: "Tagore opposed all Swadeshi." He composed *Amar Sonar Bangla* and supported anti-Partition cultural protest.
- Trap: "Surat Split created Partition." Partition is **1905**; Surat is the **1907** Congress split.

> **Exam note:** Lock **7 Aug 1905 Boycott Resolution + NCE 1906 + Swaraj @ Calcutta 1906 + methods table**.

## 11.4 Delhi Durbar (1911) & Annulment of Partition

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Delhi Durbar 1911** | Imperial assemblage for George V’s coronation as Emperor of India |
| **Annulment of Partition** | Reversal of the 1905 Bengal partition |
| **Capital transfer** | Shift of imperial capital from Calcutta to Delhi |

### Causes

1. Sustained anti-Partition and Swadeshi unrest made the 1905 map politically costly.
2. British wished to calm Bengal while redesigning imperial symbolism.
3. Calcutta’s nationalist press/intelligentsia remained a constant challenge.
4. A new capital at Delhi linked Raj legitimacy to older imperial geography.
5. Hardinge-era policy sought a grand settlement gesture in 1911.

### Course — Step by Step

1. Swadeshi–revolutionary unrest continues after 1905–08 peak.
2. Imperial planners prepare Durbar politics and territorial revision.
3. **1911 Delhi Durbar** announces **annulment of Partition**.
4. Same year announces **capital shift Calcutta → Delhi**.
5. Bengal is reunited (with later Bihar–Orissa rearrangements in the wider settlement story).
6. Nationalists claim a partial victory; Extremists/revolutionaries are not demobilised overnight.
7. Hardinge’s ceremonial Delhi politics follows; **1912 bomb** targets his procession.
8. 1911 becomes a permanent chronology trap year (annulment + capital; **not** Lucknow Pact).

### Delhi Durbar — How It Works

- The **Delhi Durbar of December 1911** was an imperial assemblage for the coronation of **King George V** as Emperor of India. It was not a Congress session and not a nationalist conference.
- At this Durbar the British announced two linked decisions: the **annulment of the 1905 Partition of Bengal**, and the transfer of the imperial capital from **Calcutta to Delhi**.
- UPPCS treats **1911** as a bundle year. **2025 Q6** asks which events happened in 1911 and the correct pair is Partition revoked + capital shifted.
- The **Lucknow Pact** does **not** belong in 1911 because Congress and the Muslim League signed it in **1916**. That is the classic distractor in the 2025 question.
- Annulment was a political retreat after years of Swadeshi unrest. It changed the provincial map but did not erase communal politics, and it did not end revolutionary underground activity.
- Lord **Hardinge** was the Viceroy of this settlement era. The new-capital ceremonial politics help explain why revolutionaries targeted his Delhi procession in the **Hardinge bomb case of 23 December 1912**.
- Chronologically, 1911 sits between the Swadeshi peak (**1905–08**) and the Hardinge/Ghadar wartime cluster (**1912–15**). Use it as a hinge year in timelines.
- For Prelims, treat 1911 as a two-fact year only: **Partition annulled + capital to Delhi**. Anything else (Lucknow Pact, Round Table, Quit India) is a later decade distractor.
- Trap: "In 1911 only the capital moved; Partition stayed." Both the map and the capital changed in **1911**.
- Trap: "Partition was revoked in 1908 after the Surat Split." Surat is a Congress split in **1907**; annulment is **1911**.
- Trap: "1911 created East Bengal and Assam." That new province was created in **1905**; 1911 reversed the Partition.
- Trap: "Lucknow Pact between Congress and League was signed in 1911." Lucknow Pact = **1916**.

> **Exam note:** **2025 Q6 = statements 1 and 2 only**.

## 11.5 Revolutionary Organisations, Centres & Activities

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Revolutionary organisation** | Secret society/party using armed struggle to end British rule |
| **Revolutionary centre** | Region where recruitment, bombs, dacoity, or major cases clustered |

### Why Revolutionary Terrorism Grew (after Swadeshi)

1. Open Swadeshi faced brutal repression and leader arrests.
2. Surat Split weakened united public leadership.
3. Youth concluded that petitions/boycott alone would not expel the Raj quickly.
4. Russian nihilist / Irish / international militant examples circulated in print.
5. Religious-cultural idioms of sacrifice (Bankim, Vivekananda influence) romanticised bravery.

### Revolutionary Organisations — How It Works

- The syllabus asks four linked things: **organisations**, **centres**, **activities**, and **events**. Learn them as maps, not as one vague “revolutionaries” label.
- **Wave 1 (about 1906–12)** grew from Swadeshi repression: Bengal’s **Anushilan** and **Jugantar**, Maharashtra’s **Abhinav Bharat**, and London’s **India House**.
- **Wave 2 (World War I years)** was overseas and wartime: **Ghadar Party (1913)** in North America, **Komagata Maru (1914)**, **Berlin Committee (1915)**, and Hindu–German conspiracy attempts.
- **Wave 3 (1920s–30s)** shifted north and then back to Bengal action: **HRA (1924)** and **Kakori (1925)** in UP, **HSRA (1928)** and Assembly/Saunders actions in Punjab–Delhi, and **Chittagong (1930)** under Surya Sen.
- UP is a full revolutionary centre, not a side note. **Mainpuri (1918)**, **Shahjahanpur (Bismil)**, **Kakori–Lucknow (1925)**, **Gorakhpur (hangings 1927)**, and **Allahabad (Azad, 1931)** are all scoring sites.
- Keep **political Extremists** (Lal–Bal–Pal Swadeshi/boycott politics) separate from **secret revolutionary societies**. Extremists worked in public nationalism; revolutionaries worked underground with bombs, raids, and conspiracies.
- Match organisations to signature events: Anushilan → Hardinge **1912**; Abhinav/India House → Dhingra/Nasik **1909**; Ghadar → wartime revolt **1914–15**; HRA → Kakori **1925**; HSRA → Saunders/Assembly **1928–29**; Surya Sen → Chittagong **1930**.
- Activities include assassinations, treasury dacoities, armoury raids, overseas plots, newspaper propaganda, and jail hunger-strike politics — not only speeches.
- Trap: "All revolutionary activity happened only in Bengal." Maharashtra, Punjab, UP, Madras, London, and North America also matter.
- Trap: "Revolutionary movement means only HSRA." Anushilan, Jugantar, Abhinav Bharat, and Ghadar are equally examinable and earlier.
- Trap: "Centres means Congress session venues." Here centres means underground geography of recruitment and action.
- Trap: "Activities means speeches and newspapers only." Activities include assassinations, dacoities, armoury raids, overseas plots, and jail sacrifice politics.

> **Exam note:** Draw one map: **Bengal–Maharashtra–Punjab–UP–overseas**.

## 11.6 Anushilan Samiti

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Anushilan Samiti** | Bengal revolutionary secret society (physical culture + political violence) |
| **Dhaka Anushilan** | Powerful eastern branch associated with **Pulin Behari Das** |
| **Delhi Conspiracy / Hardinge bomb (23 Dec 1912)** | Bomb on Viceroy Hardinge’s ceremonial elephant procession in Delhi |

### Anushilan Samiti — How It Works

- **Anushilan Samiti** began in the early 1900s Calcutta milieu as a secret society that mixed physical culture with revolutionary politics. The Swadeshi years after **1905** radicalised it into a major underground network.
- Early associated names in standard notes include **Promotho Mitter**, **Jatindranath Banerjee**, and **Barindra Kumar Ghosh**, with **Aurobindo Ghosh** as an important ideological influence.
- The eastern branch, **Dhaka Anushilan**, under **Pulin Behari Das**, made East Bengal a powerful revolutionary centre. If an option names Pulin Behari Das, think Dhaka Anushilan, not Abhinav Bharat.
- Anushilan networks reached north India. **Rash Behari Bose** used these contacts to organise the **Delhi Conspiracy**.
- On **23 December 1912**, a bomb was thrown at Viceroy **Lord Hardinge** during his ceremonial elephant procession in Delhi. **Basanta Kumar Biswas** is associated with throwing the bomb.
- UPPCS asks which organisation was responsible and keys **Anushilan Samiti** (**2023 Q42**). **Yugantar** often appears as a distractor.
- The same Bengal ecosystem also produced Muzaffarpur (**1908**) and the **Alipore Bomb Case**, so Anushilan/Jugantar questions often sit beside those events.
- Anushilan belongs to the **pre-HSRA generation**. It is not the organisation of Kakori (**HRA, 1925**) or the Assembly Bomb (**HSRA, 1929**).
- Trap: "Hardinge bomb was done by HSRA." HSRA appears only in **1928**, sixteen years later.
- Trap: "Anushilan was only a gymnasium club." It was a revolutionary secret society.
- Trap: "Pulin Behari Das founded Abhinav Bharat." Abhinav Bharat = **V.D. Savarkar**; Pulin = **Dhaka Anushilan**.
- Trap: "UPPCS Hardinge answer is always Yugantar." The keyed answer is **Anushilan Samiti**.

> **Exam note:** **Hardinge bomb → Anushilan Samiti**; remember **Pulin Behari Das (Dhaka)**.

## 11.7 Jugantar / Yugantar

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Jugantar (Yugantar)** | Action-oriented Bengal revolutionary circle (inner Anushilan stream) around Barindra Kumar Ghosh |
| **Yugantar paper (1906)** | Revolutionary weekly for propaganda |
| **Manicktolla garden house** | Calcutta bomb-making / training centre linked to Alipore case |

### Jugantar — How It Works

- **Jugantar (Yugantar)** was the action-oriented Bengal revolutionary circle linked to **Barindra Kumar Ghosh**. It worked as an inner militant stream beside the broader Anushilan world.
- From **1906**, the weekly **Yugantar** spread revolt ideology, criticised British rule, and recruited angry youth into secret work.
- The circle used the **Manicktolla garden house** in Calcutta as a bomb-making and training centre. That site becomes central when police smash the group in **1908**.
- The **Muzaffarpur bomb attempt (30 April 1908)** by **Khudiram Bose** and **Prafulla Chaki** was meant for Judge Kingsford. The wrong carriage was hit, and the police trail led back to Manicktolla.
- **Khudiram Bose** was hanged in **August 1908** and became a youthful martyr icon; **Prafulla Chaki** shot himself earlier to avoid capture.
- The resulting **Alipore Bomb Case (1908–09)** tried Aurobindo, Barindra, and associates. Aurobindo was defended by **C.R. Das** and acquitted; Barindra and others received sentences.
- In Hardinge-bomb MCQs, **Yugantar** is often placed as a tempting wrong option against **Anushilan Samiti** (**2023 Q42**).
- Jugantar is an early Bengal stream. It must not be merged with later north-Indian parties such as **HRA (1924)** or **HSRA (1928)**.
- Trap: "Jugantar founded HSRA in 1928." Jugantar is an early Bengal circle; HSRA is a later north-Indian socialist party.
- Trap: "Yugantar was a Moderate newspaper like the early Congress press." It was a revolutionary weekly.
- Trap: "Barindra Kumar Ghosh founded Abhinav Bharat." Abhinav Bharat belongs to **Savarkar** in Maharashtra.
- Trap: "Manicktolla has no link to Alipore." Manicktolla is the workshop geography of the Alipore case.

> **Exam note:** Jugantar = **Barindra + Yugantar paper + Manicktolla/Alipore stream**.

## 11.8 Abhinav Bharat, Maharashtra & India House

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Mitra Mela** | Early Savarkar secret society (Nasik) that evolved into Abhinav Bharat |
| **Abhinav Bharat Society** | Revolutionary organisation of **V.D. Savarkar** |
| **India House (London, 1905)** | Hostel/political hub founded by **Shyamji Krishna Varma** for Indian students/revolutionaries |
| **Nasik Conspiracy (1909)** | Anant Kanhere assassinates A.M.T. Jackson, District Magistrate of Nasik |

### Abhinav Bharat & Overseas — How It Works

- Maharashtra’s revolutionary stream is older than HSRA. In **1897**, the **Chapekar brothers** killed plague commissioner **Rand** in Pune, an early marker of militant action in western India.
- **V.D. Savarkar** organised youth through **Mitra Mela**, which developed into the **Abhinav Bharat Society** around **1904**. UPPCS directly asks this founder match (**2018 Q22**).
- In **1905**, **Shyamji Krishna Varma** founded **India House** in London as a hostel and political hub for Indian students. Savarkar’s influence made it a university of revolt for overseas Indians.
- On **1 July 1909**, **Madanlal Dhingra** assassinated **Curzon Wyllie** in London. India House faced intense surveillance and crackdown after this.
- In **December 1909**, **Anant Kanhere** shot **A.M.T. Jackson**, the District Magistrate of Nasik. The Nasik Conspiracy Case hit Abhinav Bharat networks hard.
- In the Madras Presidency, **Vanchinathan** killed collector **Ashe** in **1911**, reminding examiners that the southern Presidency was also a revolutionary centre.
- Keep three London–Maharashtra matches separate from Bengal and Ghadar: **Abhinav = Savarkar**, **India House = Shyamji**, **Dhingra = Curzon Wyllie 1909**.
- Do not confuse Dhingra’s London assassination (**1909**) with the Hardinge bomb in Delhi (**1912**) or with Bhagat Singh’s Assembly Bomb (**1929**).
- Trap: "Abhinav Bharat was founded by Bhagat Singh, Barindra, or Pulin Behari Das." The founder tag is **V.D. Savarkar**.
- Trap: "India House was founded by Lala Hardayal." Founder tag = **Shyamji Krishna Varma**; Hardayal is mainly the **Ghadar** stream.
- Trap: "Dhingra killed Lord Hardinge." Dhingra killed **Curzon Wyllie in London (1909)**; Hardinge was bombed in **Delhi in 1912**.
- Trap: "Nasik Jackson murder is the same as Chittagong." Nasik is **1909 Maharashtra**; Chittagong is **1930 Bengal**.

> **Exam note:** **Abhinav Bharat = Savarkar**; **India House = Shyamji Krishna Varma**; **Dhingra = Curzon Wyllie 1909**.

## 11.9 Ghadar Party

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Ghadar Party (1913)** | Diaspora revolutionary party of Indians in North America aiming to overthrow British rule by armed revolt |
| **Ghadar newspaper** | Mouthpiece calling for revolt (*ghadar* = rebellion) |
| **Komagata Maru (1914)** | Japanese ship carrying Indian immigrants denied entry in Canada; Budge Budge firing on return intensifies anger |
| **Hindu–German Conspiracy** | WWI plots linking Indian revolutionaries with German help |
| **Berlin / Indian Independence Committee (1915)** | Wartime committee in Germany (Virendranath Chattopadhyay, Bhupendranath Dutta, Hardayal links) |

### Causes

1. Racial discrimination against Indian immigrants in Canada/USA.
2. Desire of overseas Indians to liberate the homeland by force.
3. Leadership of Lala Hardayal, Sohan Singh Bhakna, Kartar Singh Sarabha and associates.
4. WWI opportunity — Britain’s global war seemed a chance for revolt.
5. Komagata Maru humiliation radicalised diaspora opinion.

### Course — Step by Step

1. **1913:** Pacific Coast Hindustan Association / Ghadar takes shape (Astoria/San Francisco stream).
2. *Ghadar* paper spreads revolt ideology among farmers/workers overseas.
3. **1914:** Komagata Maru episode; returnees face Budge Budge violence near Calcutta.
4. Ghadarites sell businesses and sail back to spark mutiny in India.
5. February 1915 uprising plans are largely crushed by intelligence leaks.
6. **1915:** Berlin Committee organised under Zimmerman Plan support.
7. Hindu–German Conspiracy and related trials smash overseas networks (incl. San Francisco trial stream).
8. **Singapore Mutiny (1915)** shows wartime military unrest linked to the broader ferment.
9. Rash Behari Bose remains a key India-side connector of revolutionary threads; later escapes to Japan.
10. Ghadar memory survives as diaspora revolutionary inspiration.

### Ghadar Party — How It Works

- The **Ghadar Party** was organised in **1913** among Indian immigrants on the Pacific coast of North America (Astoria/San Francisco stream). Key names include **Lala Hardayal**, **Sohan Singh Bhakna**, and **Kartar Singh Sarabha**.
- Its newspaper **Ghadar** openly called for revolt against British rule. This was a diaspora party, not a Bengal samiti and not an HSRA branch.
- In **1914**, the **Komagata Maru** episode and the **Budge Budge** firing on returnees intensified anger and pushed many immigrants to sell businesses and sail home for revolt.
- During World War I, Ghadarites tried to spark mutiny inside India. The planned **February 1915** rising was largely broken by intelligence leaks and arrests.
- In **1915**, a parallel wartime centre appeared in Europe: the **Berlin / Indian Independence Committee**, associated with **Virendranath Chattopadhyay**, **Bhupendranath Dutta**, and Hardayal links under German wartime support.
- The broader wartime cluster also includes the **Hindu–German Conspiracy** trials and the **Singapore Mutiny (1915)**. **Rash Behari Bose** was an important India-side connector of these threads and later escaped to Japan.
- In the **2022 chronology PYQ**, Ghadar (**1913**) is first, then Berlin Committee (**1915**), then Assembly Bomb (**1929**), then Chittagong (**1930**).
- Ghadar’s importance for Prelims is chronological and geographical: it proves revolutionary nationalism was not Bengal-only and not limited to the 1920s HSRA generation.
- Trap: "Ghadar Party was founded in Lahore in 1929." Foundation = **1913**, North America.
- Trap: "Komagata Maru founded Ghadar." Ghadar begins in **1913**; Komagata Maru in **1914** intensifies mobilisation.
- Trap: "Berlin Committee was the headquarters of Ghadar." Berlin Committee is a **separate 1915** wartime European body.
- Trap: "Ghadar captured Delhi in 1915." The inland rising plans failed.

> **Exam note:** **Ghadar 1913 → Berlin 1915**; remember **Komagata Maru 1914**.

## 11.10 Hindustan Republican Association (HRA)

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **HRA (1924)** | Hindustan Republican Association — underground party for armed overthrow and a federal republic |
| **Mainpuri Conspiracy (1918)** | Earlier UP revolutionary case involving Bismil stream (pre-HRA continuity) |
| **Kanpur meeting (1924)** | HRA organising moment (Sanyal–Bismil) |

### Causes

1. Post-Non-Cooperation frustration among militant youth.
2. Need for an all-north-India party beyond local circles.
3. Older revolutionaries (Sachindra Nath Sanyal) provide experience.
4. Funds/arms requirement pushes treasury-raid tactics.
5. UP had an existing militant tradition (Mainpuri 1918; Shahjahanpur youth).

### Course — Step by Step

1. UP militants (Bismil et al.) carry earlier local conspiracies (Mainpuri stream).
2. **1924 Kanpur:** HRA organised; Sanyal national organiser tag; Bismil UP organiser.
3. Aim: end colonial rule; establish a **Federated Republic of the United States of India**.
4. Party publishes/circulates revolutionary literature (incl. *The Revolutionary* manifesto stream in notes).
5. **Kakori (9 Aug 1925)** becomes the signature treasury action.
6. Crackdown and Kakori Case smash open networks.
7. Hangings of Bismil–Ashfaq–Roshan–Lahiri (1927); Azad escapes.
8. Survivors later reorganise toward **HSRA (1928)** socialist turn.

### HRA — How It Works

- The **Hindustan Republican Association (HRA)** was organised in **1924**, with a key meeting at **Kanpur**. Standard notes tag **Sachindra Nath Sanyal** as national organiser and **Ram Prasad Bismil** as the chief UP organiser.
- HRA’s political aim was not Congress reform. It wanted armed overthrow of British rule and a **federated republic** in India.
- Before HRA, UP already had a militant stream: the **Mainpuri Conspiracy (1918)** linked to Bismil’s earlier revolutionary work. That is why Shahjahanpur–Mainpuri–Awadh geography matters for UPPCS.
- HRA raised funds through daring actions. Its most famous operation was the **Kakori train treasury raid on 9 August 1925**.
- After Kakori, British repression smashed open HRA networks. Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan, Roshan Singh, and Rajendra Lahiri were hanged in **December 1927**; Azad escaped and kept the underground alive.
- Survivors later accepted a clearer socialist goal and reorganised as the **Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA) in 1928**. The word **Socialist** marks that later phase.
- Do not confuse the **HRA Kanpur meeting (1924)** with the **Kanpur Conspiracy Case (1924)**. The conspiracy case was an early **communist** trial (Muzaffar Ahmed, Shaukat Usmani, Nalini Gupta, S.A. Dange stream). **M.A. Ansari was not associated** with that communist case.
- For Prelims matching, HRA = **1924 organisation + 1925 Kakori + UP geography**; HSRA = **1928 socialist turn + Saunders/Assembly**.
- Trap: "Kakori was an HSRA action." In **1925** the party name in use was **HRA**; HSRA naming comes in **1928**.
- Trap: "HRA was founded by Bhagat Singh in 1929." Bhagat Singh becomes central in the **HSRA** phase after 1928; HRA’s 1924 organisers are Sanyal–Bismil stream.
- Trap: "Mainpuri Conspiracy is a Bengal Anushilan case." Mainpuri is an **UP** revolutionary episode in Bismil’s early career.

> **Exam note:** **HRA 1924 + Kakori 1925**; Mainpuri as UP prelude.

## 11.11 Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA)

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **HSRA (1928)** | Reorganised party adding **socialist** goal to republican armed struggle |
| **Feroz Shah Kotla meeting (Sep 1928)** | Delhi meeting associated with HRA→HSRA turn |
| **Saunders murder (17 Dec 1928)** | HSRA killing of Saunders in Lahore to avenge Lala Lajpat Rai’s death |
| **Lahore Conspiracy Case** | Trial of Bhagat Singh–Sukhdev–Rajguru stream |
| **Naujawan Bharat Sabha (1926)** | Youth organisation of Bhagat Singh’s circle |

### Causes of HSRA turn

1. Kakori smash-up forced reorganisation.
2. Socialist/communist ideas influenced younger cadre after mid-1920s.
3. Death of Lala Lajpat Rai (after Simon Commission lathi-charge) created revenge politics.
4. Need for clearer socio-economic goal beyond pure republicanism.
5. Bhagat Singh–Azad leadership wanted propaganda actions with mass political meaning.

### Course — Step by Step

1. **1926:** Naujawan Bharat Sabha formed (Bhagat Singh stream).
2. **1928:** Feroz Shah Kotla reorganisation; name/aim becomes **HSRA**.
3. **30 Oct 1928:** Lajpat Rai injured in Simon protest; dies later — revolutionaries blame police officers.
4. **17 Dec 1928:** Saunders killed in Lahore (Bhagat Singh, Rajguru, Azad involvement stream).
5. **8 Apr 1929:** Assembly Bomb by Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt — “to make the deaf hear.”
6. Arrests; Lahore Conspiracy Case; hunger strikes (Jatin Das dies after 63 days, 1929).
7. **23 Mar 1931:** Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, Rajguru hanged.
8. **27 Feb 1931:** Chandrashekhar Azad dies in encounter at Alfred Park, Allahabad.

### HSRA — How It Works

- After Kakori destroyed open HRA networks, remaining revolutionaries reorganised in **1928**. At the **Feroz Shah Kotla meeting in Delhi (September 1928)**, the party took the socialist turn and became the **Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA)**.
- HSRA kept armed struggle but added a clearer social goal: end colonial rule and build a **socialist republic**, not only a political republic.
- **Naujawan Bharat Sabha (1926)** worked as a youth front around Bhagat Singh’s circle. Early associates include Bhagat Singh, Yashpal, and Chhabil Das. **Ambika Chakravarti was not an early member**; she belongs to Chittagong — that is the **2021 Q108** trap.
- On **30 October 1928**, Lala Lajpat Rai was injured in a lathi-charge during the Simon Commission protest at Lahore and later died. Revolutionaries held police officers responsible.
- On **17 December 1928**, HSRA cadres killed **J.P. Saunders** in Lahore (Bhagat Singh–Rajguru–Azad stream). This was revenge politics, not a random murder.
- On **8 April 1929**, **Bhagat Singh** and **Batukeshwar Dutt** threw bombs in the **Central Legislative Assembly** and scattered leaflets saying they wanted "to make the deaf hear." The act was designed as political propaganda against repressive bills.
- The **Lahore Conspiracy Case** followed. **Jatin Das** died in September **1929** after a long hunger strike in Lahore Jail demanding better treatment for political prisoners.
- **Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru** were hanged on **23 March 1931**. **Chandrashekhar Azad** died earlier in an encounter at **Alfred Park, Allahabad, on 27 February 1931**.
- UPPCS 2022 used the odd wording "Hindustan Socialist Democratic Union" and keyed **Yatindranath, Ajay Ghosh, Phanindranath Ghosh**. In real history notes, teach the organisation as **HSRA (1928)**.
- Trap: "HSRA was founded in 1924 or carried out Kakori." **1924/1925 = HRA**; **1928 onward = HSRA**.
- Trap: "HSRA led the Chittagong Armoury Raid." Chittagong (**1930**) was **Surya Sen’s** Bengal group.
- Trap: "Azad was hanged with Bhagat Singh on 23 March 1931." Azad died in the **Allahabad encounter on 27 February 1931**.

## 11.12 Kakori Conspiracy (1925) — UP Focus

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Kakori Conspiracy / Train Action** | 9 Aug 1925 HRA raid on government treasury on a train near Kakori (Lucknow) |
| **Kakori Case** | Trial hanging Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan, Roshan Singh, Rajendra Lahiri |

### Causes

1. HRA needed money for arms and underground work.
2. Train treasury was a practical and symbolic colonial target.
3. UP network around Bismil was strong enough for a major action.
4. Post-1922 militant youth sought dramatic blows against the Raj.
5. Party wanted to prove capacity after 1924 organisation.

### Course of Kakori — Step by Step

1. After HRA’s organisation in **1924**, Bismil’s UP circle plans a treasury action to finance arms and underground work.
2. The chosen target is government cash carried on the **No. 8 Down Saharanpur–Lucknow passenger train**.
3. On **9 August 1925**, HRA men pull the chain near **Kakori** (before Lucknow), overpower the guard, and seize treasury bags.
4. In the confusion and crossfire, a passenger named **Ahmad Ali** is killed; British law treats the case as far more than simple robbery.
5. Within weeks, colonial police launch a massive hunt across Shahjahanpur, Lucknow, and linked centres; many HRA members are arrested.
6. The **Kakori Conspiracy Case** becomes a long show trial meant to smash the UP revolutionary network.
7. Death sentences are confirmed for **Ram Prasad Bismil**, **Ashfaqulla Khan**, **Thakur Roshan Singh**, and **Rajendra Lahiri**; others get long imprisonments.
8. In **December 1927**, the four are hanged at different jails (**Lahiri at Gonda on 17 Dec**; **Bismil at Gorakhpur**, **Ashfaq at Faizabad**, and **Roshan Singh at Naini** on **19 Dec** in the standard cluster). **Chandrashekhar Azad** escapes and later rebuilds toward **HSRA in 1928**.

### Kakori — How It Works

- The Kakori action took place on **9 August 1925** near **Kakori**, a station on the Saharanpur–Lucknow line just before Lucknow in the United Provinces.
- The organisation behind it was the **Hindustan Republican Association (HRA)**, formed in **1924**. The later name **HSRA** appears only after the **1928** socialist reorganisation, so Kakori must not be labelled HSRA.
- HRA cadres stopped the **No. 8 Down Saharanpur–Lucknow** train to seize **government treasury bags** carried for deposit, because the party needed money for arms and underground work.
- Key planners and participants included **Ram Prasad Bismil**, **Ashfaqulla Khan**, **Rajendra Lahiri**, **Chandrashekhar Azad**, **Manmath Nath Gupta**, and other HRA members. A passenger (**Ahmad Ali**) was killed in the crossfire, which made British courts treat the case as far more than robbery.
- **Ram Prasad Bismil** was born on **11 June 1897 at Shahjahanpur (UP)**. After the Kakori trial he was hanged on **19 December 1927 in Gorakhpur Jail**, not in Shahjahanpur. He is also remembered for patriotic poetry such as *Sarfaroshi ki Tamanna*.
- **Ashfaqulla Khan** was Bismil’s closest comrade in the raid and remains the classic UP example of Hindu–Muslim revolutionary partnership; he was hanged at **Faizabad Jail** on **19 December 1927**.
- **Rajendra Lahiri** was hanged at **Gonda on 17 December 1927**. **Thakur Roshan Singh** was hanged at **Naini (Allahabad) on 19 December 1927**. These four hangings form the Kakori martyr cluster.
- **Chandrashekhar Azad** took part in the wider HRA action network but **escaped the hangman’s rope** after Kakori and later rebuilt the movement toward **HSRA in 1928**. He died in an encounter at **Alfred Park, Allahabad, on 27 February 1931**.
- For UPPCS geography, lock the chain: **birth Shahjahanpur → action Kakori/Lucknow → hangings Gorakhpur/Faizabad/Gonda/Naini → Azad’s end at Allahabad**.
- Trap: "Kakori was led by Surya Sen or done by HSRA." Surya Sen belongs to **Chittagong 1930**; HSRA as a name belongs to **1928**, after Kakori.
- Trap: "Bismil was born in Gorakhpur or hanged in Shahjahanpur." Birthplace = **Shahjahanpur**; hanging = **Gorakhpur Jail, 19 Dec 1927**.
- Trap: "Azad was hanged in the Kakori case." He remained underground and died later at **Alfred Park, Allahabad, on 27 February 1931**.
- Trap: "Kakori happened in 1930 with Chittagong." Kakori is **1925**; Chittagong is **1930**.

> **Exam note:** Kakori = **HRA + 1925 + Lucknow belt + Bismil–Ashfaq**.

## 11.13 Chittagong Armoury Raid (1930)

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Chittagong Armoury Raid (18 Apr 1930)** | Surya Sen’s raid to seize arms and spark revolt |
| **Indian Republican Army (Chittagong)** | Local force under Surya Sen |

### Causes

1. Seize colonial arms and cripple communications.
2. Bengal revolutionary tradition after Anushilan/Jugantar phase.
3. Hope a local rising would spread.
4. Youth radicalisation after late-1920s repression.
5. Surya Sen’s organisational leadership in Chittagong.

### Course — Step by Step

1. Surya Sen organises young cadres (incl. women revolutionaries).
2. **18 Apr 1930:** Armoury/police facilities raided.
3. Telegraph/telephone and railway links targeted.
4. Temporary proclamation/flag-raising; fighters disperse under counter-attack.
5. Guerrilla resistance and manhunt continue in hills/rural bases.
6. Pritilata Waddedar’s European club attack becomes a famous follow-on episode.
7. Captures/trials over subsequent years; **Surya Sen hanged 1934**.
8. Ambika Chakrabarty’s Chittagong link explains why she is not Naujawan Sabha early member.

### Chittagong — How It Works

- The **Chittagong Armoury Raid** was carried out on **18 April 1930** in Chittagong (then Bengal Presidency) under **Surya Sen**, popularly called **Master-da**.
- The aim was military, not treasury theft: seize police/auxiliary **armoury weapons**, cut **telephone/telegraph**, and dislocate **railway links** so a local rising could begin.
- Surya Sen’s force is remembered as the **Indian Republican Army, Chittagong Branch**. Young cadres included **Ganesh Ghosh**, **Ambika Chakrabarty**, **Kalpana Dutt**, and **Pritilata Waddedar**.
- After the initial raid, fighters could not hold the town against British counter-attack. They shifted to hills and rural bases for guerrilla resistance and a long manhunt.
- **Pritilata Waddedar** later led a famous attack on the European Club at Chittagong (1932 stream in standard notes) and died after the action; **Kalpana Dutt** was captured and became a lasting woman-revolutionary symbol.
- **Surya Sen** was eventually captured and **hanged in 1934**. That closes the raid’s leadership story four years after the armoury attack.
- In chronology drills, Chittagong is the **last** of the 2022 four-event set: Ghadar **1913** → Berlin Committee **1915** → Assembly Bomb **1929** → Chittagong **1930**.
- Chittagong is a Bengal action under Surya Sen’s local group. It is not HRA/HSRA and not a UP event, even though it sits next to Kakori in many revision lists.
- Trap: "Chittagong was led by Bhagat Singh." Bhagat Singh’s famous 1929 act is the **Central Assembly Bomb** and the Lahore Conspiracy Case, not Chittagong.
- Trap: "Chittagong is just Kakori copied in Bengal." Kakori (**1925**, HRA) targeted a **train treasury** in UP; Chittagong (**1930**, Surya Sen) targeted an **armoury** in Bengal.
- Trap: "Chittagong came before Ghadar." The order is the reverse: Ghadar is **1913**, Chittagong is **1930**.
- Trap: "Ambika Chakravarti was an early Naujawan Sabha member." She belongs to the **Chittagong** stream; UPPCS 2021 excludes her from early Naujawan membership.

> **Exam note:** **Chittagong 1930 = Surya Sen**; after Assembly Bomb.

## 11.14 Key Revolutionary Events (detail blocks)

### 11.14.1 Muzaffarpur Bomb Attempt & Alipore Bomb Case (1908–09)

#### Causes
1. Kingsford (judge) was hated for harsh sentences against nationalists.
2. Jugantar/Anushilan youth sought spectacular assassination.
3. Swadeshi repression pushed militants underground.
4. Manicktolla provided bomb-making capacity.

#### Course — Step by Step
1. Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki are sent to kill Kingsford at Muzaffarpur.
2. **30 Apr 1908:** Bomb hits wrong carriage; two British women killed.
3. Prafulla Chaki shoots himself after arrest attempt; Khudiram is captured.
4. Khudiram is tried and hanged (Aug 1908) — becomes a youthful martyr icon.
5. Police uncover Manicktolla garden house; mass arrests follow.
6. **Alipore Bomb Case** tries Aurobindo, Barindra and associates.
7. Narendranath Gosain turns approver and is killed in jail by Kanailal Dutt/Satyendranath Bose stream (famous jail episode).
8. Aurobindo is defended by C.R. Das and acquitted; Barindra and others receive sentences.

#### How It Works
- On **30 April 1908**, **Khudiram Bose** and **Prafulla Chaki** threw a bomb at Muzaffarpur intending to kill Judge **Kingsford**, who was hated for harsh sentences against nationalists.
- The bomb hit the wrong carriage and killed two British women. Prafulla Chaki later shot himself to avoid capture; Khudiram was tried and **hanged in August 1908**, becoming a youthful martyr icon.
- Police investigation uncovered the **Manicktolla garden house** in Calcutta, the bomb workshop of Barindra’s circle, and produced the **Alipore Bomb Case (1908–09)**.
- **Aurobindo Ghosh** and **Barindra Kumar Ghosh** were among those tried. Approver **Narendranath Gosain** was killed in jail by revolutionary inmates, a famous episode inside the case.
- **C.R. Das** defended Aurobindo, who was **acquitted**. Barindra and several others received sentences. The trial crushed the open Manicktolla centre and drove survivors deeper underground.
- Muzaffarpur–Alipore belongs to the early Bengal revolutionary wave after Swadeshi. It is chronologically far earlier than Kakori (**1925**) or Chittagong (**1930**).
- The case also hardened British repression in Bengal and pushed surviving cadres deeper into secret work, feeding later Anushilan/Jugantar activity.
- For matching drills, lock **Muzaffarpur 1908 = Khudiram–Chaki vs Kingsford**, then **Alipore = Manicktolla trial of Aurobindo–Barindra**.
- Trap: "Aurobindo was hanged in the Alipore Case." He was **acquitted**.
- Trap: "Khudiram Bose killed Lord Hardinge." Khudiram’s case is **Muzaffarpur 1908**; Hardinge was bombed in **Delhi on 23 December 1912**.
- Trap: "Alipore Case is the same as Kakori Case." Alipore is Bengal **1908–09**; Kakori is UP **1925**.

### 11.14.2 Hardinge Bomb / Delhi Conspiracy (1912)

#### Causes
1. Revolutionaries wanted a spectacular blow at the highest colonial office.
2. Delhi’s new-capital ceremonial entry created a public procession target.
3. Anushilan networks and north-Indian contacts could supply men and bombs.
4. Swadeshi-era repression had already pushed militants underground.

#### Course — Step by Step
1. **Rash Behari Bose** organises the Delhi Conspiracy using Anushilan-linked networks.
2. **23 December 1912:** Viceroy **Lord Hardinge** enters Delhi in a ceremonial procession.
3. **Basanta Kumar Biswas** throws a bomb at Hardinge’s elephant howdah.
4. Hardinge is injured; the attendant behind him is killed in the blast.
5. A wide manhunt and conspiracy case follow across north India and Bengal links.
6. Several revolutionaries are arrested and tried; Bose later escapes abroad.
7. The case becomes a permanent org-matching favourite in UPPCS.
8. It sits chronologically after India House/Nasik (**1909**) and before Ghadar’s wartime peak (**1914–15**).

#### How It Works
- The Hardinge bomb is also called the **Delhi Conspiracy Case**. On **23 December 1912**, Viceroy **Lord Hardinge** was attacked during a ceremonial elephant procession marking Delhi’s new-capital politics after **1911**.
- The organiser associated with the plot is **Rash Behari Bose**. The thrower associated with the bomb is **Basanta Kumar Biswas**. Hardinge was injured; an attendant behind him was killed.
- UPPCS asks the responsible organisation and keys **Anushilan Samiti** (**2023 Q42**). **Yugantar** is the common wrong option because both belong to the Bengal revolutionary world.
- This attack sits after India House/Nasik (**1909**) and before Ghadar’s wartime peak (**1914–15**). It is not an HSRA action; HSRA appears only in **1928**.
- The conspiracy case produced a wide manhunt across north India and Bengal links. Several revolutionaries were arrested and tried; Bose later escaped abroad and remained active in overseas revolt politics.
- Do not confuse this Delhi Viceroy attack with **Madanlal Dhingra’s** London assassination of **Curzon Wyllie (1909)** or with the **Central Assembly Bomb (8 April 1929)** by Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt.
- The case matters for Prelims because it locks three tags together: **date 1912 + organiser Rash Behari Bose + organisation Anushilan Samiti**.
- New-capital ceremonial politics after the **1911 Durbar** made Hardinge’s Delhi procession a high-prestige target; that is why the attack is remembered as a blow at imperial authority in the new capital.
- Trap: "Hardinge bomb was thrown by Madanlal Dhingra." Dhingra killed **Curzon Wyllie in London in 1909**.
- Trap: "Hardinge bomb = Assembly Bomb." Assembly Bomb is **8 April 1929** by Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt.
- Trap: "Hardinge bomb was done by HSRA." HSRA is a **1928** north-Indian socialist reorganisation, sixteen years later.

## Common Traps — Don't Fall For These

1. **Trap:** Partition of Bengal in 1911. **Correct:** Partition **1905**; annulment **1911**.

2. **Trap:** Lucknow Pact in 1911. **Correct:** **1916** — **2025 Q6**.

3. **Trap:** Curzon = Doctrine of Lapse. **Correct:** Curzon = **Partition of Bengal** — **2025 Q40**.

4. **Trap:** Extremists = only bomb throwers. **Correct:** Extremists = assertive Swadeshi/boycott politics; revolutionaries are a distinct underground stream.

5. **Trap:** Hardinge bomb by HSRA/Yugantar (as UPPCS key). **Correct:** **Anushilan Samiti** — **2023 Q42**.

6. **Trap:** Abhinav Bharat by Bhagat Singh/Barindra. **Correct:** **V.D. Savarkar** — **2018 Q22**.

7. **Trap:** Kakori by HSRA. **Correct:** **HRA (1925)**; HSRA name **1928**.

8. **Trap:** Bismil born Gorakhpur. **Correct:** Born **Shahjahanpur**; hanged **Gorakhpur**.

9. **Trap:** Chittagong by Bhagat Singh. **Correct:** **Surya Sen (1930)**.

10. **Trap:** Ghadar Party after Assembly Bomb. **Correct:** Ghadar **1913** first in 2022 chronology.

11. **Trap:** Ansari in Kanpur Conspiracy 1924. **Correct:** **NOT associated** — **2021 Q86**.

12. **Trap:** Ambika Chakravarti early Naujawan Sabha member. **Correct:** **NOT** — **2021 Q108**.

13. **Trap:** HRA and HSRA are identical names/years. **Correct:** HRA **1924**; HSRA **1928** socialist turn.

14. **Trap:** Berlin Committee = Ghadar headquarters. **Correct:** Berlin Committee **1915** wartime Europe; Ghadar = North America **1913**.
