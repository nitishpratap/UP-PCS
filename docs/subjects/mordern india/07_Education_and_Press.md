## 7.1 Development of Education — Overview

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Colonial education policy** | British decisions on what Indians should learn, in which language, and through which institutions |
| **Downward filtration** | Idea that educating a small elite in English would filter modern knowledge down to the masses |
| **National education** | Indian-controlled schools/colleges that rejected purely colonial curricula during Swadeshi and after |

### Causes — Why British Education Policy Took Shape

1. The Company needed clerks, translators, and lower officials who could work in English administration.
2. Missionaries and Evangelicals wanted Western education to "civilise" and convert.
3. Orientalists wanted to preserve Sanskrit/Arabic learning; Anglicists wanted English science and literature.
4. After Charter Act 1813, a yearly education grant forced the government to choose a policy direction.
5. Later, Indian nationalists demanded education that built character, vernacular strength, and self-rule.

### Development of Education — How It Works

- British education in India did not begin as a mass literacy mission. It began as a tool of **governance and cultural influence**.
- Early Oriental colleges (Calcutta Madrasa, Banaras Sanskrit College) served classical learning and Company needs for local law and languages.
- The **Charter Act of 1813** set aside **£1 lakh** a year for education. That money started the Orientalist–Anglicist fight over how to spend it.
- **Macaulay's Minute (1835)** tilted policy toward **English** as the medium of higher education and created an English-educated Indian middle class.
- **Wood's Dispatch (1854)** then tried to build a full ladder: vernacular primary schools → Anglo-vernacular secondary schools → colleges → universities.
- Later commissions corrected gaps: **Hunter (1882)** pushed primary education; **Sadler (1917–19)** looked at university problems; **Sargent (1944)** sketched a national post-war system.
- Indians also built parallel streams: mission schools, reformer schools (Phule, Bethune), Aligarh, DAV, Banaras Hindu University currents, and Swadeshi **national education** institutions.
- For Prelims, treat education as a **timeline of documents**, not as vague praise of "spread of education."
- Trap: "Wood's Dispatch created English education for the first time." English policy victory came with **Macaulay 1835**; Wood built the system in **1854**.
- Trap: "Hunter Commission came before Wood's Dispatch." Correct order is Wood **1854** then Hunter **1882**.

> **Exam note:** Master the chain **1813 grant → 1835 Macaulay → 1854 Wood → 1882 Hunter → 1904 Universities Act → 1917 Sadler → 1937 Wardha → 1944 Sargent**.

### Results of Colonial Education

| Result | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| English-educated middle class | Fuel for nationalism, press, and professions |
| Neglect of mass vernacular primary education | Long literacy lag; Hunter tried to correct this |
| Universities at Calcutta, Bombay, Madras (1857) | Higher education institutionalised |
| National education experiments | Swadeshi-era alternative to colonial colleges |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- English tilt: **Macaulay 1835**
- System blueprint: **Wood 1854**
- Primary push: **Hunter 1882**
- Chronology PYQ: Wood → Hunter → Sadler → Sargent

## 7.2 Orientalist–Anglicist Controversy

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Orientalists** | Officials/scholars who favoured Sanskrit, Arabic, and Persian learning |
| **Anglicists** | Officials who favoured English education and Western sciences |
| **Downward filtration theory** | Educate a few in English; knowledge will trickle down to the many |

### Causes

1. Charter Act 1813 created an education fund without clear language policy.
2. Company needed both classical experts for law/revenue and English clerks for offices.
3. Missionaries pushed Western education; Oriental scholars feared cultural destruction.
4. Bentinck's reform climate made a decisive policy choice possible.

### Course — Step by Step

1. Oriental institutions such as Calcutta Madrasa (1781) and Banaras Sanskrit College (1791) represented the early Orientalist line.
2. Anglicists argued that Western literature and science were more useful for modern administration.
3. The General Committee of Public Instruction split between the two camps.
4. **Macaulay** arrived as Law Member and wrote the decisive **Minute on Education (1835)**.
5. Bentinck accepted the Anglicist line; English became the preferred medium of higher education.
6. Oriental learning was not wiped out overnight, but funding priority shifted toward English education.
7. The English-educated class later became the backbone of the modern press and nationalist politics.
8. Wood's Dispatch later softened pure filtration by recommending vernacular primary schooling under an English-led system.

### Orientalist–Anglicist Controversy — How It Works

- This was a fight over **what kind of Indian mind the Company wanted to produce**.
- Orientalists believed classical Indian languages preserved law, religion, and useful local knowledge for governance.
- Anglicists believed English opened science, modern ideas, and cheap trained manpower for the empire.
- **Downward filtration** meant the state would not immediately educate the masses. It would educate a thin elite and hope ideas would spread socially.
- Macaulay's rhetoric claimed a single shelf of European books outweighed Indian classical libraries. That argument won policy, not because it was culturally fair, but because it fitted administrative needs.
- The controversy matters because every later document (Wood, Hunter, national education) is a reaction to this English-elite model.
- Trap: "Orientalists wanted only missionary education." Missionaries usually sided with Anglicists/Western education.
- Trap: "Anglicists abolished all Sanskrit colleges in 1835." Priority shifted; classical institutions did not vanish instantly.
- Trap: "Filtration theory = mass primary education." It is the opposite idea: elite first.

> **Exam note:** Anglicist victory = **Macaulay Minute 1835**; mass vernacular correction attempt = **Wood 1854 / Hunter 1882**.

### Results

| Result | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| English as higher-education medium | Created bilingual professional class |
| Cultural alienation debate | Later fuel for national education |
| Need for vernacular primary layer | Wood and Hunter respond |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Debate decade: **1820s–1830s**
- Decision year: **1835**
- Winner: **Anglicists**
- Theory tag: **Downward filtration**
- Linked GG: **Bentinck**

## 7.3 Macaulay's Minute (1835)

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Macaulay's Minute on Education (2 Feb 1835)** | Official note arguing that government funds should promote English education |
| **Thomas Babington Macaulay** | Law Member of the Governor-General's Council; author of the Minute |
| **English education policy** | Preferential use of English for higher learning and public employment pathways |

### Causes

1. The Orientalist–Anglicist deadlock blocked clear use of the 1813 education grant.
2. The Company needed a class of Indians who could serve as interpreters between rulers and ruled.
3. Bentinck's government was ready for decisive administrative reform.
4. Macaulay personally believed Western knowledge was superior for modern progress.

### Course — Step by Step

1. Macaulay joined the Council as **Law Member in 1834**.
2. On **2 February 1835** he submitted the famous Minute attacking large spending on classical Oriental learning.
3. He argued that English literature and science should be the core of higher education.
4. **Lord William Bentinck** accepted the Minute and shifted policy toward English education.
5. English became the language of higher courts and of the educated public career path.
6. A new English-educated Indian middle class began to grow in Presidency towns.
7. Macaulay also worked on legal codification; the IPC was enacted later (**1860**).
8. The Minute remained the philosophical base until Wood's Dispatch expanded the institutional ladder in 1854.

### Macaulay's Minute — How It Works

- The Minute is not a school Act. It is a **policy argument** that decided the language and purpose of higher education.
- Macaulay wanted Indians who were "Indian in blood and colour, but English in tastes, opinions, morals and intellect." That line summarises the colonial cultural aim.
- Money and prestige moved toward English colleges and away from exclusive focus on Sanskrit/Arabic colleges.
- The policy created clerks, lawyers, teachers, and journalists who later used the same English skills against colonial rule.
- Do not confuse the Minute with Wood's Dispatch. Macaulay decided the **direction**; Wood designed the **system**.
- Bentinck's acceptance made 1835 the turning-year in almost every education chronology.
- Trap: "Macaulay wrote Wood's Dispatch." Wood's Dispatch is **1854**, associated with Charles Wood.
- Trap: "Macaulay Minute = primary education for all." It favoured elite English education, not mass vernacular schooling.
- Trap: "Macaulay founded Asiatic Society." Asiatic Society = **William Jones, 1784**.

> **Exam note:** Macaulay = **1835 + English + Bentinck**; Wood = **1854 + system blueprint**.

### Results

| Result | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| English higher education | Created modern professional class |
| Cultural dualism | Later nationalist critique and national education |
| Continuity into Wood era | System built on Anglicist foundation |

### Key Persons

| Person | Role |
|--------|------|
| **T.B. Macaulay** | Author of the Minute |
| **Lord William Bentinck** | Accepted English education policy |
| **Orientalist opponents** | Defended classical learning |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Year: **1835**
- Date often cited: **2 February 1835**
- GG: **Bentinck**
- Core choice: **English**
- Not the same as Wood **1854**

## 7.4 Wood's Dispatch (1854)

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Wood's Dispatch (1854)** | Comprehensive education despatch by Sir Charles Wood; called the **Magna Carta of English education in India** |
| **Education ladder** | Vernacular primary → secondary → colleges → universities |
| **Grants-in-aid** | Government aid to private/mission schools that met standards |

### Causes

1. After Macaulay, English higher education grew, but mass schooling remained weak.
2. Dalhousie's reforming government wanted a clear all-India education design.
3. Officials needed a system linking vernacular schools to colleges and jobs.
4. Private and missionary schools required a rules-based grant system.

### Course — Step by Step

1. **Sir Charles Wood**, President of the Board of Control, issued the Dispatch in **1854**.
2. It recommended a graded system from primary vernacular schools to universities.
3. It favoured English for higher education and vernacular languages for primary education.
4. It proposed **grants-in-aid** to private bodies running good schools.
5. It recommended teacher training, vocational/professional education, and education of women.
6. Following the Dispatch, universities were founded at **Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras in 1857**.
7. Departments of Public Instruction were organised in provinces to administer the system.
8. Later critics said the system still over-produced clerks and under-served villages; Hunter later focused on primary education.

### Wood's Dispatch — How It Works

- Think of Wood's Dispatch as the **blueprint of colonial school-college architecture**, not as a single classroom reform.
- It accepted Macaulay's English higher education but tried to repair the missing vernacular base.
- **Grants-in-aid** made the state a partner of missions and private societies instead of the sole school-builder.
- The 1857 Presidency universities were examining and affiliating bodies more than teaching-residential universities in the modern sense.
- Exams call it the **Magna Carta of English education in India** because it first laid down a full official system.
- In chronology questions it is the first item among Wood–Hunter–Sadler–Sargent.
- Trap: "Wood's Dispatch = 1835." That year is Macaulay.
- Trap: "Wood created universities in 1854 itself." Universities followed in **1857**.
- Trap: "Wood abandoned English education." It kept English at the top and vernaculars at the base.

> **Exam note:** Wood **1854** = Magna Carta; universities **1857**; next major commission = Hunter **1882**.

### Results

| Result | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| Systematic education hierarchy | Model for later provincial systems |
| Universities 1857 | Higher education institutionalised |
| Grants-in-aid culture | Private/mission expansion |
| Persistent primary weakness | Sets up Hunter agenda |

### Key Persons

| Person | Role |
|--------|------|
| **Sir Charles Wood** | Author of the Dispatch |
| **Lord Dalhousie** | Governor-General of the era |
| **Provincial DPI officers** | Implemented school administration |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Year: **1854**
- Nickname: **Magna Carta of English education**
- Universities: **1857** (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras)
- Languages: English higher + vernacular primary
- Chronology position: **first** in 2018 Q21 set

## 7.5 Hunter Commission (1882)

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Hunter Commission (1882)** | Education commission under W.W. Hunter to review Wood's system |
| **Primary education focus** | Commission stressed expansion and improvement of elementary schooling |
| **Ripon era education** | Local self-government climate also favoured local responsibility for schools |

### Causes

1. After Wood, secondary and collegiate education grew faster than village primary schooling.
2. There was complaint that education produced clerks, not citizens with basic literacy.
3. Missionary and private agencies wanted clearer state policy on aid and control.
4. Ripon's liberal administrative climate encouraged review and decentralisation.

### Course — Step by Step

1. Lord Ripon's government appointed the commission under **Sir William Hunter in 1882**.
2. The commission toured and examined primary, secondary, and collegiate conditions.
3. It recommended that **primary education** be the state's special responsibility priority.
4. It favoured transfer of primary control toward local boards where possible.
5. It supported continued private effort through grants-in-aid with less harassment.
6. Secondary education was to be improved but not at the cost of elementary neglect.
7. Recommendations influenced provincial education policy through the 1880s–90s.
8. Later university problems remained; those were taken up more directly by Curzon-era reforms and Sadler.

### Hunter Commission — How It Works

- Hunter is best remembered as the commission that said: **stop ignoring the primary base**.
- Wood had drawn the ladder; Hunter said the bottom rung was still weak.
- Local boards were expected to carry elementary schooling closer to villages.
- Private schools remained important; the state was to help and inspect, not monopolise every school.
- In Prelims chronology, Hunter sits between Wood (1854) and Sadler (1917–19).
- Do not mix Hunter with Hartog (1929) or Sargent (1944); those are later reviews.
- Trap: "Hunter Commission = 1854." That is Wood.
- Trap: "Hunter created the first universities." Universities of 1857 followed Wood, before Hunter.
- Trap: "Hunter = only university reform." Its signature emphasis is **primary education**.

> **Exam note:** Hunter **1882** = primary education; pair with Ripon era locally.

### Results

| Result | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| Primary education priority | Policy correction after Wood |
| Local board role | Links education to local government |
| Continued private schooling | Grants-in-aid confirmed |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Year: **1882**
- Chairman: **W.W. Hunter**
- Focus: **primary education**
- GG/Viceroy climate: **Ripon**
- Chronology: after Wood, before Sadler

## 7.6 Indian Universities Act (1904)

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Indian Universities Act, 1904** | Curzon-era law tightening government control over universities and affiliated colleges |
| **Raleigh Commission (1902)** | Commission on university reform whose ideas fed into the 1904 Act |
| **Affiliation control** | Power to police standards of colleges attached to universities |

### Causes

1. By 1900, many affiliated colleges were academically weak and poorly staffed.
2. Curzon wanted "efficiency" and central control over higher education.
3. Officials feared political radicalism among students and teachers.
4. The Raleigh Commission recommended structural tightening of universities.

### Course — Step by Step

1. Curzon appointed the **University Commission (Raleigh) in 1902**.
2. The commission criticised low standards and loose affiliation.
3. The **Indian Universities Act 1904** reconstituted university senates and increased official control.
4. Universities got stronger powers over affiliated colleges' standards.
5. Government influence in university bodies rose sharply.
6. Nationalists attacked the Act as an attempt to **officialise and gag** higher education.
7. The controversy fed the wider Swadeshi mood after 1905 and the demand for national education.
8. Later Sadler Commission revisited university problems, especially Calcutta's.

### Indian Universities Act — How It Works

- The Act is less about founding new universities and more about **controlling existing ones**.
- Curzon's language was efficiency and standards; nationalist reading was political distrust of educated Indians.
- Stricter affiliation meant weak colleges could be disciplined or shut out.
- Larger official presence in university governance reduced autonomous academic space.
- This is why 1904 belongs in both education history and the politics of Curzon's viceroyalty.
- Link forward: anger against official control helped the **National Education Movement**.
- Trap: "Universities Act founded Calcutta University." Calcutta University dates to **1857**.
- Trap: "Raleigh Commission = Hunter Commission." Raleigh is **1902**; Hunter is **1882**.
- Trap: "1904 Act = Wardha Scheme." Wardha is Gandhi's **1937** Basic Education.

> **Exam note:** 1904 Act = **Curzon + control + Raleigh backdrop**; political fallout → national education.

### Results

| Result | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| Tighter university control | Official dominance in higher education |
| Nationalist resentment | Push toward national institutions |
| Standards rhetoric | Mixed academic and political motives |

### Key Persons

| Person | Role |
|--------|------|
| **Lord Curzon** | Viceroy pushing university reform/control |
| **Sir Thomas Raleigh** | University Commission chairman |
| **Nationalist critics** | Saw Act as educational repression |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Act year: **1904**
- Commission: **Raleigh 1902**
- Viceroy: **Curzon**
- Theme: **control / standards / affiliation**
- Political link: **National Education Movement**

## 7.7 Sadler, Hartog, Sargent — Later Reviews

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Sadler Commission (1917–19)** | Calcutta University Commission; reviewed higher education structure |
| **Hartog Committee (1929)** | Reviewed education quality/wastage under colonial system |
| **Sargent Plan (1944)** | Post-war national education plan by John Sargent |

### Causes

1. University expansion created new problems of standards, intermediate education, and governance.
2. Calcutta University's size and politics made a special inquiry necessary (Sadler).
3. By the late 1920s, wastage and stagnation in schools worried policymakers (Hartog).
4. World War II's end required a reconstruction plan for national education (Sargent).

### Course — Step by Step

1. **Sadler Commission (1917–19)** studied Calcutta University and recommended separating intermediate education from university proper and strengthening teaching universities.
2. Its ideas influenced university reorganisation beyond Bengal.
3. **Hartog Committee (1929)** criticised wastage in primary education and over-expansion without quality.
4. It advised consolidation and better teaching rather than mere numerical growth.
5. During WWII planning, **John Sargent** prepared a report published as the **Sargent Plan (1944)**.
6. Sargent imagined universal primary education over a long timeline and a national system after the war.
7. Independence intervened; free India later built its own policy path, but Sargent remains an exam chronology marker.
8. For UPPCS, the tested skill is often simply placing Sadler and Sargent after Wood and Hunter.

### Later Reviews — How It Works

- These later reviews do not replace Wood or Hunter; they **repair later stages** of the same colonial system.
- **Sadler** = university/higher-education redesign, especially Calcutta.
- **Hartog** = quality, wastage, and realistic expansion.
- **Sargent** = late-colonial blueprint for a national system after the war.
- Memorise the years as a chain: **1917–19 Sadler → 1929 Hartog → 1944 Sargent**.
- In the 2018 question, only Sadler and Sargent appear with Wood and Hunter; Hartog is useful extra memory.
- Trap: "Sadler Commission = 1882." That is Hunter.
- Trap: "Sargent Plan = Wardha Scheme." Wardha is Gandhi **1937**; Sargent is official **1944**.
- Trap: "Sargent came before Sadler." No — Sadler is World War I era; Sargent is World War II end.

> **Exam note:** 2018 order = **Wood 1854 → Hunter 1882 → Sadler 1917–19 → Sargent 1944**.

### Results

| Result | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| Intermediate–university separation ideas | Sadler legacy |
| Quality-over-quantity warning | Hartog legacy |
| Post-war national schooling vision | Sargent legacy |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Sadler: **1917–19**
- Hartog: **1929**
- Sargent: **1944**
- 2018 Q21 answer: **C A B D**
- Do not insert Wardha into that four-item order unless asked

### PYQs — Later Reviews

1. **(UPPCS Prelims 2018, Q21)** Chronology including Sadler and Sargent → **Wood → Hunter → Sadler → Sargent**.

2. **(UPSC pattern)** Sargent Plan year → **1944**.

| Example | What it teaches |

## 7.8 Wardha Scheme & National Education Movement

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **National Education Movement** | Swadeshi-era drive to build Indian-controlled schools and colleges outside official control |
| **National Council of Education (1906)** | Body associated with national education experiments in Bengal |
| **Wardha Scheme / Nai Talim (1937)** | Gandhi's Basic Education scheme centred on craft, mother tongue, and character |

### Causes

1. Curzon's university controls and Partition of Bengal angered students and teachers.
2. Boycott of official institutions during Swadeshi needed a positive educational alternative.
3. Nationalists wanted education in Indian languages with national history and self-respect.
4. Gandhi later rejected bookish colonial schooling and wanted learning through productive craft.

### Course — Step by Step

1. After 1905, national schools and colleges appeared, especially in Bengal.
2. The **National Council of Education (1906)** and related bodies tried to organise scientific and literary education on national lines.
3. Institutions such as Bengal National College became symbols of educational Swadeshi.
4. The movement faced money and recognition problems; many students still needed official degrees for jobs.
5. In **1937**, Congress ministries and Gandhi's circle developed the **Wardha Scheme of Basic Education**.
6. Zakir Husain committee work helped shape the Wardha syllabus around craft-centred learning.
7. Basic Education emphasised mother tongue, manual work (for example spinning), and self-sufficiency.
8. Wardha remained an influential nationalist education idea into the freedom-struggle memory, distinct from Sargent's official plan.

### Wardha & National Education — How It Works

- National Education Movement = **political rejection of official control** after Curzon/Swadeshi.
- Wardha Scheme = **pedagogic alternative** associated with Gandhi: learn by doing, through craft, in the mother tongue.
- National education was strongest where Swadeshi politics was strongest, especially Bengal.
- Wardha is not a British commission. It is an Indian nationalist education programme.
- Compare carefully with Sargent: both speak of national schooling futures, but one is Gandhi-Congress Basic Education (1937), the other is a 1944 official plan.
- Tagore's Visva-Bharati / Shantiniketan belongs to the broader Indian alternative-education imagination, even though it is not identical to Wardha craft schooling.
- Trap: "Wardha Scheme = 1904 Universities Act." Completely different.
- Trap: "National Education Movement began with Wood's Dispatch." Wood is colonial blueprint; national education is anti-colonial alternative.
- Trap: "Wardha = Sargent Plan." Different authors, years, and philosophies.

> **Exam note:** National education ↔ **Swadeshi 1905–06**; Wardha ↔ **Gandhi Basic Education 1937**.

## 7.11 Asiatic Society of Bengal

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Asiatic Society of Bengal (1784)** | Learned society at Calcutta for study of Asian languages, history, and antiquities |
| **Sir William Jones** | Founder and first President of the Asiatic Society |
| **Warren Hastings** | Governor-General in whose period the Society was founded; declined the presidency |

### Causes

1. Early Company rule needed systematic knowledge of Indian law, languages, and customs.
2. European Enlightenment scholars wanted organised study of Sanskrit and Asian civilisations.
3. Calcutta had become the political and intellectual headquarters of British India.
4. Hastings personally encouraged Oriental learning even when he refused formal leadership of the Society.

### Course — Step by Step

1. In **1784**, the Asiatic Society of Bengal was founded at Calcutta.
2. **Sir William Jones** became its moving spirit and **first President**.
3. **Warren Hastings** supported Oriental studies but **modestly declined** the offer of presidentship in favour of Jones.
4. The Society published researches on Sanskrit literature, law, history, and inscriptions.
5. It became the institutional home of early Indology and Orientalist scholarship.
6. Jones's work on Sanskrit and Indo-European connections made the Society world-famous.
7. Later scholars continued translations and antiquarian studies under the Society's umbrella.
8. UPPCS asks both the founder name and the Hastings–Jones presidency relationship.

### Asiatic Society — How It Works

- The Society is an **education-and-knowledge institution**, not a school board. It produced research that fed colonial administration and world scholarship.
- **Founder-president = William Jones.** Hastings is of the same era and encouraged Orientalism, but he is not the founder.
- Assertion–Reason questions may say: Society established in Hastings' period and he declined presidency for Jones (**true**); Hastings was an ardent orientalist (**true**). The reason explains his general scholarly sympathy, but the specific decision to decline also rested on choosing Jones as the fit president — UPPCS key treats R as **not the correct explanation** of A.
- Pair this topic with Orientalist education policy: the Society is the scholarly base from which Orientalist confidence grew.
- Trap: "Warren Hastings founded the Asiatic Society." Founder = **Jones** — **2021 Q40**.
- Trap: "Asiatic Society founded under Bentinck." Period is **Hastings, 1784**.
- Trap: "Jones declined the presidency." It was **Hastings** who declined; Jones accepted.
- Trap: "Asiatic Society = Hindu College." Different institutions in Calcutta's intellectual world.

## 7.12 Beginning of Newspapers & Press Control

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Indian press** | Newspapers and journals in English and Indian languages that shaped public opinion |
| **Vernacular Press Act (1878)** | Lytton's law restricting vernacular (Indian-language) newspapers |
| **Censorship / security deposit** | Tools used by colonial state to discipline critical editors |

### Causes

1. Print technology and urban literacy created a market for news and opinion.
2. Reformers and nationalists needed journals to debate social and political issues.
3. The colonial state feared vernacular papers that reached wider Indian audiences.
4. English papers and vernacular papers were often treated unequally under the law.

### Course — Step by Step

1. Late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries saw early colonial and missionary papers, then Indian-owned journals.
2. Raja Ram Mohan Roy and other reformers used the press for social and political argument.
3. Mid-nineteenth-century Indian papers in Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras became permanent public voices.
4. After 1857 and during later nationalist unrest, the government watched the press more closely.
5. **Lord Lytton** enacted the **Vernacular Press Act in 1878** to gag Indian-language papers.
6. English-language papers were not hit in the same way, exposing racial/political double standards.
7. **Lord Ripon** repealed the Vernacular Press Act, earning goodwill among Indian journalists.
8. Later press laws and sedition prosecutions continued into the twentieth century whenever nationalist journalism intensified.

### Beginning of Newspapers & Press Control — How It Works

- The Indian press began as a **school of modern politics**. Editors taught readers about rights, reform, famine, and later Swadeshi.
- Vernacular papers mattered because they spoke to people outside the tiny English-knowing elite.
- Lytton's 1878 Act is the classic example of **preventive censorship** aimed at Indian-language criticism.
- Amrita Bazar Patrika's famous response in textbook memory is conversion into an English paper to escape vernacular restrictions — a favourite story illustrating the Act's discriminatory design.
- Ripon's repeal shows that press freedom was also a political bargaining chip between Viceroys.
- For matching questions, separate **founder/editor**, **paper name**, and **place**.
- Trap: "Vernacular Press Act passed by Ripon." It was **Lytton 1878**; Ripon **repealed** it.
- Trap: "Vernacular Press Act banned all newspapers." It targeted **vernacular** papers, not equally all English papers.
- Trap: "Ilbert Bill = press censorship." Ilbert is about **judges/racial jurisdiction**, not press law.

> **Exam note:** Press gag = **Lytton 1878**; repeal = **Ripon**; pair with newspaper–place matching.

### Results

| Result | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| Public sphere in print | Nationalism's communication system |
| Vernacular vs English legal inequality | 1878 Act politics |
| Editor as public leader | Tilak, Ghosh, and others |

### Exam Facts (raata)

- Vernacular Press Act: **1878**, Lytton
- Repealed by: **Ripon**
- Early reformer-press link: **Ram Mohan Roy**
- Discriminatory target: **vernacular** papers
- Not the same as Ilbert Bill **1883**

## Common Traps — Don't Fall For These

1. **Trap:** Wood's Dispatch in 1835. **Correct:** Wood **1854**; Macaulay **1835**.

2. **Trap:** Hunter before Wood. **Correct:** Wood → Hunter → Sadler → Sargent — **2018 Q21**.

3. **Trap:** Warren Hastings founded Asiatic Society. **Correct:** **Sir William Jones** — **2021 Q40**.

4. **Trap:** Hastings–Jones A/R means R explains A. **Correct:** Both true, R **not** correct explanation — **2019 Q22**.

5. **Trap:** Archbold was Principal of Lahore College. **Correct:** That statement is **false** — **2024 Q136**.

6. **Trap:** Sankaran Nair returned knighthood. **Correct:** **Tagore** returned knighthood; Nair resigned Council — **2022 Q83 / 2025 Q135**.

7. **Trap:** Swadesh — Almora / Shakti — Gorakhpur. **Correct:** Swadesh–**Gorakhpur**; Shakti–**Almora**.

8. **Trap:** Bharat Bandhu — Agra. **Correct:** Bharat Bandhu–**Hathras**; Satyawadi–**Agra**.

9. **Trap:** Vernacular Press Act by Ripon. **Correct:** Enacted by **Lytton 1878**; repealed by Ripon.

10. **Trap:** Wardha Scheme = Sargent Plan. **Correct:** Wardha **1937** Gandhi; Sargent **1944** official plan.

11. **Trap:** Indian Universities Act founded Calcutta University. **Correct:** Calcutta University **1857**; Act **1904** = control.

12. **Trap:** Orientalists wanted English medium higher education. **Correct:** That was the **Anglicist** position.

13. **Trap:** Downward filtration = mass primary schooling. **Correct:** Elite-first theory; Hunter later stressed primary education.

14. **Trap:** Kesari published from Agra. **Correct:** Kesari–**Pune** (Tilak); Agra in 2022 set is **Satyawadi**.

15. **Trap:** Ilbert Bill was a press law. **Correct:** Ilbert = racial jurisdiction in courts; press gag = Vernacular Press Act.
