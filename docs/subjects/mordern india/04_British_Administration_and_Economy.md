## 4.1 Administrative System of British India

- British India was run from the **district** upwards. The **Collector** was not only a tax officer. He was also **District Magistrate** and the civil head of the district.
- After the **Diwani of 1765** the Company had to collect Bengal’s land revenue itself.
- **Dual Government (1765–72)** left revenue with the Company and police/justice in the Nawab’s name. It produced corruption and made the **Bengal Famine of 1770** worse.
- **Warren Hastings** ended Dual Government in **1772**. Company officers then ran revenue and civil administration directly.
- **Cornwallis (1786–93)** built a professional civil service, separated revenue work from the judiciary, and made the Collector the district head.
- The **Charter Act of 1833** created the **Governor-General of India** and one all-India executive.
- The **Charter Act of 1853** started **open competition** for the civil service (later the ICS).
- After **1858** a **Secretary of State for India** sat in London and a **Viceroy** sat in India.
- The three **Presidencies** were **Bengal, Madras and Bombay**. After 1858 provinces were placed under the Viceroy, but the district collector remained the real steel frame.

> **Exam note:** Dual Government ended in **1772** under Hastings, not under Cornwallis. The Collector was never only a tax clerk.

## 4.2 Local Administration

- India’s **first Municipal Corporation** was set up at **Madras in 1688** (**2021 Q145**). It was not created by Ripon.
- Bombay got a municipal body in **1726**.
- **Lord Mayo (1870)** began **financial decentralisation**. Some funds and works (roads, schools) were given to local boards.
- **Lord Ripon’s Resolution of 1882** is why he is called the **Father of local self-government**. It asked for elected Indians on municipalities and district boards.
- The **Royal Commission on Decentralisation** sat in **1907**.
- Local bodies still had weak funds. Nominated members and British officials kept real power.
- The **73rd and 74th Amendments** are Polity, not this topic.

> **Exam note:** Ripon did **not** create the first corporation. Mayo is **1870**, not 1882.

## 4.3 British Economic Policy

- Colonial policy turned India into a **supplier of raw materials** and a **market for British manufactured goods**.
- **Land revenue** was the Company’s main Indian income after Diwani.
- From Plassey to about **1813** the Company mixed **trade monopoly** with the surplus of Bengal.
- After the Charter Act of **1813**, “free trade” was one-way: **Manchester cloth** entered freely while Indian crafts lost protection.
- After **1858** came railways on a **guarantee system**, **Home Charges**, and export of British capital.
- **Home Charges** were Indian revenues spent in Britain — India Office costs, pensions, and interest on debt.
- India exported cotton, indigo, jute, opium, tea and wheat. It imported textiles and machinery.
- Railways and the post helped Indians later, but their **first aim** was troops, trade and control.

> **Exam note:** Do not treat railways or postage as proof that British rule was mainly a welfare project.

## 4.4 British Land Revenue Systems

The British did **not** use one land system for all of India. Different regions got different systems, and that created **different classes of peasantry** (**2020 Q30**).

| Feature | Permanent Settlement | Ryotwari | Mahalwari |
|---------|----------------------|----------|-----------|
| Year | **1793** | 1820s (trials from 1790s) | **1822** report |
| Region | **Bengal, Bihar, Orissa** | **Madras, Bombay**, parts of Assam | **NWP / Punjab / parts of Central India** (UP-relevant) |
| Architect | **Cornwallis** | **Munro**, **Read** (Baramahal) | **Holt Mackenzie** (Bentinck extended) |
| Payer | **Zamindar** | **Ryot** (cultivator) | Village / **mahal** (often via **lambardar**) |
| Rights | Zamindar = proprietor | Ryot = **occupant**; state owns land | Collective village responsibility |
| Revision | **Fixed / permanent** | Periodic **20–30 yrs** | Periodic **20–30 yrs** |

- All three systems were meant to give the state a **steady cash surplus**, not to protect the peasant.
- Demand was often high and rigid. Default led to debt, sale of land, and higher famine risk.

## 4.5 Permanent Settlement, 1793

- Introduced by **Lord Cornwallis** in **1793** in **Bengal, Bihar and Orissa**. It was **not** applied to all of India.
- The **zamindar** was recognised as **proprietor**. He paid a **fixed** yearly sum to the Company.
- The actual cultivator became a **tenant**, not the owner of the soil.
- Failed farming contracts and **decennial** (ten-year) experiments came before the permanent fix.
- Under the **Sunset Law**, if the zamindar did not pay by sunset on the due day, the estate could be sold at auction. Many buyers were urban absentees.
- Because the state’s demand was frozen, the Company could **not** raise it when production later grew.
- **2025 Q40** matches Cornwallis with the Permanent Settlement of Bengal.

> **Exam note:** Not Wellesley. Cultivators did **not** become owners.

## 4.6 Ryotwari System

- **Alexander Read** first tried it in **Baramahal**. **Thomas Munro** expanded it as Governor of Madras. **Elphinstone** used it in Bombay.
- The state settled **directly with the cultivator (ryot)**. There was no Bengal-style zamindar in between.
- The ryot was an **occupant**, not a full freehold owner. Ultimate ownership stayed with the state.
- Revenue was **revised every 20–30 years**, so the state could raise demand.
- Higher cash demand pushed peasants into **cash crops** and into the hands of **moneylenders**.

> **Exam note:** Munro is Ryotwari, not Permanent Settlement. The ryot was not a freehold owner.

---

## 4.7 Mahalwari System

- Shaped by **Holt Mackenzie’s report of 1822** in the **North-Western Provinces**. **Bentinck** later extended the idea.
- The unit was the **village or mahal**, not a single field and not a Bengal zamindar.
- A **lambardar** often collected the village’s share.
- Demand was **revised periodically**. It was **not** a second Permanent Settlement.
- This is the land-revenue background of much of later **Uttar Pradesh**.

> **Exam note:** Mackenzie is Mahalwari, not Ryotwari.

## 4.8 Drain of Wealth Theory

- Drain means Indian resources were transferred to Britain **without an equal return**.
- **Home Charges** were a main channel: India Office costs, pensions, and debt interest paid in Britain from Indian revenues.
- Civil and military salaries spent or saved in England were another channel.
- **Unrequited exports** meant goods left India without matching imports coming back.
- **Dadabhai Naoroji** wrote *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India*, published in **1901** (**2021 Q139** — not 1900).
- **R.C. Dutt** wrote *The Economic History of India* as a critique of colonial exploitation.
- **Bipan Chandra** wrote *The Rise and Growth of Economic Nationalism in India* (**2019 Q97**).
- M.G. Ranade and G.V. Joshi also belong to this economic-nationalist stream.
- Early nationalists attacked **“Un-British rule”** (the Drain), not always the Empire itself. **2024 Q135:** both statements are true, but **R does not explain A**.

> **Exam note:** Year trap = **1901**, not 1900. Drain ≠ Gandhi’s invention. Drain ≠ deindustrialization.

## 4.9 Commercialization of Agriculture

- Peasants were pushed from food-for-the-family towards **cash crops** for the market: indigo, cotton, jute, opium, tea and sugarcane.
- Land revenue had to be paid in **cash**, so even a bad harvest forced sales.
- Railways and ports carried crops out to Bombay, Calcutta and Madras.
- Credit came from **moneylenders**. Default often meant loss of land.
- When food crops were displaced, **famine** risk rose. Commercialization did **not** mean peasant prosperity.
- The **Indigo Revolt of 1859–60** in Bengal was against forced indigo. That date is the lock in **2025 Q127**.

## 4.10 Deindustrialization

- Indian **handloom, metal-work and other crafts** declined under one-way free trade.
- Cheap **Manchester** cloth undercut Indian weavers. India exported **raw cotton** and imported finished cloth — the reverse of the older pattern.
- After conquest, courts and nobles no longer bought on the old scale, so artisans lost patrons.
- Many weavers were pushed into agriculture. That overcrowded the countryside. Bentinck’s remark on weavers’ bones is the usual quote.
- Modern factories came **late** and stayed thin. The nineteenth-century story is craft **decline**, not “Britain industrialised India.”

> **Exam note:** Deindustrialization is **not** the same thing as Drain of Wealth.

## 4.11 Railways

- The first passenger line ran from **Bombay to Thane in 1853**. It was **not** opened in 1857.
- **Dalhousie** pushed railways along with the telegraph and the post.
- Finance used the **guarantee system**: Indian revenues promised a return to British investors even if the line lost money.
- The first aims were moving **troops**, taking raw materials to ports, and running the administration — not passenger comfort.
- In the UP region the main junctions were **Kanpur, Lucknow and Allahabad**.

> **Exam note:** Railways are not a “welfare-only” story.

---

## 4.12 Telegraph

- The pioneer in India was **Dr William O’Shaughnessy**, not Dalhousie.
- An experimental line ran from **Calcutta to Diamond Harbour in 1851**.
- Dalhousie expanded the network in the 1850s.
- The telegraph was used for military control in **1857**.

> **Exam note:** Dalhousie **expanded** the telegraph. He did **not** invent it.

---

## 4.13 Postal System

- **Warren Hastings** started a Company postal system in **1774**, centred on Calcutta.
- Public use grew gradually; **1837** is a usual marker.
- The **Post Office Act of 1854** and **uniform penny postage** came under **Dalhousie**.
- The **Post Office Savings Bank** began in **1882**.
- Mail later rode the railways and reached villages more widely than the telegraph.

> **Exam note:** Hastings **started** the post. Dalhousie made postage **cheap and uniform**. Penny postage is **1854**, not 1774.

---

## 4.14 Impact of British Rule on Indian Economy

This section only joins the results. The mechanisms are already taught above.

- Agriculture produced different peasant classes under different revenue systems. Cash crops rose. Famine risk rose. Major famine markers are **1770 Bengal**, **1876–78**, and **1943 Bengal**.
- Industry saw handicrafts decline. Modern mills came late and stayed thin.
- Finance sent Indian surplus to Britain through the Drain and Home Charges.
- Rail, telegraph and post served control and trade first. Nationalists used them later.
- Society produced typical colonial classes: zamindar, moneylender and landless labourer.
- A dual economy grew. A few export pockets sat beside a distressed mass.
- India was **not** “fully modernised by 1947.” Infrastructure is not proof of benevolent rule.

**UP Focus**

- Land revenue in the North-Western Provinces (later UP) was mainly **Mahalwari**.
- The main rail junctions were **Kanpur, Lucknow and Allahabad**.
- Indigo was the cash crop of the Bihar–Bengal belt, with an eastern UP fringe.

## Common Traps — Don't Fall For These

1. **Munro = Permanent Settlement** → Munro = **Ryotwari**; Cornwallis = **1793**.
2. **Mackenzie = Ryotwari** → **Mahalwari 1822**.
3. **Naoroji book = 1900** → **1901** (2021 Q139).
4. **First railway = 1857** → **1853 Bombay–Thane**.
5. **Penny postage = Hastings 1774** → Hastings started post; **Dalhousie 1854** unified cheap postage.
6. **Permanent Settlement = all India** → Bengal, Bihar, Orissa.
7. **Ryot = full owner** → **occupant**; state owns land.
8. **Ripon created first municipal corporation** → **Madras 1688**.
9. **Drain = deindustrialization** → transfer vs craft decline.
10. **Railways = Indian welfare only** → troops, trade, guarantee profits.
11. **Mahalwari = permanent like 1793** → **periodic revision**.
12. **Collector = revenue clerk only** → also **District Magistrate**.
13. **Commercialization ended famines** → often **raised** famine risk.
14. **Dalhousie invented telegraph** → **O'Shaughnessy** experimented; Dalhousie expanded.
15. **2020 Q30 answer is B** → **A** (R explains A).  
16. **2024 Q135 answer is C** → **A** (both true; R does **not** explain A).
