## Round 2 — Articles 12 and 13

**Q13.** What is the significance of Article 12?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It defines “State” for Part III.** Fundamental Rights bind this State.

</details>

**Q14.** What entities are included within the definition of State under Article 12?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** Government and Parliament of India. Government and Legislature of each State. Local authorities. Other authorities in India or under the control of the Government of India.

</details>

**Q15.** Does State include the Union Government?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes.**

</details>

**Q16.** Does it include State Governments?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes.**

</details>

**Q17.** Does it include local authorities?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes** — municipalities, panchayats and similar bodies.

</details>

**Q18.** Can a private body fall under Article 12?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes, if it is an instrumentality of the State** (*R.D. Shetty / Ajay Hasia*): statute, government control, public funds, public functions.

</details>

**Q19.** What is the significance of Article 13?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It is the gate of judicial review for Fundamental Rights.** Inconsistent laws are void to that extent.

</details>

**Q20.** What does Article 13 say about laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: They are void to the extent of inconsistency.** “Law” includes ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, notification, custom and usage.

</details>

**Q21.** What is meant by judicial review in the context of Article 13?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Courts can examine a law and strike it down if it abridges Fundamental Rights.** Article 13(4) keeps constitutional amendments outside this “law” definition (24th Amendment, after *Golaknath*).

</details>

**Q22.** What is the difference between pre-Constitution laws and post-Constitution laws under Article 13?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 13(1) makes pre-1950 inconsistent laws void to that extent (eclipse).** 13(2) forbids the State from making a new abridging law, which is void.

</details>

**Q23.** What is the doctrine of severability?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Only the invalid part of a statute is struck down** if it can be separated. The rest may stand.

</details>

**Q24.** What is the doctrine of eclipse?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A pre-Constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right is dormant, not dead.** It can revive if the Fundamental Right is later amended (*Bhikaji Narain*).

</details>

**Q25.** Can Parliament completely exclude judicial review through an ordinary law?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** Judicial review is part of the Basic Structure (*Kesavananda / Minerva Mills*).

</details>

## Round 3 — Right to Equality

**Q26.** Which Article guarantees equality before law and equal protection of laws?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 14.** It is available to **any person**.

</details>

**Q27.** What is the difference between equality before law and equal protection of laws?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Equality before law is British (Dicey): no special privileges.** Equal protection of laws is American: equal treatment in equal circumstances.

</details>

**Q28.** Is Article 14 based on absolute equality?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** It forbids class legislation but permits **reasonable classification**. Arbitrary State action also violates 14 (*Royappa / Maneka*).

</details>

**Q29.** What is meant by reasonable classification?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The State may treat unequals differently if the grouping is rational**, not arbitrary.

</details>

**Q30.** What are the two requirements for a valid classification?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Intelligible differentia, and a rational nexus with the object of the law.**

</details>

**Q31.** Can the State make special provisions for disadvantaged groups while still complying with Article 14?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes.** Arts. 15(3)–(6) and 16(4)–(6) are enabling special provisions. They sit with, not against, equality.

</details>

**Q32.** Which Article prohibits discrimination on religion, race, caste, sex and place of birth?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 15** (citizens).

</details>

**Q33.** Which Article provides equality of opportunity in public employment?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 16** (citizens only).

</details>

**Q34.** Does Article 16 guarantee equality in all employment, or specifically public employment?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Public employment under the State only.**

</details>

**Q35.** What is the significance of Article 16(4)?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It enables reservation in services for backward classes not adequately represented.**

</details>

**Q36.** Is reservation under Article 16(4) a Fundamental Right of backward classes or an enabling provision?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: An enabling provision of the State**, not a Fundamental Right of any class (*Indra Sawhney*).

</details>

**Q37.** Which Article abolishes untouchability?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 17.** It is absolute. It is **equality**, not Right against Exploitation.

</details>

**Q38.** Is untouchability defined in the Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** Practice in any form is a punishable offence. The Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 gives statutory content.

</details>

**Q39.** Which Article abolishes titles?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 18.** Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are **not** titles in this sense (*Balaji Raghavan*).

</details>

**Q40.** Can the State confer military or academic distinctions?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes.** Article 18 excepts military and academic distinctions.

</details>

**Q41.** Can a citizen accept a title from a foreign State?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No**, without the President’s consent as the Article requires. An Indian citizen shall not accept a foreign title.

</details>

**Q42.** Which case is associated with the abolition of privy purses?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Madhav Rao Scindia vs Union of India (1971)** struck down a Presidential order derecognising rulers. The **26th Amendment, 1971** then abolished privy purses and ruler titles.

</details>

## Round 4 — Article 19

**Q43.** Which Article guarantees the six freedoms?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 19.** Citizens only.

</details>

**Q44.** What were the original seven freedoms under Article 19?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, property, and profession.**

</details>

**Q45.** Which freedom was removed?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Property — Article 19(1)(f)**, by the **44th Amendment**.

</details>

**Q46.** What are the six freedoms currently guaranteed under Article 19?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** (a) speech and expression (b) assembly (c) association, unions and co-operatives (d) movement (e) residence (g) profession.

</details>

**Q47.** Is Article 19 available to foreigners?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** Citizens only.

</details>

**Q48.** Can these freedoms be restricted?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes, by reasonable restrictions in Articles 19(2) to 19(6).** The restriction must be by **law**.

</details>

**Q49.** What is meant by reasonable restrictions?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Limits that are by law, for listed grounds, and that courts can test as fair and proportionate**, not executive whim.

</details>

**Q50.** What are the grounds on which freedom of speech and expression can be restricted?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 19(2):** sovereignty and integrity of India; security of the State; friendly relations with foreign States; public order; decency or morality; contempt of court; defamation; incitement to an offence.

</details>

**Q51.** Can freedom of speech be restricted in the interest of sovereignty and integrity of India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes** (16th Amendment added this ground).

</details>

**Q52.** Can freedom of speech be restricted on grounds of public order?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes.**

</details>

**Q53.** Can freedom of speech be restricted on grounds of defamation?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes.**

</details>

**Q54.** Can freedom of speech be restricted on grounds of contempt of court?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes.**

</details>

**Q55.** Can freedom of assembly be exercised with arms?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** Article 19(1)(b) is assemble **peaceably and without arms**.

</details>

**Q56.** What is the difference between the freedoms under Article 19(1)(a) and Article 19(1)(b)?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 19(1)(a) is speech and expression**, including the press. **19(1)(b) is peaceful unarmed assembly.** Their restriction grounds also differ (19(2) vs 19(3)).

</details>

## Round 5 — Articles 20–22

**Q57.** What three protections are provided under Article 20?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No ex post facto criminal law. No double jeopardy. No self-incrimination.** Torture is **not** a separate Article 20 clause.

</details>

**Q58.** What is an ex-post-facto law?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A law that punishes an act that was not an offence when done, or increases the penalty after the act.** Article 20(1) bars this in criminal law.

</details>

**Q59.** What is double jeopardy?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once** (Art. 20(2)).

</details>

**Q60.** What is self-incrimination?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No accused shall be compelled to be a witness against himself** (Art. 20(3)).

</details>

**Q61.** Are Article 20 protections available to foreigners?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes.** Article 20 is for all persons.

</details>

**Q62.** Which Article guarantees Right to Life and Personal Liberty?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 21.**

</details>

**Q63.** Why is Article 21 considered one of the most important Fundamental Rights?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: After Maneka Gandhi it covers fair, just and reasonable procedure**, and a wide set of rights such as privacy, livelihood, health, dignity and speedy trial.

</details>

**Q64.** What is the difference between Article 20 and Article 21?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 20 is three conviction protections.** Article 21 is life and personal liberty, including privacy (*Puttaswamy*). Privacy is **not** in Article 19.

</details>

**Q65.** What does Article 21A provide?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Free and compulsory education** for children aged **6–14**.

</details>

**Q66.** Which Constitutional Amendment inserted Article 21A?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The 86th Amendment, 2002.**

</details>

**Q67.** What age group is covered by Article 21A?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 6 to 14 years.** Early childhood below 6 is DPSP Article 45.

</details>

**Q68.** Which Article provides safeguards against arbitrary arrest and detention?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 22.**

</details>

**Q69.** What are the basic safeguards available to an arrested person?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Grounds of arrest, right to a lawyer, and production before a magistrate within 24 hours** (ordinary arrest). Preventive detention has a separate code in 22(3)–(7).

</details>

**Q70.** Within how much time must an arrested person be produced before a magistrate?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 24 hours**, excluding journey time.

</details>

**Q71.** Does Article 22 provide protection against preventive detention?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes, a limited code.** An Advisory Board and a maximum period fixed by Parliament apply. It is not a total ban on preventive detention.

</details>

**Q72.** What is preventive detention?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Detention to prevent a person from doing a future prejudicial act**, not to punish a past offence.

</details>

**Q73.** What is the difference between punitive detention and preventive detention?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Punitive detention follows conviction for a past offence.** Preventive detention is without that trial, to prevent a future act.

</details>

</details>

</details>

**Q89.** Which Article deals with religious instruction in educational institutions?

<details>

</details>

**Q90.** Can religious instruction be provided in an institution wholly maintained by State funds?

<details>

</details>

**Ans: Yes.**

## Round 9 — Remedies and writs

**Q99.** Which Article provides the Right to Constitutional Remedies?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 32.**

</details>

**Q100.** Why did Dr. B.R. Ambedkar call Article 32 the heart and soul of the Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Without a guaranteed remedy, Fundamental Rights would be paper rights.** Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right.

</details>

**Q101.** Which five writs can the Supreme Court issue?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari and Quo Warranto.**

</details>

**Q102.** Which writ means “produce the body”?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Habeas Corpus.**

</details>

**Q103.** When is Habeas Corpus issued?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: To produce a detained person and test the legality of detention.**

</details>

**Q104.** Can Habeas Corpus be issued against a private individual?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes.**

</details>

**Q105.** Which writ commands a public authority to perform a public duty?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Mandamus.**

</details>

**Q106.** Which writ is called Mandamus?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: “We command.”** It lies against a public authority to perform a public duty. It does not lie against a private person for a purely private duty.

</details>

**Q107.** Can Mandamus be issued against the President or Governor?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No**, not against the President or Governor personally.

</details>

**Q108.** Which writ is issued to prevent a lower court or tribunal from exceeding its jurisdiction?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Prohibition**, issued **before** the decision.

</details>

**Q109.** What is the difference between Prohibition and Certiorari?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Prohibition stops a proceeding before the decision.** Certiorari quashes an order **after** it is made.

</details>

**Q110.** Which writ is used to challenge a person’s right to hold a public office?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Quo Warranto.**

</details>

**Q111.** What is Quo Warranto?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: “By what authority.”** It tests whether a person legally holds a substantive public office created by law.

</details>

**Q112.** What is the difference between Article 32 and Article 226?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 32 is a Fundamental Right to move the Supreme Court for Fundamental Rights only.** Article 226 lets High Courts issue writs for Fundamental Rights **and any other purpose**.

</details>

**Q113.** Which has a wider scope — Article 32 or Article 226?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 226.**

</details>

## Round 12 — Rights vs Duties

**Q136.** What is the major difference between a Fundamental Right and a Fundamental Duty?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A Fundamental Right is justiciable against the State.** A Fundamental Duty is a citizen obligation and is not directly enforceable.

</details>

**Q137.** Can Fundamental Duties be used by courts while interpreting Fundamental Rights?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes.** Courts often read rights in the light of duties.

</details>

**Q138.** Can a Fundamental Duty override a Fundamental Right?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** Duties aid interpretation. They do not destroy Part III.

</details>

**Q139.** Are Fundamental Duties enforceable in the same way as Fundamental Rights?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** There is no Article 32 remedy for a duty as such.

</details>

**Q140.** Why are Fundamental Duties considered important despite being non-justiciable?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: They remind citizens that rights have correlative obligations**, guide legislation, and help courts interpret the Constitution.

</details>

## Round 14 — Landmark cases

**Q149.** What was the Golaknath case about?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Whether Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights** (*Golaknath vs State of Punjab, 1967*).

</details>

**Q150.** What did the Supreme Court initially hold regarding Parliament’s power to amend Fundamental Rights in Golaknath?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Parliament cannot amend Fundamental Rights.** An amendment was treated as “law” under Article 13. The **24th Amendment, 1971** reversed that for the text.

</details>

**Q151.** What is the significance of Kesavananda Bharati vs State of Kerala?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 1973 — Parliament can amend any part, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot destroy the Basic Structure.**

</details>

**Q152.** What is the Basic Structure Doctrine?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Core features of the Constitution cannot be abrogated by Article 368.** The list is illustrative, not a closed schedule.

</details>

**Q153.** Can Parliament amend Fundamental Rights after Kesavananda Bharati?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes**, so long as the Basic Structure survives.

</details>

**Q154.** Can Parliament destroy the basic structure while exercising its amendment power?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** *Minerva Mills* (1980) struck down the attempt to make Article 368 unlimited.

</details>

**Q155.** What was the significance of Maneka Gandhi vs Union of India for Article 21?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 1978 — “procedure established by law” must be fair, just and reasonable**, not any procedure.

</details>

**Q156.** How did the interpretation of Article 21 change after Maneka Gandhi?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 21 expanded into a cluster of rights** (livelihood, dignity, privacy later in *Puttaswamy*, speedy trial, health) and was read with Articles 14 and 19.

</details>

**Q157.** What was the significance of Minerva Mills vs Union of India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 1980 — limited amending power and judicial review are Basic Structure.** Harmony of Fundamental Rights and DPSPs was restored.

</details>

**Q158.** What did the Supreme Court say about the balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: They are complementary.** DPSPs cannot destroy Fundamental Rights. Harmony is Basic Structure (*Minerva Mills*).

</details>
