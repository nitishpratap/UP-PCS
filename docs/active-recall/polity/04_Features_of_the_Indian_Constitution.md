## Level 1 — Basic features

**Q1.** What are the major features of the Indian Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** Lengthiest written Constitution. Blend of rigidity and flexibility. Federal system with unitary bias (quasi-federal). Parliamentary government. Fundamental Rights, DPSP and Duties. Secular State. Single citizenship. Universal adult franchise. Independent and integrated judiciary. Emergency provisions. Independent constitutional bodies. Three-tier government after 1992. Basic Structure limit on amendment.

</details>

**Q2.** Why is the Indian Constitution described as a written Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Its provisions are enacted in a single enacted document** adopted by the Constituent Assembly and enforced from 26 January 1950. It is not a mainly conventional constitution like the UK.

</details>

**Q3.** Is the Indian Constitution completely written? Explain.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Almost entirely written, but not only the text.** Conventions also operate, such as Cabinet secrecy, the Prime Minister’s leadership, and the Official Opposition. The written document remains supreme.

</details>

**Q4.** Why is the Indian Constitution described as a lengthy or detailed Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It is the lengthiest written Constitution of any sovereign country.** It originally had 395 Articles, 22 Parts and 8 Schedules, and has expanded far beyond that.

</details>

**Q5.** What factors contributed to the large size of the Indian Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** Vast diversity. A **single Constitution** for Union and States. Detailed administrative, Emergency, FR and DPSP provisions. Heavy borrowing from the **GOI Act, 1935**. Legalistic drafting and safeguards for minorities, SC/ST and services.

</details>

**Q6.** What is meant by a blend of rigidity and flexibility in the Indian Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Some provisions are hard to change and some are easy.** Amendment uses Article 368 special procedures, but several changes follow only a simple majority of Parliament.

</details>

**Q7.** How is the Indian Constitution both rigid and flexible?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It is not as rigid as the USA and not as flexible as the UK.** Simple-majority paths (Arts. 2–4, 169) sit beside special majority, and federal provisions need special majority plus ratification by half the States.

</details>

**Q8.** What is meant by a federal system with unitary bias?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Dual polity and division of powers give a federal form, while a strong Centre gives a unitary spirit.** K.C. Wheare called this **quasi-federal**. Ambedkar said federal in form, unitary in spirit.

</details>

**Q9.** Why is India described as a Union of States rather than a federation of states?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 1 uses “Union of States.”** The federation is not an agreement among States. States have **no right to secede**. Parliament can alter boundaries under **Article 3**.

</details>

**Q10.** What does single citizenship mean?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: There is only one citizenship of India** (Part II, Arts. 5–11). There is no separate State citizenship as in the USA.

</details>

**Q11.** Which country influenced India’s idea of single citizenship?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The United Kingdom.** Canada also follows single citizenship in a federal frame. The USA follows dual citizenship.

</details>

**Q12.** What is the significance of universal adult franchise?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Every adult citizen may vote without property, tax or educational qualification** (Art. 326). It gives the Preamble’s democratic character real electoral meaning from the first general election.

</details>

**Q13.** What was the voting age originally prescribed under the Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 21 years.**

</details>

**Q14.** Which Constitutional Amendment reduced the voting age from 21 to 18?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The 61st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1988** (in force 1989).

</details>

**Q15.** What is meant by parliamentary government?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The real executive is drawn from the legislature and is responsible to it**, especially the Lok Sabha. The President is the nominal executive. The Prime Minister and Council of Ministers are the real executive.

</details>

## Level 2 — Federal and unitary features

**Q16.** List five federal features of the Indian Constitution.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** Dual polity of Union and States. Written Constitution and supremacy of the Constitution. Division of powers in three lists. Independent judiciary with judicial review. Rigid amendment of federal provisions with State ratification. Bicameralism with a Rajya Sabha of States is another federal mark.

</details>

**Q17.** List five unitary features of the Indian Constitution.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** Single Constitution. Single citizenship. Residuary powers with the Centre. Integrated judiciary. Centre-appointed Governor. Emergency provisions. Unequal Rajya Sabha representation. Article 3 power over State boundaries. No right to secede.

</details>

**Q18.** How does the distribution of powers establish India’s federal character?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Legislative power is divided by the Seventh Schedule** under Article 246 into Union, State and Concurrent Lists. Each government has its own field in normal times.

</details>

**Q19.** What are the three legislative lists?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Union List, State List and Concurrent List** (Seventh Schedule).

</details>

**Q20.** Which list contains subjects of national importance?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The Union List.** It is also the longer and stronger list.

</details>

**Q21.** Who has residuary legislative powers in India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Parliament / the Centre** (Art. 248 and Union List entry 97). This follows the **Canada** pattern, not the USA.

</details>

**Q22.** Why is the Indian federation considered strong-centred?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** Residuary powers lie with the Centre. Union law prevails on the Concurrent List (Art. 254). Emergency can unitarise the system. Parliament can in specified cases legislate on the State List. States cannot secede.

</details>

**Q23.** Under what circumstances can Parliament legislate on a subject in the State List?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** Rajya Sabha resolution in the national interest (**Art. 249**). National Emergency (**Art. 250**). Two or more States requesting (**Art. 252**). Implementing an international treaty (**Art. 253**). During President’s Rule (**Art. 356**).

</details>

**Q24.** Can Parliament alter the boundaries of a State?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes, under Article 3.** The State legislature’s view is taken but is **not binding**. The path is a simple majority of Parliament.

</details>

**Q25.** Does a State have the right to secede from the Union?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** India is an indestructible Union of destructible States.

</details>

**Q26.** What is the significance of Article 3 in India’s federal structure?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Parliament may form new States and alter names, areas or boundaries.** This unitary tilt shows that States are not sovereign contracting parties.

</details>

**Q27.** Why is the Rajya Sabha considered a federal chamber?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It represents the States in Parliament.** It also has special federal powers such as Article 249 (State List in national interest) and Article 312 (All-India Services).

</details>

**Q28.** Is the representation of States in the Rajya Sabha equal?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** Seats are allotted largely by population (Fourth Schedule). The USA Senate gives equal representation to each State.

</details>

**Q29.** How does the Indian system differ from the American federal system?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** India has a strong Centre, single citizenship, an integrated judiciary, residuary powers with the Centre, and unequal Rajya Sabha seats. The USA has classical dual federalism, dual citizenship, dual courts, residuary powers with States, and equal Senate representation.

</details>

**Q30.** Why is India sometimes described as quasi-federal?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: K.C. Wheare used the label because India is federal in form but has a strong Centre.** Ambedkar’s line is federal in form, unitary in spirit.

</details>

## Level 3 — Parliamentary system

**Q31.** What are the main features of the parliamentary form of government?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** Dual executive. Majority or coalition rule from the Lok Sabha. Collective responsibility. Individual responsibility. Political homogeneity. Double membership of ministers in Parliament. Leadership of the Prime Minister. Power to dissolve the Lok Sabha. Cabinet secrecy. Official Opposition.

</details>

**Q32.** What is the difference between the nominal executive and the real executive?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The nominal executive is the constitutional head who acts on aid and advice.** The real executive is the Council of Ministers that actually governs.

</details>

**Q33.** Who is the nominal executive at the Union level?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President** (Arts. 53 and 74).

</details>

**Q34.** Who exercises the real executive power?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister** (Arts. 74 and 75).

</details>

**Q35.** To whom is the Council of Ministers collectively responsible?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The Lok Sabha** (Art. 75(3)).

</details>

**Q36.** What does collective responsibility mean?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The Council of Ministers stands and falls together.** A no-confidence vote in the Lok Sabha can remove the entire Cabinet.

</details>

**Q37.** What is the difference between collective responsibility and individual responsibility?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Collective responsibility is of the whole Cabinet to the Lok Sabha.** Individual responsibility means each minister holds office during the pleasure of the President and may resign for a portfolio failure, by convention on the Prime Minister’s advice.

</details>

**Q38.** Why is the Prime Minister considered the pivot of the parliamentary system?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The PM is the keystone of the Cabinet.** The PM selects ministers, allocates portfolios, coordinates policy, and can advise dissolution of the Lok Sabha.

</details>

**Q39.** What is the relationship between the executive and the legislature in a parliamentary system?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Fusion, not strict separation.** Ministers are usually members of Parliament. The executive is responsible to the legislature.

</details>

**Q40.** What happens to the Council of Ministers if it loses the confidence of the Lok Sabha?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It must resign.** The government falls as a whole under collective responsibility.

</details>

## Level 4 — Fundamental Rights and DPSP

**Q41.** Why are Fundamental Rights considered an essential feature of the Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: They limit State power and are justiciable.** They protect liberty, equality and dignity and can be enforced in courts.

</details>

**Q42.** How many Fundamental Rights are currently guaranteed by the Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Six.** Originally there were seven. The Right to Property was removed from Part III by the **44th Amendment, 1978**.

</details>

**Q43.** Which Fundamental Right was removed from the list of Fundamental Rights?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The Right to Property** (former Arts. 19(1)(f) and 31).

</details>

**Q44.** What is the present constitutional status of the Right to Property?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It is a constitutional legal right, not a Fundamental Right.** It sits in Part XII as **Article 300A**.

</details>

**Q45.** Which Article protects the Right to Property?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 300A.** No person shall be deprived of property save by authority of law.

</details>

**Q46.** What is the difference between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Fundamental Rights are justiciable limits on the State (Part III).** DPSPs are non-justiciable policy directives for a welfare State (Part IV).

</details>

**Q47.** Are DPSPs enforceable by courts?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** They are not directly enforceable. Courts may still use them to interpret laws and rights.

</details>

**Q48.** Why are DPSPs nevertheless considered fundamental to governance?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 37 says they are fundamental in the governance of the country** and that it shall be the duty of the State to apply them in making laws.

</details>

**Q49.** What is the relationship between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: They are complementary.** Harmony between FR and DPSP is part of the Basic Structure (*Minerva Mills*, 1980). DPSPs cannot destroy Fundamental Rights.

</details>

**Q50.** Which constitutional mechanism makes Fundamental Rights enforceable?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The right to constitutional remedies under Article 32**, including writs. High Courts have a parallel writ power under Article 226.

</details>

**Q51.** Which Fundamental Right has been described by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar as the heart and soul of the Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 32**, the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

</details>

## Level 5 — Independent judiciary

**Q52.** What does independence of judiciary mean?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Judges decide without fear or favour of the executive or legislature.** Independence protects constitutional supremacy, federal disputes, Fundamental Rights and the Rule of Law.

</details>

**Q53.** What are the major safeguards for judicial independence?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** Security of tenure and a hard removal process. Salaries charged on the Consolidated Fund. Conduct of judges not discussed in the legislature except on a removal motion. Power of judicial review. Article 50 (separation of judiciary from executive).

</details>

**Q54.** Why is security of tenure important for judges?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A judge cannot be removed at the pleasure of the executive.** Removal needs proved misbehaviour or incapacity through the parliamentary address process.

</details>

**Q55.** Who appoints judges of the Supreme Court?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President.** By evolved practice the Collegium recommends names. The constitutional appointer remains the President.

</details>

**Q56.** Can Parliament remove a Supreme Court judge?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Parliament can pass an address for removal; the President then issues the order.** Each House must support the motion by a special majority on proved misbehaviour or incapacity. Parliament does not dismiss a judge by ordinary vote.

</details>

**Q57.** What is judicial review?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The power of courts to examine laws and executive action and strike them down if they violate the Constitution.**

</details>

**Q58.** What is the difference between judicial review and judicial activism?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Judicial review is the constitutional power to test validity against the Constitution.** Judicial activism is a more assertive use of that power, including PILs and directions, which critics may call overreach.

</details>

**Q59.** What is the significance of Article 32?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It is itself a Fundamental Right to constitutional remedies.** The Supreme Court can issue writs to enforce Part III. Ambedkar called it the heart and soul of the Constitution.

</details>

**Q60.** What is meant by the integrated judicial system in India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: One hierarchy under the Supreme Court.** Subordinate Courts lead to High Courts, which lead to the Supreme Court. It is not a dual unrelated federal-and-state court system.

</details>

**Q61.** How does India’s judicial system differ from that of the USA?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: India is integrated under one apex court.** The USA has a dual system of federal courts and separate State courts running more in parallel.

</details>

## Level 6 — Secular, socialist and democratic

**Q62.** What does the term secular mean in the Indian constitutional context?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The State treats all religions equally and may intervene to reform social evils.** This is positive or reformist secularism, not a US-style wall of separation. *S.R. Bommai* (1994) held secularism to be Basic Structure.

</details>

**Q63.** Was the word secular present in the original Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Not in the Preamble.** Secular content already existed in Arts. 25–28 and the equality clauses. The Preamble word was added by the **42nd Amendment, 1976**.

</details>

**Q64.** Which amendment inserted the words Socialist, Secular and Integrity into the Preamble?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The 42nd Constitutional Amendment, 1976.** On 26 January 1950 the Preamble status was only Sovereign Democratic Republic.

</details>

**Q65.** What does socialism mean in the Indian constitutional context?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Democratic socialism aimed at reducing inequality and building a welfare State**, not a Soviet one-party command economy. It is read with DPSPs and the Preamble’s Justice.

</details>

**Q66.** What is the difference between Indian and Western concepts of secularism?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Western (especially US) secularism stresses separation of Church and State.** Indian secularism is equal respect for all religions plus State power to reform (positive secularism).

</details>

**Q67.** Why is India described as a democratic republic?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Democratic means authority rests on the people through adult franchise.** Republic means the Head of State is elected, not a hereditary monarch.

</details>

**Q68.** What is the difference between a democracy and a republic?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Democracy is about who holds political power — the people.** A republic is about how the Head of State is chosen — election, not heredity. The UK is a democracy but not a republic.

</details>

**Q69.** Why is the Indian President called the head of a republic?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President is an elected Head of State**, not a hereditary monarch.

</details>

**Q70.** What is the significance of popular sovereignty in the Indian Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The Preamble begins “We, the People of India.”** Constitution-making authority is traced to the people, not to the Crown or the States.

</details>

## Level 7 — Universal adult franchise and elections

**Q71.** What is universal adult franchise?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Every adult citizen has the right to vote** if not disqualified, without property, tax or educational bars (Art. 326).

</details>

**Q72.** Why was universal adult franchise a remarkable feature of the Constitution at independence?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Independent India adopted adult suffrage at once**, despite mass illiteracy and poverty, instead of a staged property or literacy franchise.

</details>

**Q73.** Which body conducts elections to Parliament and State legislatures?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The Election Commission of India.**

</details>

**Q74.** Which Article provides for the Election Commission?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 324.**

</details>

**Q75.** What is the principle of one person, one vote?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Each adult citizen’s vote has equal value** in the same election. There is no plural voting by property or status.

</details>

**Q76.** What is meant by secret ballot?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The voter’s choice is not disclosed.** Secrecy protects free and fair elections.

</details>

**Q77.** What is the constitutional basis of an independent Election Commission?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 324 vests superintendence, direction and control of elections in the Commission.** Tenure, service conditions and a hard removal process for the Chief Election Commissioner insulate it from day-to-day executive control.

</details>

## Level 8 — Emergency and unitary bias

**Q78.** Why does the Constitution contain emergency provisions?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: To meet war, external aggression, armed rebellion, constitutional breakdown in a State, or a financial threat to credit.** In a crisis the Centre can act with greater unity of power.

</details>

**Q79.** What are the three types of emergencies?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** National Emergency (**Art. 352**). State Emergency / President’s Rule (**Art. 356**). Financial Emergency (**Art. 360**). All sit in Part XVIII.

</details>

**Q80.** Which emergency can convert the federal structure into a more unitary structure?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A National Emergency under Article 352.** Parliament can then legislate on the State List (Art. 250). President’s Rule also shifts State executive and legislative power to the Centre.

</details>

**Q81.** What happens to the distribution of legislative powers during a National Emergency?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Parliament may make laws on State List subjects** (Art. 250). The lists are not deleted, but the Centre’s legislative reach expands.

</details>

**Q82.** Can Fundamental Rights be suspended during every type of emergency?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** Automatic or Presidential suspension of rights is tied to **National Emergency** (Arts. 358 and 359). After the **44th Amendment**, Articles **20 and 21** cannot be suspended. President’s Rule and Financial Emergency do not suspend Fundamental Rights in that way.

</details>

**Q83.** Which emergency has never been imposed in India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Financial Emergency under Article 360.**

</details>

**Q84.** How does President’s Rule affect the federal structure?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President assumes the State executive, and Parliament may exercise the State legislature’s powers.** The State’s normal dual-government working is suspended for that period.

</details>

**Q85.** Why are emergency provisions considered evidence of the Constitution’s unitary bias?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: In a crisis the Centre can override the normal federal division.** Ambedkar’s fact is that the Constitution can work federally in peace and like a unitary system in Emergency.

</details>

## Level 9 — Constitutional amendment

**Q86.** Why is the Indian Constitution neither completely rigid nor completely flexible?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Different provisions use different amendment paths.** Some need only a simple majority. Some need a special majority. Federal provisions also need ratification by half the States.

</details>

**Q87.** What are the three broad methods of constitutional amendment?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** Simple majority of Parliament (outside Art. 368). Special majority under Article 368. Special majority plus ratification by at least half of the State legislatures.

</details>

**Q88.** Which provisions can be amended by a simple majority?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Formation and alteration of States (Arts. 2–4) and abolition or creation of Legislative Councils (Art. 169)** are the standard facts. These paths are like ordinary legislation and are not counted as Art. 368 amendments.

</details>

**Q89.** Which provisions require a special majority of Parliament?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Most provisions under Article 368**, including many FR, DPSP and institutional clauses. Special majority means a majority of the **total membership** of each House plus **two-thirds of members present and voting**.

</details>

**Q90.** Which provisions require a special majority plus ratification by at least half of the States?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Federal provisions.** Facts include election of the President, extent of Union and State executive power, Supreme Court and High Courts, distribution of legislative powers, representation of States in Parliament, and Article 368 itself.

</details>

**Q91.** Can the entire Constitution be amended?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Parliament may amend any part, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot destroy the Basic Structure.** *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) set that limit.

</details>

**Q92.** What is the Basic Structure Doctrine?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Parliament cannot abrogate or destroy the Constitution’s basic features while using Article 368.** The list is illustrative, not a closed constitutional schedule.

</details>

**Q93.** Which case established the Basic Structure Doctrine?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Kesavananda Bharati vs State of Kerala, 1973** (13-judge bench).

</details>

**Q94.** Can Parliament amend Fundamental Rights?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes, after the 24th Amendment (1971) and Kesavananda (1973), but not so as to destroy the Basic Structure.** *Golaknath* (1967) had said Parliament could not amend FR. That bar did not survive.

</details>

**Q95.** Can Parliament amend the basic structure of the Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** *Minerva Mills* (1980) struck down the attempt in the 42nd Amendment to make Article 368 unlimited.

</details>

**Q96.** From which country was the parliamentary system borrowed?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The United Kingdom.**

</details>

**Q97.** From which country were Fundamental Rights inspired?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The United States of America.**

</details>

**Q98.** From which country was the idea of judicial review borrowed?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The United States of America.**

</details>

**Q99.** From which country were Directive Principles of State Policy borrowed?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Ireland.**

</details>

**Q100.** From which country was the idea of Fundamental Duties borrowed?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The USSR model**, added by the **42nd Amendment, 1976** (Swaran Singh Committee). They were not in the original 1950 text.

</details>

**Q101.** From which country was the idea of the Concurrent List borrowed?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Australia.** Residuary powers are from **Canada**, not Australia.

</details>

**Q102.** From which country was the procedure for constitutional amendment inspired?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: South Africa** is the usual coaching fact for the amendment procedure. Do not confuse it with the USA’s more uniformly rigid method.

</details>

**Q103.** From which country was the idea of emergency provisions borrowed?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Weimar Germany** for suspension of Fundamental Rights. The structural emergency frame also draws on the **GOI Act, 1935**.

</details>

**Q104.** From which country was the idea of single citizenship borrowed?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The United Kingdom.**

</details>

**Q105.** From which country was the idea of a federation with a strong Centre borrowed?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Canada**, including residuary powers with the Centre and a Centre-appointed Governor.

</details>

**Q106.** Which features were taken from or influenced by the Government of India Act, 1935?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** Federal scheme and three lists. Office of Governor. Judiciary / Federal Court path. Public Service Commissions. Emergency lineage. A large part of the administrative skeleton.

</details>

## Level 11 — Statement-based UPPCS stems

**Q107.** Consider the following statements:

1. India has a federal constitution with a strong unitary bias.
2. The Constitution provides for single citizenship.
3. States have an inherent right to secede from the Union.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

A. 1 and 2 only
B. 2 and 3 only
C. 1 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A.** Statement 3 is wrong. States have **no** right to secede.

</details>

**Q108.** Consider the following statements:

1. The Indian Constitution is completely rigid.
2. Some constitutional provisions can be amended by simple majority.
3. Some provisions require ratification by at least half of the States.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

A. 1 and 2 only
B. 2 and 3 only
C. 1 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: B.** Statement 1 is wrong. The Constitution is a **blend** of rigidity and flexibility.

## Level 12 — Fifteen must-know recap

**Q113.** Why is the Constitution called federal with a unitary bias?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Dual polity and three lists are federal.** Strong Centre, Emergency, Article 3, single citizenship and residuary powers with the Union are the unitary bias. Wheare: quasi-federal.

</details>

**Q114.** Name five federal features and five unitary features.

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Federal:** dual polity, written Constitution, division of powers, independent judiciary, State ratification of federal amendments. **Unitary:** single Constitution, single citizenship, residuary with Centre, integrated judiciary, Centre-appointed Governor.

</details>

**Q115.** Why is the Constitution both rigid and flexible?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Three amendment paths exist.** Arts. 2–4 can move by simple majority. Ordinary Art. 368 needs special majority. Federal clauses need special majority plus half the States.

</details>

**Q116.** Why is it one of the lengthiest written constitutions?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Diversity, a single Constitution for Centre and States, GOI Act 1935 skeleton, and detailed FR, DPSP, Emergency and administrative clauses.**

</details>

**Q117.** What is the meaning and significance of parliamentary government?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Real executive from the legislature, responsible to the Lok Sabha.** That responsibility is the defining test of India’s parliamentary system.

</details>

**Q118.** What is the meaning of single citizenship?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Only Indian citizenship, not a separate State citizenship.** Source fact: UK.

</details>

**Q119.** What is the meaning of universal adult franchise?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Adult suffrage under Article 326.** Age is **18** after the **61st Amendment**.

</details>

**Q120.** What is the meaning of independent judiciary?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Judges are insulated from executive and legislative pressure** by tenure, salary charge and a hard removal process, so they can enforce the Constitution.

</details>

**Q121.** What is the meaning of judicial review?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Courts can strike down unconstitutional law or action.** Source fact: USA.

</details>

**Q122.** How do Fundamental Rights differ from DPSPs?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: FRs are justiciable (USA source).** DPSPs are non-justiciable but fundamental in governance (Ireland source). Harmony is Basic Structure.

</details>

**Q123.** What is the meaning of secularism in India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Equal respect for all religions plus reformist State power.** The Preamble word came in 1976. Substance is older (Arts. 25–28).

</details>

**Q124.** What is the meaning of democratic republic?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: People are sovereign through adult franchise, and the Head of State is elected.**

</details>

**Q125.** What are the three types of constitutional amendment?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Simple majority. Special majority. Special majority plus ratification by half the States.**

</details>

**Q126.** What is the Basic Structure Doctrine, and which case established it?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Parliament cannot destroy the Constitution’s basic features.** *Kesavananda Bharati*, **1973**.

</details>

**Q127.** Recite the high-yield borrowed-feature map: country to feature.

**Ans:** UK — parliamentary system, single citizenship. USA — Fundamental Rights, judicial review. Ireland — DPSP. Canada — strong Centre, residuary powers. Australia — Concurrent List. Germany — Emergency FR suspension. USSR — Fundamental Duties. GOI Act 1935 — structural skeleton. South Africa — amendment procedure.

</details>
