## Round 1 — Basic structure

**Q1.** Which Part of the Constitution deals with the Union Judiciary?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Part V, Chapter IV** (Arts. 124–147).

</details>

**Q2.** Which Articles deal with the Supreme Court?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Articles 124 to 147.**

</details>

**Q3.** Which Article establishes the Supreme Court of India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 124.** The Court was inaugurated on **28 January 1950**. First CJI: **H.J. Kania**.

</details>

**Q4.** Which Articles broadly deal with High Courts?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Articles 214 to 231** (Part VI, Chapter V). Subordinate courts: **233–237**.

</details>

**Q5.** What is meant by an integrated judicial system?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: One hierarchy** — subordinate courts → High Court → Supreme Court as the apex for Union and States. Independent is a different claim (separate from the other organs).

</details>

**Q6.** Does India have separate federal and state court systems like the USA?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** The USA is dual. India is fully integrated.

</details>

**Q7.** What is the highest court of India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The Supreme Court of India.** Seat: **Delhi** (Art. 130).

</details>

**Q8.** What is the difference between the Supreme Court, a High Court and subordinate courts?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The Supreme Court is the national apex with federal, writ, appellate, SLP and advisory jurisdiction.** A High Court is the apex of a State (or group of States) with writ and superintendence powers. Subordinate courts are district and sessions courts under High Court control (Art. 235).

</details>

**Q9.** Who appoints Supreme Court judges?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President** (Art. 124(2)), after consultation as evolved into the Collegium.

</details>

**Q10.** Who appoints High Court judges?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President** (Art. 217), after consultation with the CJI, the Governor, and the Chief Justice of the High Court.

</details>

## Round 2 — Supreme Court composition

**Q11.** What is the constitutional basis for the composition of the Supreme Court?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 124** — a Chief Justice of India and other judges as Parliament may by law prescribe. Strength is by **ordinary law**, not Article 368.

</details>

**Q12.** Who determines the sanctioned strength of Supreme Court judges?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Parliament by law.** Present strength is **34** (2019). Original strength was **8** (CJI + 7).

</details>

**Q13.** What is the minimum age for becoming a Supreme Court judge?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The Constitution does not prescribe a minimum age.** It only lists qualifications and a retirement age of **65**.

</details>

**Q14.** What qualifications are required to become a Supreme Court judge?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Citizen of India, plus one of: five years as a High Court judge, ten years as a High Court advocate, or a distinguished jurist in the President’s opinion.**

</details>

**Q15.** Can a distinguished jurist be appointed directly to the Supreme Court?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes** (Art. 124). There is **no** distinguished-jurist route to a High Court.

</details>

**Q16.** What is the retirement age of a Supreme Court judge?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 65 years.** High Court: **62**.

</details>

**Q17.** Can a retired Supreme Court judge practise law in any court in India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No** (Art. 124(7)). Practice is barred in any court or before any authority in India.

</details>

**Q18.** Can a retired Supreme Court judge be appointed to another constitutional position?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes**, to offices the Constitution or a statute allows (for example sitting under Art. 128 with consent, or other post-retirement statutory offices). Bar practice remains forbidden.

</details>

**Q19.** Who administers the oath to a Supreme Court judge?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President**, or a person appointed by the President. A High Court judge takes oath before the **Governor**.

</details>

**Q20.** Who decides the salary and allowances of Supreme Court judges?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Parliament by law** (Art. 125). Salaries are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**.

</details>

## Round 3 — Appointment

**Q21.** Who formally appoints Supreme Court judges?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President.**

</details>

**Q22.** What is the Collegium System?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A judicially evolved method in which the CJI and senior-most Supreme Court judges recommend appointments and transfers.** The word is not in the Constitution.

</details>

**Q23.** Is the word Collegium mentioned in the Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.**

</details>

**Q24.** Who is the head of the Supreme Court Collegium?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The Chief Justice of India.**

</details>

**Q25.** Who are the other members of the Supreme Court Collegium?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The four next senior-most Supreme Court judges** (Third Judges Case, 1998). For High Court appointments the SC Collegium is CJI plus two, with the High Court Collegium also involved.

</details>

**Q26.** What is the role of the Chief Justice of India in judicial appointments?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The CJI initiates and heads the Collegium.** “Consultation” with the CJI means concurrence of the Collegium, not a solo opinion.

</details>

**Q27.** What was the First Judges Case?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: S.P. Gupta (1981).** It gave the **executive primacy** in appointments. Consultation did not mean concurrence.

</details>

**Q28.** What was the significance of the Second Judges Case?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association (1993).** It created the Collegium (CJI + two) and gave **judicial primacy**.

</details>

**Q29.** What did the Third Judges Case establish?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 1998 advisory opinion.** Supreme Court Collegium = **CJI + four**. High Court Collegium path = CJI + two.

</details>

**Q30.** What is the significance of the Fourth Judges Case?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: NJAC case, 2015.** It struck down the 99th Amendment and restored the Collegium.

</details>

**Q31.** What was the National Judicial Appointments Commission?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A six-member body to appoint judges** — CJI + two senior SC judges + Law Minister + two eminent persons. Any two members could veto.

</details>

**Q32.** Which Constitutional Amendment created the NJAC?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The 99th Amendment, 2014** (Arts. 124A–C).

</details>

**Q33.** Why was the NJAC struck down?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It was held to destroy judicial independence**, which is Basic Structure, by giving the executive and eminent persons a veto.

</details>

**Q34.** Which case struck down the NJAC?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association vs Union of India (2015)** — the Fourth Judges Case.

</details>

## Round 4 — Independence

**Q35.** Why is judicial independence important?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: So judges can decide without fear of the executive or legislature**, to protect constitutional supremacy, Fundamental Rights and the federal balance.

</details>

**Q36.** What constitutional safeguards protect judicial independence?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans:** Security of tenure and a hard removal process. Charged salaries. No discussion of a judge’s conduct except on a removal motion (Arts. 121 / 211). Ban on post-retirement SC practice. Contempt power. Collegium. High Court control of subordinate courts (Art. 235). Own staff (Arts. 146 / 229). Article 50.

</details>

**Q37.** Can the executive arbitrarily remove a Supreme Court judge?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.**

</details>

**Q38.** What is the procedure for removing a Supreme Court judge?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 124(4) plus the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968.** A motion needs **100 Lok Sabha or 50 Rajya Sabha** members, a three-member inquiry, then a special-majority address by **both Houses in the same session**. The President then issues the order. No judge has ever been removed. Ramaswami (1993) failed in the Lok Sabha. Soumitra Sen (2011) passed the Rajya Sabha and then resigned.

</details>

**Q39.** On what grounds can a Supreme Court judge be removed?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Proved misbehaviour or incapacity.**

</details>

**Q40.** Who can initiate the removal process?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Members of either House of Parliament**, with the numbers required by the 1968 Act.

</details>

**Q41.** What type of majority is required in Parliament?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Special majority in each House** — a majority of total membership plus two-thirds of members present and voting.

</details>

**Q42.** Who formally removes a Supreme Court judge?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President**, after the parliamentary address.

</details>

**Q43.** Can Parliament remove a judge through an ordinary majority?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.**

</details>

**Q44.** Can the President remove a Supreme Court judge independently?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** The President cannot act without the address of both Houses.

</details>

**Q45.** What is meant by security of tenure?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A judge holds office until the constitutional age**, and can be removed only by the impeachment-style process, not at pleasure.

</details>

**Q46.** Why are salaries of judges charged on the Consolidated Fund of India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Charged expenditure is discussed, not voted**, so the annual Budget cannot be used to starve the Court.

</details>

**Q47.** Can Parliament reduce a judge’s salary after appointment?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Not to the judge’s disadvantage**, except during a **Financial Emergency** (Art. 360).

</details>

**Q48.** Why is the constitutional prohibition on discussion of judges’ conduct in Parliament important?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Articles 121 and 211 stop political debate on a sitting judge except on a removal motion**, so independence is not eroded on the floor.

</details>

## Round 5 — Supreme Court jurisdiction

**Q49.** What are the major types of jurisdiction of the Supreme Court?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Original (including federal and writ), appellate, advisory, review, and special leave.** It is also a court of record.

</details>

**Q50.** What is original jurisdiction?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Jurisdiction to hear a matter first**, not as an appeal.

</details>

**Q51.** Which Article deals with the Supreme Court’s original jurisdiction in federal disputes?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 131.** Writ original jurisdiction is **Article 32**. Election of President/VP is **Article 71**.

</details>

**Q52.** What kinds of disputes fall under Article 131?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Union vs State, or State vs State.** Not Fundamental Rights, not a private party, not a pre-Constitution treaty that excludes the Court.

</details>

**Q53.** Can a private citizen directly invoke Article 131?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.**

</details>

**Q54.** What is writ jurisdiction?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Power to issue habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto.**

</details>

**Q55.** Which Article gives the Supreme Court power to issue writs?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 32**, for Fundamental Rights only. Parliament may confer other writ purposes under Art. **139**.

</details>

**Q56.** What are the five constitutional writs?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto.**

</details>

**Q57.** What is appellate jurisdiction?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Appeals from High Courts** — constitutional (132), civil (133), criminal (134), plus certificate under 134A.

</details>

**Q58.** What is advisory jurisdiction?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President may refer a question of law or fact for the Court’s opinion** (Art. 143).

</details>

**Q59.** Which Article deals with the President’s power to seek the Supreme Court’s advisory opinion?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 143.** Under 143(1) the Court **may** decline. Under 143(2) (pre-Constitution treaties) it **shall** report.

</details>

**Q60.** Is the Supreme Court’s advisory opinion binding on the President?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** It is highly persuasive, not binding.

</details>

**Q61.** What is special leave to appeal?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A discretionary appeal the Supreme Court may grant against any court or tribunal in India**, except armed-forces courts (Art. 136).

</details>

**Q62.** Which Article gives the Supreme Court Special Leave jurisdiction?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 136.**

</details>

## Round 6 — Writs

**Q63.** What is Habeas Corpus?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: “Produce the body.”** It tests the legality of detention.

</details>

**Q64.** Against whom can Habeas Corpus be issued?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Public authorities and private persons.**

</details>

**Q65.** What does Mandamus mean?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: “We command.”** It orders a public authority to perform a public duty.

</details>

**Q66.** Against whom can Mandamus be issued?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Public authorities, government, and inferior courts**, for a public legal duty.

</details>

**Q67.** Can Mandamus be issued against a private individual?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Generally no**, unless the person is performing a public duty.

</details>

**Q68.** Can Mandamus be issued against the President?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No**, not against the President or a Governor personally.

</details>

**Q69.** What is Prohibition?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: “To forbid.”** It stops a lower court or tribunal from exceeding jurisdiction **before** the decision.

</details>

**Q70.** Against which bodies is Prohibition generally issued?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Judicial and quasi-judicial bodies**, not purely administrative or private persons.

</details>

**Q71.** What is Certiorari?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: “To be certified.”** It quashes an order **after** it is made.

</details>

**Q72.** What is the difference between Prohibition and Certiorari?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Prohibition is before the decision. Certiorari is after**, to quash.

</details>

**Q73.** What is Quo Warranto?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: “By what authority.”** It tests the legal right to hold a public office.

</details>

**Q74.** Against whom can Quo Warranto be issued?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A person who occupies a substantive public office created by law.** Any person may seek it.

</details>

**Q75.** Which writ challenges unlawful occupation of a public office?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Quo Warranto.**

</details>

## Round 7 — Supreme Court powers

**Q76.** What is judicial review?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The power of courts to test laws and executive action against the Constitution and strike down what is ultra vires.**

</details>

**Q77.** What is the constitutional basis of judicial review?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It is scattered — Arts. 13, 32, 131, 136, 143, 226, 227 — not one single Article.** It is Basic Structure.

</details>

**Q78.** Can the Supreme Court declare a parliamentary law unconstitutional?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes**, if it violates the Constitution.

</details>

**Q79.** Can the Supreme Court review constitutional amendments?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes, on the Basic Structure ground** after *Kesavananda*.

</details>

**Q80.** What is the Basic Structure Doctrine?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Parliament cannot destroy the Constitution’s basic features while using Article 368.**

</details>

**Q81.** Which case established the Basic Structure Doctrine?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Kesavananda Bharati vs State of Kerala, 1973.**

</details>

**Q82.** What is judicial activism?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A more assertive use of judicial power** — PILs, continuing mandamus, expansive Article 21 — to fill gaps where other organs have failed.

</details>

**Q83.** What is judicial restraint?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Courts stay close to the text and leave policy to the legislature and executive**, as in the narrow *Gopalan* reading of Article 21.

</details>

**Q84.** What is Public Interest Litigation?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Litigation for a public cause, with relaxed locus standi**, under Arts. 32 and 226. Not in the constitutional text. Associated with **Bhagwati** and **Krishna Iyer**.

</details>

**Q85.** Who can file a PIL?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Any public-spirited person**, not only the aggrieved (*S.P. Gupta*, 1981).

</details>

**Q86.** Can a person file a PIL for a completely private dispute?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** A PIL is for a public injury, not a private quarrel.

</details>

**Q87.** What is the significance of PIL in expanding access to justice?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It lets the poor and unrepresented reach the Court** (*Hussainara Khatoon* and later environmental and rights cases).

</details>

## Round 9 — Articles 141 and 142

**Q109.** What does Article 141 provide?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts within the territory of India.**

</details>

**Q110.** Are decisions of the Supreme Court binding on all courts in India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes.** The Supreme Court itself may overrule its earlier view.

</details>

**Q111.** What is meant by law declared by the Supreme Court?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The ratio of its judgments**, which every court in India must follow.

</details>

**Q112.** What does Article 142 empower the Supreme Court to do?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: To pass any decree or order necessary for complete justice** in a pending matter, enforceable throughout India.

</details>

**Q113.** What is meant by complete justice under Article 142?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Power to mould relief so that justice is not defeated by a gap in procedure or statute**, unique to the Supreme Court. High Courts have no equivalent Article.

</details>

**Q114.** Can Article 142 be used to override the Constitution?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.**

</details>

**Q115.** Can Article 142 be used to violate an existing statutory provision?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It cannot be used as a substitute for amending a statute or to ignore an express statutory bar** (*Supreme Court Bar Association*, 1998). It is not a parallel legislature.

</details>

## Round 10 — Review and curative

**Q116.** Which Article gives the Supreme Court power to review its own judgments?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 137.**

</details>

**Q117.** What is a review petition?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A request that the same Court reconsider its judgment**, on limited grounds such as an error apparent on the face of the record.

</details>

**Q118.** What is a curative petition?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A rare last remedy after a review is dismissed**, to cure a gross miscarriage of justice.

</details>

**Q119.** Which case established the concept of a curative petition?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Rupa Ashok Hurra vs Ashok Hurra, 2002.**

</details>

**Q120.** Why was the curative petition introduced?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: To prevent abuse of process and still correct a profound injustice** after review has failed.

</details>

**Q121.** Is a curative petition available as a matter of ordinary right?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** It is exceptional and tightly filtered.

</details>

## Round 11 — High Courts

**Q122.** Which Article establishes High Courts?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 214.** There are **25** High Courts at present.

</details>

**Q123.** Is there a High Court for every State?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 214 says there shall be a High Court for each State**, but Article **231** allows a common High Court for two or more States by **ordinary law**.

</details>

**Q124.** Can one High Court have jurisdiction over multiple States?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes** (Art. 231) — for example Punjab and Haryana.

</details>

**Q125.** Who appoints High Court judges?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President.**

</details>

**Q126.** What is the retirement age of a High Court judge?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 62 years** (raised from 60 by the 15th Amendment).

</details>

**Q127.** What qualifications are required to become a High Court judge?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Citizen of India, plus ten years in a judicial office in India or ten years as an advocate of a High Court.** There is **no** distinguished-jurist clause.

</details>

**Q128.** Can a retired High Court judge practise law in any court in India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Not in the same High Court or courts subordinate to it** (Art. 220). Practice in the Supreme Court and other High Courts is allowed.

</details>

**Q129.** Can a High Court judge be transferred?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes** (Art. 222). Consent of the judge is **not** required.

</details>

**Q130.** Who transfers High Court judges?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President**, after consultation (in practice the Collegium).

</details>

**Q131.** Who appoints the Chief Justice of a High Court?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The President.**

</details>

**Q132.** Can a High Court judge be removed by the Governor?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** Removal follows the same parliamentary process as for a Supreme Court judge (Art. 218). Resignation is to the **President**.

</details>

## Round 12 — High Court jurisdiction

**Q133.** Which Article gives High Courts writ jurisdiction?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 226.**

</details>

**Q134.** Is Article 226 wider or narrower than Article 32?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Wider.**

</details>

**Q135.** Why is Article 226 considered wider?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It covers Fundamental Rights and any other purpose** (any legal right). Article 32 is FR only. The 15th Amendment added a **cause of action** territorial test.

</details>

**Q136.** Can High Courts issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes.**

</details>

**Q137.** Can High Courts issue writs for purposes other than Fundamental Rights?

<details>

**Ans: Yes.**

</details>

**Q138.** Can the Supreme Court issue writs for purposes beyond Fundamental Rights under Article 32?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: No.** Article 32 is FR only unless Parliament uses Article **139**.

</details>

**Q139.** What is supervisory jurisdiction of High Courts?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Power to superintend all courts and tribunals in the territory**, including calling for records and making rules.

</details>

**Q140.** Which Article provides High Courts with supervisory jurisdiction?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 227.** The 44th Amendment restored full superintendence after the 42nd had cut it.

</details>

**Q141.** Can a High Court supervise subordinate courts and tribunals?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes**, except courts and tribunals of the armed forces.

</details>

## Round 14 — Tribunals

**Q149.** What is a tribunal?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A specialised adjudicatory body**, often outside the regular civil court hierarchy, for service, tax, labour and similar disputes.

</details>

**Q150.** Which Constitutional Amendment added provisions relating to tribunals?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: The 42nd Amendment, 1976** — **Part XIVA**. Tribunals are **not** Part XI.

</details>

**Q151.** Which Articles deal with tribunals?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Articles 323A and 323B.**

</details>

**Q152.** What is the difference between an Administrative Tribunal and a judicial court?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A court is a regular judicial forum under the High Court.** An administrative tribunal is a specialised first-instance body for service disputes, still subject to High Court review under 226/227.

</details>

**Q153.** Which Article deals with Administrative Tribunals?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 323A.** Only **Parliament** may create them. CAT was set up in **1985**.

</details>

**Q154.** Which Article deals with tribunals for other matters?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Article 323B.** Parliament **or** a State legislature may create them (tax, labour, land, elections to the legislature, and listed heads).

</details>

**Q155.** What is the role of the Central Administrative Tribunal?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: It is the first-instance forum for Union civil-service disputes.** It is not a substitute for the High Court.

</details>

**Q156.** Can tribunal decisions be reviewed by High Courts?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Yes**, under Arts. 226 and 227.

</details>

**Q157.** Which case established that tribunals are subject to High Court judicial review?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: L. Chandra Kumar vs Union of India, 1997.** High Court power over tribunals is Basic Structure. *Sampath Kumar* (1987) had treated CAT as a substitute; that was overruled.

</details>

## Round 15 — Doctrines

**Q158.** What is the Doctrine of Basic Structure?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Core features cannot be destroyed by amendment** (*Kesavananda*, 1973).

</details>

**Q159.** What is the Doctrine of Severability?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Only the invalid part of a law is struck down** if it can be separated from the rest.

</details>

**Q160.** What is the Doctrine of Eclipse?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A pre-Constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right is dormant, not dead**, and may revive if the conflict is later removed.

</details>

**Q161.** What is the Doctrine of Pith and Substance?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A law is judged by its true nature and character.** Incidental encroachment on another list does not make it invalid.

</details>

**Q162.** What is the Doctrine of Colourable Legislation?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: What cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly.** The legislature cannot dress an ultra vires law as if it were intra vires.

</details>

**Q163.** What is the Doctrine of Repugnancy?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: On a Concurrent List matter, Union law prevails over inconsistent State law** (Art. 254), unless the State law has Presidential assent and Parliament has not occupied the field again.

</details>

**Q164.** What is the Doctrine of Territorial Nexus?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A State law may have extra-territorial effect if there is a real connection** between the State and the subject matter.

</details>

**Q165.** What is Prospective Overruling?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: A new constitutional ruling applies only to the future**, so past transactions are not unsettled.

</details>

**Q166.** Which case introduced prospective overruling in India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: Golaknath vs State of Punjab, 1967** (borrowed from US practice).

</details>

## Round 16 — Landmark cases

**Q167.** What was the significance of A.K. Gopalan vs State of Madras?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 1950 — a narrow reading of Article 21.** “Procedure established by law” meant any procedure enacted by the legislature. Articles 14, 19 and 21 were read in silos.

</details>

**Q168.** What was the significance of Maneka Gandhi vs Union of India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 1978 — procedure must be fair, just and reasonable.** Articles 14, 19 and 21 form a golden triangle. *Gopalan* was displaced.

</details>

**Q169.** What happened in Kesavananda Bharati vs State of Kerala?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 1973 — Parliament can amend any part, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot destroy the Basic Structure.**

</details>

**Q170.** What was the significance of Golaknath vs State of Punjab?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 1967 — Parliament cannot amend Fundamental Rights.** An amendment was treated as “law” under Article 13. Prospective overruling was used. The 24th Amendment and *Kesavananda* later changed the amendment rule.

</details>

**Q171.** What did Minerva Mills vs Union of India establish?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 1980 — limited amending power and judicial review are Basic Structure.** Harmony of Fundamental Rights and DPSPs.

</details>

**Q172.** What was the significance of S.R. Bommai vs Union of India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 1994 — Article 356 is justiciable.** Secularism and federalism are Basic Structure. Floor test, not the Governor’s subjective report, is the real test of majority.

</details>

**Q173.** What was established in Indra Sawhney vs Union of India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 1992 — 27% OBC reservation; 50% ceiling; creamy layer excluded; no reservation in promotion** (later qualified by amendments for SC/ST). Article 16(4) is enabling, not a Fundamental Right of any class.

</details>

**Q174.** What was the significance of I.R. Coelho vs State of Tamil Nadu?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 2007 — laws put in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be tested on Basic Structure.**

</details>

**Q175.** What did the NJAC case decide?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 2015 — the 99th Amendment and NJAC Act were struck down.** Collegium restored.

</details>

**Q176.** What was the significance of Vishaka vs State of Rajasthan?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 1997 — guidelines against workplace sexual harassment until a statute was enacted.** An example of judicial activism filling a legislative gap. POSH Act followed in 2013.

</details>

**Q177.** What was the significance of K.S. Puttaswamy vs Union of India?

<details>
<summary>Show answer</summary>

**Ans: 2017 — privacy is a Fundamental Right under Article 21.**

</details>
