## Content

### Context — poverty and hunger as social justice questions

Poverty is not only low income. It is the absence of capabilities, assets, education, health, nutrition, secure work, social dignity and voice. Hunger is not only absence of grain. It includes inadequate calorie intake, poor diet diversity, child and maternal malnutrition, anaemia, weak food absorption due to disease and sanitation, and inability to access food during shocks. Therefore, poverty and hunger are capability failures as much as income failures.

The constitutional basis lies in **Article 21** as dignified life, **Article 38** as reduction of inequalities, **Article 39(a)** as adequate livelihood, **Article 41** as public assistance in unemployment and want, and **Article 47** as duty of the state to raise nutrition and public health. The **National Food Security Act, 2013**, **MGNREGA 2005**, **ICDS**, **Poshan Abhiyaan**, **PM POSHAN**, **PMAY**, **NRLM**, **PM-KISAN** and **Ayushman Bharat** are policy responses to this constitutional welfare obligation.

### Causes of poverty and hunger

Poverty and hunger arise from overlapping causes. Landlessness, caste exclusion, gender inequality, disability, poor education and low health status reduce people's ability to earn. Informal work and low wages keep households vulnerable even when employed. Agrarian distress, small holdings, debt and climate shocks affect rural income. Urban poor face insecure housing, casual work and high living costs. Inflation reduces real purchasing power. Regional imbalance means some districts lag in schools, health centres, roads and jobs.

Hunger has additional causes. A household may have cereal access but still suffer from malnutrition due to low protein, low vegetables, poor sanitation, disease, unsafe water, poor maternal health and lack of nutrition knowledge. Child stunting and wasting are linked to maternal nutrition, breastfeeding, immunisation, sanitation and health care. Thus, food security must include **availability**, **access**, **affordability**, **diet diversity** and **absorption**.

| Cause | Poverty link | Hunger/nutrition link |
|-------|--------------|-----------------------|
| **Landlessness and low assets** | Low income and debt dependence. | Low purchasing power for food diversity. |
| **Informal employment** | No job security or social protection. | Food intake falls during unemployment/shocks. |
| **Caste/gender exclusion** | Limited education, work and wages. | Women/children eat last or less in some households. |
| **Poor health** | Medical expenditure pushes families into poverty. | Disease reduces nutrient absorption. |
| **Low education** | Poor skills and low productivity. | Low nutrition awareness and poor child care. |
| **Climate and disasters** | Crop loss, migration and debt. | Food supply and incomes disrupted. |
| **Governance gaps** | Scheme exclusion and leakage. | PDS/ICDS/Poshan benefits fail at last mile. |

### Public Distribution System and poverty alleviation

The **Public Distribution System (PDS)** distributes foodgrains through fair price shops. Under the **National Food Security Act, 2013**, priority households and **Antyodaya Anna Yojana** households are entitled to subsidised foodgrains. During and after COVID-19, **Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana (PMGKAY)** provided free foodgrains to a very large population. **One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC)** improved portability for migrant workers.

PDS contributes to poverty alleviation in five ways. First, it reduces food expenditure, increasing real income for poor households. Second, it prevents hunger during inflation, unemployment and crisis. Third, it stabilises prices by maintaining public grain distribution. Fourth, Antyodaya prioritises the poorest households. Fifth, digitisation, e-POS and ONORC have improved transparency and portability in many states.

However, PDS is not a complete poverty solution. It is cereal-heavy and does not automatically solve protein, micronutrient or diet diversity gaps. Exclusion errors occur when eligible households lack ration cards or biometric authentication fails. Fair price shop quality varies. Migrants still face portability and awareness problems. Leakages have declined in many places but not disappeared. Therefore, PDS is a strong hunger-prevention tool and indirect income support, but poverty eradication also needs jobs, health, education, housing and nutrition diversification.

### India's progress on hunger and poverty after 2014

India has made significant progress in multidimensional poverty reduction after 2014. According to NITI Aayog's multidimensional poverty estimates, around **24.82 crore people escaped multidimensional poverty between 2013-14 and 2022-23**, and the multidimensional poverty headcount ratio declined substantially. Improvements came from sanitation, housing, cooking fuel, electricity, bank accounts, health insurance, foodgrain distribution and direct benefit delivery.

Major policy drivers include **Jan Dhan-Aadhaar-Mobile** financial inclusion, **DBT**, **Swachh Bharat Mission**, **PMAY**, **Ujjwala**, **Saubhagya**, **Jal Jeevan Mission**, **Ayushman Bharat**, **PM-KISAN**, **MGNREGA**, **DAY-NRLM**, **PM POSHAN**, **Poshan Abhiyaan**, **NFSA/PDS**, **PMGKAY** and **ONORC**. These schemes reduced deprivation in housing, sanitation, bank access, cooking fuel and food availability.

Progress on hunger and nutrition is more mixed. Foodgrain access improved, especially through PMGKAY and PDS portability. But child stunting, wasting, anaemia, diet diversity, maternal nutrition and obesity-under-nutrition double burden remain concerns. India's ranking in the Global Hunger Index has been debated by the government due to methodology issues, but nutrition indicators from domestic surveys still show unfinished work. Poverty reduction is visible, yet unemployment, informal work, regional inequality and food price inflation continue to affect poor households.

### Government schemes to eliminate poverty and hunger

| Scheme/law | Main target | Poverty/hunger role |
|------------|-------------|--------------------|
| **NFSA/PDS** | Poor and priority households. | Provides subsidised/free foodgrains. |
| **Antyodaya Anna Yojana** | Poorest of the poor. | Higher foodgrain entitlement for most vulnerable households. |
| **PMGKAY** | Food security during and after crisis. | Free foodgrains reduce hunger risk. |
| **MGNREGA** | Rural poor. | Wage employment and local assets. |
| **ICDS/Anganwadi** | Children, pregnant and lactating mothers. | Supplementary nutrition and early childhood care. |
| **Poshan Abhiyaan** | Nutrition outcomes. | Convergence and monitoring for malnutrition reduction. |
| **PM POSHAN** | School children. | Mid-day meal, attendance and child nutrition. |
| **PMAY-G/U** | Rural and urban poor. | Housing as asset and dignity. |
| **DAY-NRLM/SHGs** | Rural poor women. | Livelihood, credit and enterprise. |
| **PM-KISAN/KCC/PMFBY** | Small farmers. | Income, credit and risk support. |
| **Ayushman Bharat/PMJAY** | Poor and vulnerable households. | Reduces catastrophic health expenditure. |
| **ONORC** | Migrants and mobile poor. | Ration portability across states. |

The strongest anti-poverty strategy is convergence. A poor household needs food, work, health, education, housing, drinking water, sanitation, credit and social security together. Fragmented delivery reduces impact.

### Poverty, hunger and body politic

Poverty and hunger have direct implications for body politic, meaning the political life and stability of the state. They influence voting behaviour, party manifestos, welfare promises, social coalitions, protest movements, trust in the state and legitimacy of democracy. Food, jobs and income are not only economic issues; they are political issues because they determine whether citizens feel protected by the state.

In electoral politics, parties promise free/subsidised foodgrains, cash transfers, farm loan waivers, unemployment allowance, MSP support, pensions, housing, LPG, electricity and health insurance because vulnerable voters prioritise livelihood security. Welfare delivery can create political trust when citizens receive benefits without middlemen. At the same time, poverty can fuel clientelism, vote-bank targeting and competitive populism. Hunger can trigger protests, migration distress and social unrest. The debate over **freebies vs welfare rights** reflects this tension: food security and work rights protect dignity, but fiscally irresponsible promises may harm long-term development.

Poverty and hunger also affect democratic participation. Poor citizens may vote in high numbers but may lack time, education or security to influence policy beyond elections. Malnutrition and poor education weaken citizenship capabilities. Therefore, poverty elimination strengthens democracy by making citizens less dependent, more informed and more capable of participating.

### Main issues related to poverty and hunger

The main issues are persistent deprivation, nutrition insecurity, working poverty, inequality and vulnerability to shocks. Many households move above the poverty line but fall back due to illness, job loss, crop failure or debt. Informal workers may be employed but poor. Women and children face intra-household nutrition inequality. Urban poverty is hidden in slums and informal settlements. Tribal and remote regions face access barriers. Climate change threatens crops and food prices. Digital welfare systems can reduce leakage but create new exclusion.

The policy challenge is to move from calorie security to nutrition security, from temporary relief to productive employment, from scheme coverage to outcomes, and from centralised databases to accountable local delivery.

### Contemporary relevance

- **PMGKAY and NFSA** have made foodgrain security politically and socially central after COVID-19.
- **NITI Aayog's MPI estimates** show progress in multidimensional poverty reduction, but nutrition indicators require continued focus.
- **ONORC** is crucial for migrant workers and urban poor portability.
- **Poshan Tracker, ICDS and PM POSHAN** connect hunger policy with maternal-child nutrition.
- **MGNREGA** remains important for climate resilience and rural wage security.
- **DBT and Jan Dhan** improve welfare delivery but require grievance correction for failed payments.
- **Food inflation and unemployment** keep poverty politically sensitive despite welfare expansion.
- **Freebies vs welfare debate** makes poverty and hunger central to fiscal and electoral politics.

### Limits — balanced view

India has reduced many visible deprivations, but poverty and hunger remain complex. Foodgrain distribution protects against starvation but cannot ensure balanced nutrition. Cash transfers help income but cannot replace public health and education. Growth creates resources but does not automatically reach informal workers. Welfare politics can empower voters but can also become short-term populism. The next stage must combine food security, nutrition, jobs, human capital and accountable welfare delivery.

### Q1: Review the contribution of the public distribution system in poverty alleviation. (2024, 8M, 125W)

**Introduction:**
> The Public Distribution System provides subsidised/free foodgrains to poor households under NFSA and related schemes. It contributes to poverty alleviation by reducing food expenditure and hunger risk.

**Main Points:**
- **Food Security** — PDS ensures minimum cereal availability for poor households.
- **Real Income Support** — Lower food spending leaves income for health, education and other needs.
- **Crisis Protection** — PMGKAY protected households during COVID and post-crisis stress.
- **Poorest Focus** — Antyodaya targets the poorest households with higher support.
- **Price Stabilisation** — Public grain distribution reduces vulnerability to food inflation.
- **Migrant Support** — ONORC improves portability of ration benefits.
- **Limitations** — Exclusion, cereal bias, biometric failure and fair price shop quality reduce impact.

**Keywords (underline in exam):** PDS · NFSA · Antyodaya · PMGKAY · ONORC · Food Security · Poverty Alleviation


**Conclusion:**

### Q2: What is India's progress on hunger and poverty after 2014? Explain. (2024, 12M, 200W)

**Introduction:**
> After 2014, India has expanded welfare delivery and reduced multidimensional poverty, but hunger and nutrition outcomes remain an unfinished agenda.

**Progress:**
- **MPI Reduction** — NITI Aayog estimates around 24.82 crore people escaped multidimensional poverty between 2013-14 and 2022-23.
- **Food Security Expansion** — NFSA, PMGKAY and PDS portability protected poor households from hunger shocks.
- **Financial Inclusion** — Jan Dhan, Aadhaar and DBT improved delivery of benefits.
- **Basic Amenities** — SBM, PMAY, Ujjwala, Saubhagya and Jal Jeevan Mission reduced non-income deprivation.
- **Health Protection** — Ayushman Bharat/PMJAY reduced catastrophic hospitalisation risk for eligible households.
- **Rural Livelihoods** — MGNREGA, DAY-NRLM and PM-KISAN supported income security.
- **Nutrition Focus** — Poshan Abhiyaan, ICDS and PM POSHAN target maternal-child nutrition.

**Concerns:**
- **Nutrition Gap** — Stunting, wasting, anaemia and diet diversity remain concerns.
- **Employment Quality** — Informal work and unemployment keep households vulnerable.
- **Regional Inequality** — Some states and districts lag behind national progress.
- **Inflation/Shocks** — Food prices, climate and health shocks can push families back into poverty.
- **Measurement Debate** — GHI ranking is contested, but domestic nutrition indicators still need improvement.

**Keywords (underline in exam):** Multidimensional Poverty · NFSA · PMGKAY · DBT · Poshan Abhiyaan · MGNREGA · Nutrition Security

**Conclusion:**
