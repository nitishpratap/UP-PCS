## Content

### Context — why neighbours matter most

India's neighbourhood is the first circle of its foreign policy because geography makes security, trade, migration, water, culture and connectivity inseparable. A stable neighbourhood allows India to focus on development and global leadership; an unstable neighbourhood diverts resources to borders, terrorism, refugees, maritime threats and crisis diplomacy. India's approach combines **Neighbourhood First**, **SAGAR** in the Indian Ocean, **Act East**, **BIMSTEC**, **BBIN**, development partnership, disaster relief, defence cooperation and people-to-people ties.

The neighbourhood is also where India's rise is most tested. Smaller neighbours welcome India's market, aid, health services, education and disaster response, but they also fear dominance. China uses infrastructure, debt, trade, diplomacy and security partnerships to expand influence in South Asia and the Indian Ocean. Therefore, India must combine sensitivity with firmness: respect sovereignty, deliver projects on time, avoid big-brother attitudes, and protect core security interests.

### Neighbourhood policy framework

| Framework | Meaning | Neighbourhood relevance |
|-----------|---------|-------------------------|
| **Neighbourhood First** | Priority to immediate neighbours in diplomacy and development. | Grants, credit lines, connectivity, HADR, capacity building. |
| **SAGAR** | Security and Growth for All in the Region. | Indian Ocean cooperation with Sri Lanka, Maldives and island states. |
| **BIMSTEC** | Bay of Bengal regional grouping. | Connects India with Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, Myanmar and Thailand. |
| **BBIN** | Bangladesh-Bhutan-India-Nepal cooperation. | Transport, energy and sub-regional connectivity. |
| **Act East** | Links Northeast India with Southeast Asia. | Bangladesh and Myanmar connectivity become strategic. |
| **HADR** | Humanitarian Assistance and Disaster Relief. | Maldives water crisis, Sri Lanka crisis, Nepal earthquake, COVID support. |
| **Development partnership** | Grants, lines of credit, infrastructure and training. | Builds goodwill and capacity in Afghanistan, Nepal, Bangladesh, Maldives, Sri Lanka. |

India's core challenge is to convert cultural proximity into trusted state-to-state relations. Goodwill can weaken if projects are delayed, if domestic politics in neighbours uses anti-India nationalism, or if China offers faster finance without political conditions.

### Sri Lanka — geopolitical significance

Sri Lanka sits close to India's southern coast across the Palk Strait and near major Indian Ocean sea lanes connecting West Asia, Africa, Europe and East Asia. This makes it vital for India's maritime security, energy imports and SAGAR vision. The island lies near the approaches to the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea; hostile influence there could threaten India's southern flank.

Sri Lanka also matters because of the Tamil ethnic question, fishermen disputes, Buddhist-cultural links, trade, tourism, energy, and Indian Ocean diplomacy. The **Hambantota port lease to China** and Chinese presence in Colombo Port City created Indian concerns about strategic encirclement. During Sri Lanka's economic crisis, India provided major assistance through credit lines, fuel, food, medicines and currency support, reinforcing India's role as first responder.

For India, Sri Lanka is therefore not just a small neighbour. It is a maritime partner, cultural neighbour, Tamil-linked domestic political issue, and a test case for balancing Chinese influence through timely and respectful assistance.

### Maldives — mutual interests

India-Maldives relations are linked to mutual interests. Maldives sits along key Indian Ocean sea lanes and is close to India's Lakshadweep and western coast. Maritime security, piracy prevention, terrorism control, HADR, climate vulnerability and tourism connect the two. India provides defence training, coastal surveillance, medical evacuation, infrastructure support, capacity building and disaster relief. Maldives depends on India for many essential services; India depends on Maldives for a secure maritime neighbourhood.

The relationship faces periodic domestic political sensitivities, including sovereignty debates and the China factor. Projects and defence cooperation can become controversial in Maldivian politics. India's approach must therefore be transparent, demand-driven and respectful while protecting maritime security interests.

### Bangladesh — cooperation and disputes

India-Bangladesh relations are among India's most successful neighbourhood stories after 2009, but disputes remain. Cooperation includes the 1971 liberation legacy, **Land Boundary Agreement 2015**, security cooperation against Northeast insurgents, power trade, connectivity, rail-road links, coastal shipping, energy pipelines, and growing trade. Bangladesh is central to India's Northeast connectivity and Bay of Bengal strategy.

Major disputes include **Teesta water sharing**, other transboundary river management, border killings, illegal migration perceptions, trade imbalance, non-tariff barriers, Rohingya spillover, smuggling, and Bangladesh's concerns over India's domestic debates such as NRC/CAA. China is also a factor through infrastructure and defence supplies. Improvement requires early Teesta settlement, humane border management, balanced trade, faster connectivity, river basin cooperation, people-to-people links and respect for domestic sensitivities.

| Issue | Why it matters | Way forward |
|-------|----------------|-------------|
| **Teesta water** | Affects agriculture and trust in Bangladesh. | Centre-state consensus with West Bengal; basin approach. |
| **Border killings** | Creates public resentment. | Non-lethal border management and joint patrolling. |
| **Migration/NRC perception** | Politically sensitive in both countries. | Legal clarity, development cooperation and careful language. |
| **Trade imbalance** | Bangladesh seeks fair market access. | Remove non-tariff barriers and improve logistics. |
| **Rohingya issue** | Security and humanitarian pressure. | India-Bangladesh-Myanmar coordination and humanitarian support. |
| **China factor** | Strategic competition in infrastructure. | Timely Indian projects and quality connectivity. |

### Pakistan — why relations repeatedly sour

India-Pakistan relations remain trapped by structural problems. The most important is cross-border terrorism, especially after attacks such as Parliament 2001, Mumbai 2008, Pathankot 2016, Uri 2016 and Pulwama 2019. Kashmir remains a core political dispute for Pakistan and a sovereignty/security issue for India. The Pakistan military establishment has often shaped India policy, limiting civilian flexibility. LoC firing, infiltration, radical groups, propaganda and mistrust repeatedly derail talks.

Water rhetoric around the Indus Waters Treaty, trade suspension, visa restrictions and weak people-to-people contact deepen distance. Dialogue often appears promising but collapses after terror incidents or domestic political pressure; hence the phrase that relations are an illusion captures the gap between diplomatic statements and ground reality. The way forward is terror-free environment, LoC stability, humanitarian channels, trade where possible, and backchannel communication, but India cannot ignore security red lines.

### Nepal — contentions and China factor

India-Nepal ties are civilisational, open-border and people-centric. The 1950 Treaty, open border, Gurkha recruitment, trade/transit dependence, hydropower, pilgrimage, marriages and migrant labour create a unique relationship. Yet irritants recur: Nepal's demand to revise the **1950 Treaty**, perception of Indian interference, trade and transit dependence, border disputes at **Kalapani, Lipulekh and Limpiyadhura**, Susta issue, Madhesi representation, hydropower project delays, and anti-India nationalism.

The China factor has grown through infrastructure, BRI, political outreach, trade/transit alternatives and symbolic balancing. Nepal uses China to diversify options, while China gains strategic presence near the Himalayas. India must respond not by coercion but by faster project delivery, respectful diplomacy, border dialogue, energy cooperation, educational-health links and subnational connectivity.

### Afghanistan — soft power and US withdrawal

India's Afghanistan policy has relied heavily on soft power because India lacks direct land access and wants goodwill among Afghan people. India built the **Afghan Parliament building**, **Salma Dam/India-Afghanistan Friendship Dam**, **Zaranj-Delaram highway**, schools, hospitals, power lines and community projects. It provided scholarships, medical visas, wheat, medicines and training. This built goodwill and countered Pakistan's narrative.

The US withdrawal and Taliban return affected India by increasing terrorism concerns, empowering Pakistan-backed networks, threatening Indian projects, reducing diplomatic space, and complicating connectivity to Central Asia. At the same time, India continues humanitarian assistance because Afghan people remain important. India's interests include preventing terror safe havens, protecting minorities, maintaining regional presence, using Chabahar route where possible, and coordinating with Iran, Russia, Central Asia and global partners.

### China — LAC, OBOR and structural competition

India-China relations combine trade interdependence and strategic competition. Informal summits at Wuhan and Mamallapuram reduced tension temporarily, but they did not resolve structural issues. The LAC remains undemarcated; differing perceptions lead to face-offs. China has built infrastructure near the border; India has also improved border roads and military logistics. China's assertive rise, Tibet factor, Pakistan partnership, CPEC, Indian Ocean presence and trade imbalance create mistrust.

OBOR/BRI deepened India's concerns because the **China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC)** passes through Pakistan-occupied Kashmir, violating India's sovereignty claims. India also worries about debt dependence, strategic ports, lack of transparency, environmental standards and Chinese influence in South Asia. Thus, India-China ties are competitive, cautious and issue-based: cooperation in trade/climate/multilateral platforms coexists with rivalry at the border and in the Indo-Pacific.

### Contemporary relevance

- **Sri Lanka's economic crisis assistance** strengthened India's first-responder image and countered Chinese leverage.
- **Maldives political shifts** show why India must combine security cooperation with sensitivity to sovereignty narratives.
- **Bangladesh connectivity** remains vital for India's Northeast and BIMSTEC/BBIN strategy.
- **Nepal map dispute and China outreach** keep border and trust issues active.
- **Afghanistan under Taliban** keeps terrorism, humanitarian assistance and regional coordination central.
- **India-China LAC tensions after 2020** make border infrastructure, deterrence and diplomatic-military talks crucial.
- **BRI/CPEC** remains India's central sovereignty objection to Chinese connectivity strategy.
- **Neighbourhood First** succeeds only when projects are timely, visible and locally trusted.

### Limits — balanced view

India cannot rely only on history, culture or size in its neighbourhood. Smaller neighbours seek autonomy and external options. China offers finance and speed. Domestic politics in neighbours often uses India as an issue. India's own domestic debates can affect bilateral trust. Therefore, India must combine strategic firmness with empathy, faster project delivery, border sensitivity, economic integration, climate cooperation, and people-centred diplomacy.
