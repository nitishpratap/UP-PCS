## Content

### Context — external policies and India's interests

India's national interest is affected not only by its own policy but also by the policies and politics of developed and developing countries. US visa rules affect Indian professionals. Gulf labour policies affect Indian workers and remittances. West Asian conflicts affect energy and diaspora safety. China's rise affects borders, trade and the Indian Ocean. Climate policies of developed countries affect trade and finance. UN and WHO decisions affect global legitimacy, health and security. Therefore, IR Topic 4 asks the student to connect foreign policy with domestic interests and diaspora protection.

The Indian diaspora is central to this topic. It includes NRIs, PIOs and OCI cardholders across the US, Gulf, UK, Canada, Africa, Southeast Asia and Australia. The diaspora contributes through remittances, investment, technology, political influence, philanthropy, cultural diplomacy and crisis linkages. But diaspora also creates responsibility: India must protect migrant workers, students, professionals and vulnerable citizens abroad during war, pandemics, racial attacks, job loss and immigration policy shifts.

### Diaspora policy — engage, enable and protect

India's diaspora policy has moved from distance to active engagement. Earlier, India feared dual loyalty and brain drain; now diaspora is treated as a strategic asset. The policy has three pillars: **engage** diaspora emotionally and culturally, **enable** investment and knowledge links, and **protect** overseas Indians in crisis.

| Policy tool | Purpose |
|-------------|---------|
| **Pravasi Bharatiya Divas** | Symbolic and policy engagement with overseas Indians. |
| **OCI card** | Long-term visa and cultural-economic connection. |
| **e-Migrate portal** | Protects emigrant workers in notified countries. |
| **MADAD portal** | Consular grievance redressal. |
| **Pravasi Bharatiya Bima Yojana** | Insurance support for vulnerable emigrant workers. |
| **Know India Programme** | Connects young diaspora with Indian culture. |
| **Evacuation operations** | Vande Bharat, Operation Ganga, Operation Kaveri and other crisis responses. |
| **Diaspora diplomacy** | Uses community networks for technology, investment and political goodwill. |

The diaspora in the United States is especially valuable. Indian-Americans are highly visible in technology, medicine, academia, business, start-ups, politics and public life. They support India-US relations through lobbying, opinion-making, investment, philanthropy, university links, innovation networks and political representation. Their presence helps India's national interest by improving India's image, supporting strategic partnership, helping technology transfer and building constituencies for India-friendly policies.

Challenges remain. US visa rules, H-1B uncertainty, green card backlogs, racism, hate crimes, campus safety, technology restrictions and domestic polarisation affect Indian diaspora. In the Gulf, labour exploitation, passport confiscation, wage delay, heat stress, job nationalisation and conflict risk affect workers. In conflict zones, evacuation and legal protection become urgent. India's diaspora policy must therefore combine pride with protection.

### SCO declaration and cross-border terrorism

India has long argued that terrorism cannot be justified by ideology, religion, grievance or state policy. It emphasises cross-border terrorism, terror financing, safe havens, radicalisation and double standards. A declaration by a major forum like the **Shanghai Cooperation Organization (SCO)** endorsing action against cross-border terrorism is strategically significant because the SCO includes Eurasian powers and countries where India's concerns must be heard.

The importance is threefold. First, it internationalises India's language on terrorism in a multilateral platform. Second, it pressures states to avoid selective approaches to terrorism. Third, it helps India frame terrorism as a regional security threat, not merely an India-Pakistan bilateral complaint. However, SCO operates by consensus and includes countries with divergent interests; therefore, declaration language matters only if followed by action against financing, safe havens and extremist networks.

### UNSC reform and UN peace-security limits

The **UNSC** still reflects the 1945 power structure: five permanent members with veto and ten elected non-permanent members. It does not reflect contemporary population, economy, decolonisation, Global South and emerging power realities. India argues that a Council without permanent representation for India, Africa and Latin America lacks legitimacy. India's claim rests on population, democracy, economy, peacekeeping contribution, responsible nuclear conduct, Global South leadership and G4 reform diplomacy.

The UN has also struggled to maintain peace and security in conflicts such as Syria, Ukraine and Gaza because the veto blocks consensus when P5 interests clash. Earlier failures like Rwanda and Srebrenica showed limits in preventing mass atrocities. Yet calling the UN a total failure is one-sided. It provides humanitarian assistance, peacekeeping, sanctions frameworks, legitimacy, mediation, development agencies and international norms. The correct view is that the UN is indispensable but unreformed and often paralysed by great-power politics.

### WHO structure and funding

The **World Health Organization** is a UN specialised agency headquartered in Geneva. Its main organs are the **World Health Assembly (WHA)**, **Executive Board** and **Secretariat** headed by the Director-General. It also has six regional offices. WHA is the decision-making body of member states; the Executive Board gives effect to WHA decisions; the Secretariat provides technical and administrative work.

WHO funding comes from **assessed contributions** paid by member states and **voluntary contributions** from states, foundations and other donors. Assessed contributions are flexible, while voluntary contributions are often earmarked. This creates a funding challenge: WHO's priorities may be shaped by donor preferences, and its emergency capacity depends on predictable finance. COVID showed WHO's importance but also its limited enforcement power.

### West Asia — energy, Abraham Accords and US-Iran tension

West Asia affects India through oil and gas, diaspora, remittances, Chabahar, trade, shipping and strategic balancing. India must maintain ties with Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Oman, Iran, Israel and Palestine simultaneously. The **Abraham Accords** normalised relations between Israel and some Arab states, especially UAE and Bahrain, creating space for Israel-Gulf economic and security cooperation. For India, this supports formats like **I2U2** and connectivity ideas, but the Palestinian issue remains unresolved.

US-Iran tensions affect India's oil diversification, Chabahar connectivity, Gulf diaspora safety, shipping insurance, rupee payment mechanisms and relations with the US and Gulf states. India's response should be strategic autonomy, energy diversification, support for de-escalation, protection of diaspora and preservation of Chabahar for Afghanistan/Central Asia access.

### Climate change and developing countries

Developing countries face the harshest climate impacts despite lower historical emissions. Agriculture, water, health, coastal zones, migration and public finance are affected. Heat waves reduce labour productivity; floods and droughts damage crops; sea-level rise threatens coasts; disease burdens increase; and adaptation diverts funds from poverty reduction. India supports **Common but Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR)**, climate finance, technology transfer and climate justice. Developed-country policies such as carbon border measures can affect Indian exports unless handled through negotiation and domestic green transition.

### UP, labour migration and attracting MNCs

During the pandemic, reverse migration exposed Uttar Pradesh's dependence on migrant labour and the need to create local jobs. UP tried to attract multinational companies and investment through investor summits, industrial corridors, land banks, single-window **Nivesh Mitra**, labour skill mapping, **ODOP**, Defence Industrial Corridor, electronics manufacturing, data centres, expressways and logistics infrastructure. The aim was to convert labour availability into manufacturing and service-sector opportunity.

The effort is useful but not sufficient by itself. MNCs require law and order, reliable power, skilled labour, logistics, contract enforcement, urban services and stable policy. Migrant workers need skilling, housing, social security and decent work. Otherwise investment attraction may not translate into inclusive employment.

### Afghanistan after US withdrawal

US withdrawal from Afghanistan affected India by enabling Taliban return, increasing terrorism concerns, strengthening Pakistan's leverage, threatening Indian development projects and reducing diplomatic space. India has continued humanitarian aid, including wheat and medicines, while maintaining security vigilance. India's interest is to prevent terror safe havens, protect minorities, preserve goodwill and coordinate with regional powers.

### Contemporary relevance

- **SCO declarations on terrorism** are useful for India's global campaign against cross-border terrorism but need implementation.
- **UNSC reform** remains central to India's global leadership claim after G20 and Global South outreach.
- **WHO funding reform** is important after COVID because earmarked voluntary funding constrains institutional autonomy.
- **Indian-American influence** is rising in technology, academia, business and politics, strengthening India-US ties.
- **Gulf labour reforms and nationalisation policies** affect Indian workers and remittances.
- **Israel-Hamas conflict and US-Iran tension** test India's balanced West Asia policy.
- **Climate finance and carbon border measures** affect India's exports and development space.
- **UP's investment push** shows how global supply-chain shifts can be linked with domestic employment.

### Limits — balanced view

India benefits from diaspora networks, multilateral platforms and strategic partnerships, but external politics can quickly create risks. Diaspora influence cannot replace formal diplomacy. UNSC reform remains slow despite India's strong claim. SCO declarations may not change state behaviour. WHO guidance depends on member cooperation. West Asia balancing is difficult during conflict. Climate justice demands may not yield adequate finance. Therefore, India needs resilient diplomacy, diversified partnerships, domestic capacity and credible diaspora protection.

### Q3: Describe the organisational structure and funding of WHO. (2024, 8M, 125W)

**Introduction:**
> WHO is the UN specialised agency for global health, headquartered in Geneva. Its structure combines member-state decision-making with technical secretariat work.

**Main Points:**
- **World Health Assembly** — Supreme decision-making body of all member states.
- **Executive Board** — Implements WHA decisions and provides technical direction.
- **Secretariat** — Headed by Director-General and manages programmes.
- **Regional Offices** — Six regional offices support decentralised health work.
- **Mandate** — Disease surveillance, standards, emergency response and technical guidance.
- **Assessed Contributions** — Mandatory member-state payments, flexible in use.
- **Voluntary Contributions** — Funds from states and donors, often earmarked.
- **Funding Concern** — Earmarked voluntary funds can distort WHO priorities.

**Keywords (underline in exam):** WHO · WHA · Executive Board · Director-General · Regional Offices · Assessed Contributions · Voluntary Funding

**Exam diagram (30 sec):**
```
WHO = WHA + Executive Board + Secretariat + Regional Offices
Funding = assessed + voluntary
```

**Conclusion:**
> WHO's structure is universal, but predictable and flexible funding is needed for stronger global health action.

### Q4: UNO has failed in maintaining international peace and security. Describe with examples. (2024, 12M, 200W)

**Introduction:**
> The UN was created to maintain international peace and security, but its record is mixed because great-power politics often blocks collective action.

**Evidence of Failure:**
- **Veto Paralysis** — UNSC action is blocked when P5 interests clash.
- **Ukraine War** — UN could not prevent or stop major war involving a P5 member.
- **Syria Conflict** — Repeated vetoes weakened humanitarian and peace efforts.
- **Gaza Crisis** — Deep divisions limited timely consensus and enforcement.
- **Rwanda/Srebrenica Legacy** — UN failed to prevent mass atrocities.
- **Terrorism Definition Gap** — No universal convention on terrorism due to political disagreement.
- **Peacekeeping Limits** — Missions often lack mandate, resources or consent.

**Balanced View:**
- **Humanitarian Role** — UN agencies provide relief, food, refugees and health support.
- **Peacekeeping Successes** — Many missions reduced violence where consent existed.
- **Norm Creation** — UN creates legal and diplomatic frameworks.
- **Reform Need** — UNSC expansion and veto restraint are essential.

**Keywords (underline in exam):** UNO · UNSC · Veto · Ukraine · Syria · Gaza · Peacekeeping · UN Reform

**Exam diagram (30 sec):**
```
UN peace role blocked by veto + great-power rivalry
Still useful for aid + norms + peacekeeping
```

**Conclusion:**
> The UN has not fully succeeded in peace enforcement, but reform is better than abandoning the only universal forum.
