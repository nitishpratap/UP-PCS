## Content

### Context — why international institutions matter

International institutions help manage problems that states cannot solve alone: war, health emergencies, poverty, climate change, human rights, trade, finance, oceans and legal disputes. Their authority comes from treaties, member consent, mandates and legitimacy rather than world government. For India, these institutions are both opportunities and constraints. They provide development finance, health guidance, legal forums and diplomatic platforms, but also reflect power asymmetries and slow reform.

### World Bank and sustainable development in India

The World Bank Group includes **IBRD**, **IDA**, **IFC**, **MIGA** and **ICSID**. For India's sustainable development, IBRD and IDA are most relevant. The World Bank supports poverty reduction, infrastructure, water, sanitation, urban development, health, education, agriculture, rural livelihoods, disaster resilience, energy transition and institutional reforms. India has been one of the largest borrowers and partners.

Its contribution to sustainable development lies in long-term finance, technical expertise, global best practices, monitoring frameworks and support to states. Projects in rural roads, watershed management, clean energy, health systems, education, urban water, river basin management, disaster recovery and livelihoods have supported growth with social development. During COVID-19, World Bank support helped health system strengthening and social protection.

Critically, World Bank assistance is not free of concerns. Loan conditionalities, bureaucratic procedures, environmental and social safeguards, displacement concerns and one-size-fits-all policy templates have been criticised. India's current approach should use World Bank finance selectively for technology, climate resilience, urban systems and state capacity while preserving policy autonomy.

| Area | World Bank role | Sustainable development value |
|------|-----------------|-------------------------------|
| **Infrastructure** | Roads, urban services, water systems. | Growth with service access. |
| **Health/education** | Systems strengthening and outcome monitoring. | Human capital development. |
| **Rural livelihoods** | SHGs, watershed, agriculture support. | Poverty reduction and resilience. |
| **Climate/disaster** | Adaptation, clean energy, disaster recovery. | Risk reduction and green transition. |

### Arctic Council

The **Arctic Council** was established by the **Ottawa Declaration, 1996**. Its eight member states are Canada, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Russia, Sweden and the United States. It also includes **Permanent Participants**, representing Indigenous peoples' organisations, and observers such as India, which became an observer in 2013. The Council works through scientific assessments and working groups on environment, climate, biodiversity, pollutants, emergency prevention and sustainable development.

Its mandate excludes military security. This is important because Arctic geopolitics is increasing due to melting ice, shipping routes, hydrocarbons, rare earths and great-power competition, but the Council's formal work remains environmental and developmental. For India, Arctic interest relates to climate science, monsoon linkages, polar research, energy, shipping and global commons.

### WHO during COVID-19

The **World Health Organization (WHO)** is a UN specialised agency established in 1948 and headquartered in Geneva. Its mandate is to act as the directing and coordinating authority on international health. During COVID-19, WHO issued alerts, declared a **Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC)**, later characterised COVID-19 as a pandemic, issued technical guidelines, coordinated situation reports, supported testing and surveillance protocols, helped the **Solidarity Trial**, and worked with partners through **COVAX** for vaccine access.

WHO's role was important because pandemics require shared information, standards and coordination. However, criticism arose over delayed warning allegations, dependence on member-state data, China-related transparency concerns, funding dependence and lack of enforcement power. WHO can advise and coordinate; it cannot compel sovereign states to disclose or act. COVID showed the need for stronger global health surveillance, pandemic treaty discussions, financing and transparent reporting.

### NHRC in India

The **National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)** is a domestic statutory body created under the **Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993**. It is not an international institution, but it is often studied with human rights bodies because it implements global human rights norms within India. It inquires into human rights violations, intervenes in court proceedings, visits jails and detention centres, reviews safeguards, promotes research, spreads awareness and recommends compensation or prosecution.

Its limitations include recommendatory powers, no direct punishment authority, delays, vacancies, limited jurisdiction over armed forces and dependence on government for implementation. Still, NHRC is important for custodial deaths, prison conditions, bonded labour, trafficking, disability rights, encounter allegations and rights awareness.

### UN Security Council and India's permanent membership claim

The **UN Security Council (UNSC)** has 15 members: five permanent members with veto power (US, UK, France, Russia, China) and ten elected non-permanent members. It has primary responsibility for international peace and security. India claims permanent membership because it is the world's largest democracy, most populous country, major economy, nuclear-weapon state outside NPT framework but responsible actor, major troop contributor to UN peacekeeping, voice of the Global South, and supporter of multilateralism.

India is part of the **G4** with Germany, Japan and Brazil seeking UNSC reform. Its claim is strong, but obstacles include P5 politics, Chinese resistance, Uniting for Consensus opposition, disagreement over veto expansion and slow UN reform. India's argument is that UNSC legitimacy suffers when Africa, Latin America and a country like India lack permanent representation.

### International Court of Justice

The **International Court of Justice (ICJ)** is the principal judicial organ of the United Nations, established under the UN Charter and located at The Hague. It has 15 judges elected by the UN General Assembly and Security Council. Its jurisdiction has two parts: **contentious jurisdiction** between states and **advisory jurisdiction** for UN organs and specialised agencies.

Contentious jurisdiction is consent-based. States may accept jurisdiction through special agreement, treaty clauses or optional clause declarations. Individuals, companies and NGOs cannot directly bring cases. Advisory opinions are not strictly binding but carry high legal and moral authority. ICJ judgments are binding on parties, but enforcement depends on state compliance and, if necessary, the UN Security Council. This creates limitations when powerful states resist compliance.

### Contemporary relevance

- **World Bank climate finance** is relevant for India's green transition, urban resilience and disaster adaptation.
- **Arctic warming** affects monsoon research, sea levels and global shipping routes, making India's observer role useful.
- **WHO reform** remains important after COVID for pandemic preparedness and transparent health reporting.
- **NHRC** remains central in custodial justice, prison reform and rights protection debates.
- **UNSC reform** is a major plank of India's global leadership claim after its G20 Presidency and Global South outreach.
- **ICJ** remains relevant for disputes involving state responsibility, consular access, genocide, maritime and treaty questions.

### Limits — balanced view

International institutions are necessary but not sufficient. They depend on state consent, funding, geopolitics and compliance. The World Bank can finance development but not replace domestic capacity. WHO can guide but not enforce transparency. ICJ can decide but not always secure compliance. UNSC can maintain peace but is constrained by veto politics. India's policy should therefore support reform, use institutions strategically and build domestic strength.

### Q2: Structure and functioning of Arctic Council. (2021, 12M, 200W)

**Introduction:**
> The Arctic Council is the leading intergovernmental forum for cooperation on Arctic environment and sustainable development, established by the Ottawa Declaration, 1996.

**Structure:**
- **Members** — Eight Arctic states: Canada, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Russia, Sweden and USA.
- **Permanent Participants** — Indigenous peoples' organisations participate specially.
- **Observers** — Non-Arctic states, including India, and organisations may observe.
- **Working Groups** — Focus on monitoring, biodiversity, pollutants, emergency prevention and sustainable development.
- **Chairmanship** — Rotates among Arctic states.

**Functioning:**
- **Environmental Cooperation** — Studies climate change, pollution and Arctic ecosystems.
- **Sustainable Development** — Addresses Indigenous livelihoods and regional resilience.
- **Scientific Assessments** — Produces knowledge on ice melt, biodiversity and pollutants.
- **Consensus Style** — Decisions are cooperative and non-military.
- **India's Interest** — Climate-monsoon links, polar science, shipping and energy.

**Limitations:**
- **No Security Mandate** — Military issues are outside formal mandate.
- **Geopolitical Tension** — Russia-West tensions affect cooperation.
- **Climate Urgency** — Scientific cooperation may be slower than Arctic change.

**Keywords (underline in exam):** Arctic Council · Ottawa Declaration · Indigenous Peoples · Observer India · Arctic Climate · Sustainable Development

**Exam diagram (30 sec):**
```
Arctic Council = 8 Arctic states + Indigenous participants + observers
Mandate: environment + sustainable development, not military security
```

**Conclusion:**
> The Arctic Council is vital for polar governance, but geopolitics and climate urgency test its effectiveness.

### Q3: Role of WHO during COVID-19. (2020, 12M, 200W)

**Introduction:**
> WHO is the UN specialised agency for global health coordination. During COVID-19, it played a major advisory, surveillance and coordination role.

**Main Points:**
- **Early Alert** — WHO monitored outbreak information and issued global alerts.
- **PHEIC Declaration** — It declared COVID-19 a Public Health Emergency of International Concern.
- **Technical Guidelines** — Issued guidance on testing, masking, treatment, surveillance and public health measures.
- **Data Coordination** — Published situation reports and global dashboards.
- **Research Support** — Coordinated the Solidarity Trial for therapeutics.
- **Vaccine Access** — Supported COVAX with partners for equitable vaccine access.
- **Capacity Support** — Assisted weaker health systems with protocols and training.
- **Public Communication** — Countered misinformation and promoted risk communication.

**Criticism:**
- **Delayed Response Allegations** — Questions arose over initial handling and China data dependence.
- **Limited Enforcement** — WHO can advise but cannot compel sovereign states.
- **Funding Dependence** — Voluntary funding affects priorities.

**Keywords (underline in exam):** WHO · COVID-19 · PHEIC · COVAX · Solidarity Trial · Global Health · Pandemic Preparedness

**Exam diagram (30 sec):**
```
WHO during COVID: alert + guidelines + data + research + vaccine access
Limit: no enforcement power
```

**Conclusion:**
> WHO was indispensable for coordination, but COVID exposed the need for stronger global health governance.

- **Recommendation** — Recommends compensation, prosecution or corrective action.

### Q5: Is India a powerful claimant of permanent membership in the Security Council? Give reasoned answer. (2019, 12M, 200W)

- **Uniting for Consensus** — Some countries oppose new permanent seats.

### Q6: Critically examine the jurisdiction of International Court of Justice. (2018, 8M, 125W)

**Introduction:**
> The ICJ is the principal judicial organ of the UN, located at The Hague. Its jurisdiction is important but limited by state consent.

**Main Points:**
- **Contentious Jurisdiction** — It decides legal disputes between states, not individuals.
- **Consent-based** — States must accept jurisdiction through treaty, special agreement or optional clause.
- **Advisory Jurisdiction** — Gives legal opinions to UN organs and specialised agencies.
- **Binding Judgments** — Contentious judgments bind parties to the case.
- **No Direct Criminal Role** — It is different from the International Criminal Court.
- **Enforcement Limit** — Compliance depends on states; UNSC politics can affect enforcement.
- **Slow Process** — Cases can be lengthy and legalistic.

**Keywords (underline in exam):** ICJ · The Hague · Contentious Jurisdiction · Advisory Opinion · State Consent · UN Charter

**Exam diagram (30 sec):**
```
ICJ jurisdiction
 |-- contentious: states + consent
 |-- advisory: UN organs/agencies
Limits: enforcement + politics
```

**Conclusion:**
> ICJ strengthens rule-based international order, but sovereignty and enforcement limits restrict its reach.

- **Global South Demand** — India and others seek inclusive multilateralism.

**Exam diagram (30 sec):**
