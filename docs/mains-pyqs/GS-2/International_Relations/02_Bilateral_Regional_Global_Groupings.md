## Content

### Context — India's multi-alignment in a changing world

India's external engagement has shifted from non-alignment to **multi-alignment**: India works with different partners on different issues while preserving strategic autonomy. This is visible in India's simultaneous engagement with the **G20**, **QUAD**, **BRICS**, **SCO**, **I2U2**, **BIMSTEC**, **ASEAN**, **European Union**, **United States**, **Russia**, **West Asian countries** and Global South platforms. The purpose is not alliance dependency but securing India's interests in trade, technology, energy, maritime security, climate finance, supply chains and global governance reform.

The global context is changing: US-China rivalry, Russia-Ukraine war, post-COVID supply chain restructuring, energy insecurity, climate stress, digital geopolitics, protectionism, and conflict in West Asia. India must diversify markets, protect energy flows, keep strategic autonomy, build domestic manufacturing and present itself as a bridge between developed and developing worlds.

### India-EU, FTAs and trade strategy

India-EU engagement should be viewed as strategic, not only commercial. The EU is a major market, technology source, climate actor and regulatory power. India seeks investment, supply-chain diversification, green technology, digital cooperation and market access. The EU seeks democratic partners, resilient supply chains, Indo-Pacific cooperation and alternatives to excessive China dependence. The India-EU Trade and Technology Council and FTA negotiations fit this wider geopolitical shift.

FTAs can help India reduce vulnerability to adverse US trade policies by diversifying export markets. Agreements such as India-UAE CEPA, India-Australia ECTA, India-EFTA TEPA and negotiations with the EU/UK create alternative markets and supply-chain openings. But FTAs are not magic. Indian exporters must meet rules of origin, standards, quality, logistics, scale and competitiveness requirements. If domestic manufacturing is weak, FTAs may increase imports more than exports. Thus, FTAs mitigate US policy shocks only when linked with PLI, logistics, standards, skilling and export diversification.

| Agreement/grouping | India's interest | Main caution |
|--------------------|------------------|--------------|
| **India-EU** | Trade, technology, green transition, Indo-Pacific, data. | Carbon border rules, labour/environment standards. |
| **FTAs/CEPAs** | Market diversification and export resilience. | Rules of origin, import surge, standards compliance. |
| **India-UAE CEPA** | West Asia market, re-export hub, energy-finance links. | Gems/jewellery, rules and re-export monitoring. |
| **India-Australia ECTA** | Minerals, education, services, Indo-Pacific. | Agriculture sensitivities and standards. |
| **EFTA TEPA** | Investment commitment and high-value trade. | Implementation and domestic competitiveness. |

### Southeast Asia, ASEAN and BIMSTEC

India's Southeast Asia policy evolved from Look East to **Act East**. It aims to connect Northeast India with Southeast Asia, deepen ASEAN ties, secure sea lanes, develop trade, and balance China's influence. ASEAN centrality, Indo-Pacific Oceans Initiative, Mekong-Ganga Cooperation, India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway and cultural-Buddhist links are core elements.

SAARC's stagnation, largely due to India-Pakistan hostility and terrorism, pushed India toward **BIMSTEC**. BIMSTEC connects South Asia and Southeast Asia through the Bay of Bengal, including India, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, Myanmar and Thailand. It supports connectivity, energy, counter-terrorism, disaster management, fisheries, climate and trade. It is especially important for India's Northeast and Bay of Bengal strategy. BIMSTEC does not fully replace SAARC, but it gives India a Pakistan-free regional platform.

### QUAD, Indo-Pacific and Malabar

The **Quadrilateral Security Dialogue (QUAD)** includes India, the United States, Japan and Australia. It supports a free, open and inclusive Indo-Pacific, rule of law, maritime security, supply-chain resilience, cyber security, critical technologies, vaccines, infrastructure and disaster response. India's rationale is to balance Chinese assertiveness without entering a formal military alliance. QUAD also gives India access to technology, maritime domain awareness and strategic coordination with major democracies.

**Malabar naval exercise** improves interoperability among QUAD navies and signals deterrence, but it cannot by itself contain China. Containment requires economic resilience, military preparedness, partnerships, domestic capacity and diplomatic balancing. India must keep QUAD issue-based and compatible with strategic autonomy.

### G20 and India's global leadership claim

The **G20** is a major forum for global economic governance, representing advanced and emerging economies. The 2021 Rome Summit theme focused on **People, Planet, Prosperity**, highlighting health recovery, climate and inclusive growth after COVID. India's 2023 G20 Presidency under the theme **One Earth, One Family, One Future** elevated Global South concerns, digital public infrastructure, climate finance, LiFE, women-led development, debt stress and inclusive growth. The inclusion of the **African Union** as a permanent G20 member was a major diplomatic success.

India's claim to world leadership rests on population, economy, digital public infrastructure, vaccine capacity, space achievements, diaspora, democracy, Global South voice, climate commitments and strategic geography. Limits include per capita income, human development gaps, border tensions, import dependence, and need for domestic manufacturing strength.

### West Asia, energy and conflict management

West Asia is central to India's energy security, diaspora, remittances, trade, connectivity and counter-terror interests. India imports significant oil and gas from the region and has millions of workers in Gulf countries. India maintains balanced ties with Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Iran, Israel and Palestine. **I2U2** and proposed **India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC)** show India's interest in connectivity and technology cooperation.

The **Abraham Accords** normalised relations between Israel and several Arab states, especially UAE and Bahrain, opening possibilities for trade, technology, security and connectivity. For India, this reduces the old Israel-Arab binary and supports minilateral cooperation like I2U2. But the Israel-Palestine conflict still creates humanitarian, diaspora, energy and diplomatic challenges. India's response should balance support for a two-state solution, condemnation of terrorism, humanitarian assistance, protection of diaspora and strategic ties with Israel and Arab states.

US-Iran tensions affect India through oil imports, Chabahar connectivity, diaspora safety, shipping insurance, rupee trade, and relations with the US and Gulf partners. India should diversify energy, preserve Chabahar space, avoid escalation, and maintain strategic autonomy.

### India-USA, Russia-Ukraine and strategic autonomy

India-USA relations have expanded through defence, technology, diaspora, education, Indo-Pacific cooperation, 2+2 ministerial dialogue, foundational agreements, clean energy and critical technologies. The **2+2 dialogue** brings foreign and defence ministers together, making strategic coordination more integrated. Areas of contention include trade tariffs, visa issues, data rules, agricultural market access, climate burden-sharing, CAATSA/Russia defence links, and differing approaches to some global crises.

The Russia-Ukraine conflict has causes in NATO expansion debates, Ukraine's geopolitical orientation, Russia's security claims, Crimea 2014, Donbas tensions and failure of diplomatic settlements. For India, the war affected energy, fertilisers, food prices, defence supplies and strategic diplomacy. India's stance has balanced respect for sovereignty, call for dialogue, energy security and strategic autonomy.

### China, OBOR and LAC

OBOR/BRI reflects China's infrastructure-led global influence. India's main objection is **CPEC passing through Pakistan-occupied Kashmir**, which violates India's sovereignty claims. India also worries about debt dependence, lack of transparency, strategic ports and Chinese influence in India's neighbourhood. India-China relations in this light are competitive engagement: trade continues, but strategic mistrust is deep.

The LAC dispute deepened despite Wuhan/Mamallapuram because informal summits could not resolve an undemarcated boundary, infrastructure race, differing patrol perceptions, China's assertiveness, Tibet sensitivities, CPEC, trade imbalance and broader power rivalry. India must combine military deterrence, border infrastructure, diplomacy and economic resilience.

### Kashmir mediation and Afghanistan

India opposes third-party mediation on Kashmir because the **Simla Agreement 1972** and **Lahore Declaration 1999** establish bilateral resolution between India and Pakistan. Mediation internationalises the issue, gives Pakistan diplomatic leverage, and undermines India's sovereignty position. India accepts bilateral dialogue only in a terror-free environment.

US withdrawal from Afghanistan affected India by enabling Taliban return, increasing terror concerns, strengthening Pakistan's leverage, risking Indian projects, and reducing India's diplomatic space. India's response is humanitarian aid, regional coordination, security vigilance and cautious engagement while protecting interests.

### Climate change and developing countries

Climate change affects developing countries disproportionately despite their lower historical emissions. Impacts include heat stress, floods, droughts, sea-level rise, food insecurity, water stress, health risks, displacement, fiscal burden and development diversion. India argues for **common but differentiated responsibilities**, climate finance, technology transfer, adaptation support and climate justice. Climate diplomacy is therefore part of global agreements affecting India's interests.

### Contemporary relevance

- **India-EU FTA and TTC** matter amid supply-chain diversification and China-risk reduction.
- **FTAs with UAE, Australia and EFTA** show export diversification strategy against protectionism.
- **QUAD** is moving into technology, supply chains, HADR and maritime domain awareness.
- **G20 Presidency 2023** strengthened India's Global South leadership claim.
- **Israel-Palestine conflict** tests India's West Asia balance between Israel, Palestine, Arab states and diaspora safety.
- **Russia-Ukraine war** keeps strategic autonomy, energy and defence supply questions alive.
- **US-Iran tension** keeps Chabahar, oil and Gulf security relevant.
- **BRI/CPEC and LAC** remain central to India-China strategic mistrust.

### Limits — balanced view

India's groupings policy is useful only if domestic capacity matches diplomatic ambition. FTAs need competitive manufacturing; QUAD needs maritime and technological capacity; G20 leadership needs human development credibility; West Asia balancing needs crisis management; BIMSTEC needs timely projects; climate leadership needs finance and implementation. Multi-alignment is an opportunity, but overextension and slow delivery can reduce credibility.

### Q2: Can India's FTAs mitigate adverse effects of US trade policies on exports? Critically evaluate. (2025, 12M, 125W)

**Introduction:**
> FTAs diversify India's export markets and reduce dependence on any single market, including the US. But their benefit depends on domestic competitiveness.

**Main Points:**
- **Market Diversification** — FTAs with UAE, Australia, EFTA and future EU/UK deals can open alternative markets.
- **Tariff Relief** — Lower duties can offset some protectionist barriers elsewhere.
- **Supply-chain Entry** — FTAs help Indian firms join regional value chains.
- **Services Advantage** — Mobility and mutual recognition can help IT, professionals and education.
- **Export Confidence** — Stable rules attract investment in export sectors.
- **Limit: Standards** — Rules of origin, SPS/TBT and carbon norms may restrict gains.
- **Limit: Import Surge** — Weak sectors may face competition.
- **US Market Uniqueness** — FTAs cannot fully replace high-value US demand.
- **Need** — PLI, logistics, quality, skilling and trade facilitation must accompany FTAs.

**Keywords (underline in exam):** FTAs · Export Diversification · Rules of Origin · UAE CEPA · EFTA TEPA · Standards · Protectionism

**Exam diagram (30 sec):**
```
US policy shock -> FTA diversification
But gains need competitiveness + standards + logistics
```

**Conclusion:**
> FTAs can reduce vulnerability to US trade shocks, but only as part of a wider export-competitiveness strategy.
