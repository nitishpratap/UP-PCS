
# ARTICLE 20 — PROTECTION IN RESPECT OF CONVICTION FOR OFFENCES


Article 20 protects persons against **arbitrary and excessive punishment**.


Important:


This protection is available to:

- Citizens
- Foreigners
- Legal persons (companies, corporations)

---


# THREE PROTECTIONS UNDER ARTICLE 20


Article 20 contains three safeguards:

1. Protection against **Ex-post-facto laws**
2. Protection against **Double Jeopardy**
3. Protection against **Self-incrimination**

---


# 1. NO EX-POST-FACTO LAW


An **Ex-post-facto law** is a law that:

- Punishes an act that was **not an offence when committed**, or
- Increases punishment **after the act was committed**

Article 20 prohibits such laws.


---


### What is prohibited

1. Conviction under retrospective criminal law
2. Greater punishment than existed when offence occurred

---


### What is allowed


This restriction applies **only to criminal laws**.


It does **not apply to:**

- Civil laws
- Tax laws

Example:


Government can impose taxes **retrospectively**.


---


### Other Important Points

1. Article 20 prohibits **conviction and sentence**, but **not trial**.
2. Protection does **not apply to preventive detention**.
3. Protection does **not apply when security is demanded from a person**.

---


# 2. NO DOUBLE JEOPARDY


Principle:

> No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once.

Meaning:


A person cannot be tried and punished **twice for the same crime**.


---


### Important Condition


This protection applies only when:

- First trial was before **court of law or judicial tribunal**.

It does **not apply to:**

- Departmental inquiries
- Administrative proceedings

Because these are not judicial proceedings.


---


# 3. NO SELF-INCRIMINATION


Article 20(3):

> No person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself.

This means:


The accused cannot be forced to provide evidence against himself.


---


### Applies to

- Oral evidence
- Documentary evidence

---


### Does NOT apply to

1. Compulsory production of **material objects**
2. **Thumb impression**
3. **Specimen signatures**
4. **Blood samples**
5. **Body examination**

---


### Other Important Point


This protection applies only to **criminal proceedings**, not civil cases.


---


# ARTICLE 21 — PROTECTION OF LIFE AND PERSONAL LIBERTY


Article 21 states:

> No person shall be deprived वंचित of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.

This right is available to:

- Citizens
- Foreigners

---


# EARLY INTERPRETATION — GOPALAN CASE (1950)


Supreme Court interpreted Article 21 **narrowly**.


Judgement said:


Protection exists only against **arbitrary executive action**.


But if a law exists, the State can deprive liberty.


Reason:


Article 21 uses the phrase:


**“Procedure established by law”**


Not **“Due process of law”**.


Therefore courts initially could not examine fairness of laws.


---


# EXPANDED INTERPRETATION — MANEKA GANDHI CASE (1978)


Supreme Court changed interpretation.


Held:


Procedure must be:

- Fair
- Just
- Reasonable

This effectively introduced **Due Process of Law** concept.


---


### Result of Maneka Gandhi Judgement


Article 21 now protects against:

- Arbitrary executive action
- Arbitrary legislative action

---


# MEANING OF RIGHT TO LIFE


Supreme Court ruled:


Right to life does **not mean mere animal existence**.


It includes:

> Right to live with human dignity and meaningful life.

---


# RIGHTS DERIVED FROM ARTICLE 21


Supreme Court expanded Article 21 to include many rights.

# ARTICLE 21A — RIGHT TO EDUCATION


Article 21A provides:

> State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children aged **6 to 14 years**.

This provision was added by:


**86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002**


---


# SIGNIFICANCE


Made **elementary education a Fundamental Right**.


However:


Higher education is **not a Fundamental Right**.


---


# PREVIOUSLY UNDER DIRECTIVE PRINCIPLES


Earlier Article 45 stated:


State should provide free education up to 14 years.


But since DPSP is **non-justiciable**, it could not be enforced.


After 86th amendment:


Education became **justiciable**.


---


# CHANGES MADE BY 86TH AMENDMENT


Three changes:


### 1. Added Article 21A


Free education for children **6-14 years**.


---


### 2. Modified Article 45


Now states:


State shall provide **early childhood care and education up to age 6**.


---


### 3. Added Fundamental Duty


Article 51A added duty of parents:


Provide opportunities for education to children **6-14 years**.


---


# RIGHT TO EDUCATION ACT (RTE), 2009


Parliament enacted **Right to Education Act, 2009**.


Key objective:


Provide **free and compulsory elementary education**.


Features:

- Full time elementary education
- Quality education
- Inclusive education
- Equal opportunities

---


# ARTICLE 22 — PROTECTION AGAINST ARREST AND DETENTION


Article 22 provides safeguards to persons who are:

- Arrested
- Detained

---


# TWO TYPES OF DETENTION


### 1. Punitive Detention


Detention **after conviction by court**.


Purpose:


Punish the offender.


---


### 2. Preventive Detention


Detention **without trial**.


Purpose:


Prevent a person from committing future crime.


---


# ARTICLE 22 HAS TWO PARTS

1. Safeguards under **ordinary law**
2. Safeguards under **preventive detention law**

---


# RIGHTS OF ARRESTED PERSON (ORDINARY LAW)


A person arrested under ordinary law has following rights:

1. Right to be informed of grounds of arrest
2. Right to consult a lawyer
3. Right to be produced before magistrate within **24 hours**
4. Cannot be detained beyond **24 hours** without magistrate approval

---


### These rights are NOT available to:

1. Enemy aliens
2. Persons detained under **preventive detention laws**

---


# PREVENTIVE DETENTION SAFEGUARDS


Under preventive detention:

1. Detention cannot exceed **3 months** unless advisory board approves.
2. Advisory board must consist of **High Court judges**.
3. Grounds of detention must be communicated.
4. Detenu must be given opportunity to make representation.

---


# POWERS OF PARLIAMENT


Parliament may determine:

1. Circumstances for detention beyond 3 months
2. Maximum period of detention
3. Procedure of advisory board

---


# CHANGE BY 44TH AMENDMENT


44th Amendment reduced detention without advisory board from:


3 months → **2 months**


But this provision **has not been implemented yet**.


Therefore **3 months rule still continues**.


---


# LEGISLATIVE POWER ON PREVENTIVE DETENTION


Preventive detention powers divided between:


### Parliament


For matters related to:

- Defence
- Foreign affairs
- Security of India

---


### Parliament + State Legislatures


For matters related to:

- Security of state
- Public order
- Essential supplies

---


# PREVENTIVE DETENTION LAWS IN INDIA


Major laws include:

1. Preventive Detention Act (1950) – expired 1969
2. Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA), 1971 – repealed 1978
3. COFEPOSA Act, 1974
4. National Security Act (NSA), 1980
5. Prevention of Blackmarketing Act, 1980
6. TADA Act, 1985 – repealed 1995
7. PITNDPS Act, 1988
8. POTA Act, 2002 – repealed 2004

---


# IMPORTANT NOTE


India is one of the few democratic countries where **preventive detention is part of the Constitution itself**.


Examples:

- Not present in **USA**
- Used in **UK only during wars**

But in India it existed even during **British rule**.


Examples:

- Bengal State Prisoners Regulation (1818)
- Defence of India Act (1939)

---


# RIGHT AGAINST EXPLOITATION


_(Articles 23–24)_


The Constitution prohibits **human exploitation** in any form.


These rights are available to:

- Citizens
- Non-citizens

They protect individuals **against both State and private persons**.


---


# ARTICLE 23


# PROHIBITION OF TRAFFIC IN HUMAN BEINGS AND FORCED LABOUR


Article 23 prohibits:

1. **Traffic in human beings**
2. **Begar (forced labour)**
3. **Other similar forms of forced labour**

Violation of this provision is **punishable by law**.


---


# TRAFFIC IN HUMAN BEINGS


Includes:

- Buying and selling humans
- Prostitution
- Devadasi system
- Slavery

To prevent such exploitation, Parliament enacted:


**Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956**


---


# BEGAR


Begar means:

> Forced labour without payment.

Example:


Under the zamindari system, peasants were forced to work without wages.


---


# FORCED LABOUR


Forced labour includes:

- Physical coercion (दबाव)
- Legal coercion
- Economic compulsion

Example:


Working for **less than minimum wages**.


---


# IMPORTANT LAWS AGAINST FORCED LABOUR

1. Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976
2. Minimum Wages Act, 1948
3. Contract Labour Act, 1970
4. Equal Remuneration Act, 1976

---


# EXCEPTION TO ARTICLE 23


State can impose **compulsory service for public purposes**.


Examples:

- Military service
- Social service

But:


No discrimination based on

- Religion
- Race
- Caste
- Class

---


# ARTICLE 24


# PROHIBITION OF CHILD LABOUR


Article 24 prohibits:


Employment of children **below 14 years** in:

- Factories
- Mines
- Hazardous occupations

But children may work in **non-hazardous activities**.


---


# IMPORTANT LAW


**Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986**


---


# OTHER LAWS REGULATING CHILD LABOUR

1. Employment of Children Act, 1938
2. Factories Act, 1948
3. Mines Act, 1952
4. Merchant Shipping Act, 1958
5. Plantation Labour Act, 1951
6. Motor Transport Workers Act, 1961
7. Apprentices Act, 1961
8. Bidi and Cigar Workers Act, 1966

---


# SUPREME COURT DIRECTIVE (1996)


Court ordered creation of:


**Child Labour Rehabilitation Welfare Fund**


Employer must deposit:


**₹20,000 for each child employed illegally**


Funds used for:

- Education
- Health
- Nutrition of children

---


# COMMISSION FOR PROTECTION OF CHILD RIGHTS ACT, 2005


Established:

- National Commission for Protection of Child Rights
- State Commissions
- Children's Courts

Purpose:


Speedy trial of offences against children.


---


# GOVERNMENT BAN (2006)


Children below 14 cannot work as:

- Domestic servants
- Workers in hotels
- Dhabas
- Restaurants
- Shops
- Factories
- Resorts
- Tea shops

Violation leads to **criminal prosecution**.


---


# CHILD LABOUR AMENDMENT ACT, 2016


Renamed law:


**Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act**


---


### Key Provisions

1. Complete ban on employment of children **below 14 years**.
2. Adolescents (14–18 years) cannot work in **hazardous occupations**.
3. Punishment increased:
- 6 months to 2 years imprisonment
- Fine ₹20,000 to ₹50,000

Repeat offence:

- 1–3 years imprisonment

---


# RIGHT TO FREEDOM OF RELIGION


_(Articles 25–28)_


These articles guarantee **religious freedom**.


Available to:

- Citizens
- Non-citizens

India follows the principle of **Secularism**.


---


# ARTICLE 25


# FREEDOM OF CONSCIENCE AND RELIGION


Every person has:

1. Freedom of conscience
2. Freedom to profess religion
3. Freedom to practice religion
4. Freedom to propagate religion

---


# MEANING OF THESE TERMS


### Freedom of Conscience


Inner freedom to follow any religion.


---


### Right to Profess


Openly declare religious beliefs.


---


### Right to Practice


Perform rituals, ceremonies, worship.


---


### Right to Propagate


Spread religious ideas.


Important:


Propagation **does not include forced conversion**.


---


# LIMITATIONS ON ARTICLE 25


Freedom of religion is subject to:

1. Public order
2. Morality
3. Health
4. Other Fundamental Rights

---


# POWERS OF THE STATE


State may:

1. Regulate secular activities related to religion.

Examples:

- Temple administration
- Religious property
1. Implement social reforms.

Example:


Opening Hindu temples to all castes.


---


# SPECIAL PROVISIONS


Article 25 explains:

1. Wearing **Kirpan** is part of Sikh religion.
2. The term **Hindu includes:**
- Sikhs
- Jains
- Buddhists

---


# ARTICLE 26


# FREEDOM TO MANAGE RELIGIOUS AFFAIRS


Every religious denomination has the right to:

1. Establish religious institutions
2. Manage religious affairs
3. Own property
4. Administer property

---


# CONDITIONS FOR RELIGIOUS DENOMINATION


Supreme Court states denomination must have:

1. Common religious beliefs
2. Common organization
3. Distinctive name

---


# COURT EXAMPLES


Recognized as denominations:

- Ramakrishna Mission
- Ananda Marga

Not recognized:

- Aurobindo Society

---


# ARTICLE 27


# FREEDOM FROM TAXATION FOR RELIGION


No person shall be compelled to pay taxes for promotion of any particular religion.


Meaning:


State cannot use tax money to promote a specific religion.


However:


Taxes may support **all religions equally**.


---


# TAX VS FEE


Article 27 prohibits **tax**, not **fee**.


Fee may be charged for:

- Religious institution management
- Pilgrimage services
- Safety arrangements

---


# ARTICLE 28


# FREEDOM FROM RELIGIOUS INSTRUCTION


Article 28 regulates religious education in institutions.


Four types of institutions:


---


### 1. Institutions fully funded by State


Religious instruction **not allowed**.


---


### 2. Institutions administered by State but established by trust


Religious instruction **allowed**.


---


### 3. Institutions recognized by State


Religious instruction allowed **voluntarily**.


---


### 4. Institutions receiving State aid


Religious instruction allowed **voluntarily**.


---


# CONSENT RULE


Students cannot be forced to attend religious instruction.


For minors:


Guardian consent required.


---


# CULTURAL AND EDUCATIONAL RIGHTS


_(Articles 29–30)_


These rights protect **minority interests**.


---


# ARTICLE 29


# PROTECTION OF MINORITY CULTURE


Any section of citizens with distinct:

- Language
- Script
- Culture

has the right to **preserve it**.


---


# SECOND PROVISION OF ARTICLE 29


No citizen shall be denied admission into educational institutions on grounds of:

- Religion
- Race
- Caste
- Language

---


# IMPORTANT POINT


Article 29 protects:

- Minorities
- Majority groups

Because it refers to **“section of citizens”**.


---


# SUPREME COURT OBSERVATION


Right to conserve language includes:


Right to **agitate or campaign** for language protection.


Political speeches for language protection are **not corrupt practice**.


---


# ARTICLE 30


# RIGHT OF MINORITIES TO ESTABLISH EDUCATIONAL INSTITUTIONS


Minorities (religious or linguistic) have right to:

1. Establish educational institutions
2. Administer them

---


# PROTECTION OF MINORITY INSTITUTIONS


State cannot discriminate in granting aid.


---


# PROPERTY PROTECTION


44th Amendment added protection:


Compensation for property acquisition must not harm minority rights.


---


# TYPES OF MINORITY EDUCATIONAL INSTITUTIONS


Three types:

1. Institutions seeking **recognition + aid**
2. Institutions seeking **recognition only**
3. Institutions seeking **neither aid nor recognition**

---


# STATE REGULATION


State may regulate:

- Academic standards
- Syllabus
- Discipline
- Sanitation
- Teacher qualifications

---


# IMPORTANT SUPREME COURT CASE


_(Malankara Syrian Catholic College Case, 2007)_


Court clarified minority rights.


---


### Rights of minority institutions include

1. Choosing governing body
2. Appointing staff
3. Admitting students
4. Setting reasonable fees
5. Using institutional property

---


### Important principle


Article 30 ensures **equality with majority**, not special privilege.


---


### Regulation allowed


State regulations allowed for:

- Academic standards
- Employee protection
- Student welfare

But:


State cannot interfere with **core management rights**.


---


# RIGHT TO CONSTITUTIONAL REMEDIES


_(Article 32)_


Fundamental Rights would be meaningless **without enforcement mechanisms**.


Therefore, the Constitution provides **Right to Constitutional Remedies**.


This right allows citizens to **approach the Supreme Court directly** if their Fundamental Rights are violated.


---


# IMPORTANCE OF ARTICLE 32


Dr. B. R. Ambedkar described Article 32 as:

> **“The very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it.”**

Reason:


It guarantees **effective enforcement of Fundamental Rights**.


---


# ARTICLE 32 — MAIN PROVISIONS


Article 32 contains four provisions.


---


### 1. Right to Approach Supreme Court


Citizens have the right to move the **Supreme Court** for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.


---


### 2. Power of Supreme Court to Issue Writs


The Supreme Court can issue:

- Directions
- Orders
- Writs

for enforcing Fundamental Rights.


These writs include:

1. Habeas Corpus
2. Mandamus
3. Prohibition
4. Certiorari
5. Quo Warranto

---


### 3. Parliament May Empower Other Courts


Parliament can empower other courts to issue writs.


However:


This does not affect the powers of Supreme Court.


Note:


High Courts already have this power under **Article 226**.


---


### 4. Suspension During Emergency


The right to move the Supreme Court can be suspended during **National Emergency**.


This is done under **Article 359**.


---


# SUPREME COURT AS GUARDIAN OF FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS


Supreme Court has been made:

- Defender
- Guardian
- Protector

of Fundamental Rights.


---


### Nature of Supreme Court Jurisdiction


The jurisdiction under Article 32 is:


**Original**


Meaning:


A citizen can **directly approach the Supreme Court**.


---


### Wide Jurisdiction


Supreme Court can issue:

- Orders
- Directions
- Writs

for protecting rights.


---


# PURPOSE OF ARTICLE 32


Article 32 ensures that protection of Fundamental Rights is:

- Guaranteed
- Effective
- Speedy
- Inexpensive

---


# IMPORTANT LIMITATION


Article 32 can be used **only for Fundamental Rights**.


It cannot be used for:

- Legal rights
- Statutory rights
- Customary rights

Violation of Fundamental Right is necessary.


---


# ARTICLE 32 vs ARTICLE 226


High Courts also have writ powers under **Article 226**.


But their powers are **broader**.


---


# DIFFERENCE BETWEEN ARTICLE 32 AND ARTICLE 226


| Feature      | Article 32                  | Article 226                             |
| ------------ | --------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Court        | Supreme Court               | High Court                              |
| Purpose      | Only for Fundamental Rights | Fundamental Rights + other legal rights |
| Jurisdiction | Original                    | Original                                |
| Scope        | Narrow                      | Wider                                   |
| Nature       | Fundamental Right itself    | Not a fundamental right                 |


---


### Supreme Court View


If remedy is available in High Court, the aggrieved person should **first approach High Court**.


However:


Availability of alternative remedy **does not bar Article 32**.


---


# WRITS — TYPES AND SCOPE


The Supreme Court and High Courts can issue **five types of writs**.


These writs are borrowed from **English law**.


They were originally known as **Prerogative Writs**.


Reason:


They were issued by the **King of England**, considered the “fountain of justice”.


---


# FIVE WRITS

1. Habeas Corpus
2. Mandamus
3. Prohibition
4. Certiorari
5. Quo Warranto

---


# 1. HABEAS CORPUS


Meaning:


**“To have the body.”**


Purpose:


Protects **personal liberty**.


---


### When issued


Issued when a person is:

- illegally detained
- unlawfully imprisoned

---


### Court Order


The court orders the authority to:


**produce the detained person before the court**.


If detention is illegal:


The person is released.


---


### Who can file


Petition can be filed by:

- Detained person
- Friend
- Relative
- Any concerned individual

---


# 2. MANDAMUS


Meaning:


**“We command.”**


Purpose:


Command a public authority to perform its duty.


---


### Issued against

- Public officials
- Government
- Public corporations
- Lower courts

---


### Not issued against

- President
- Governor
- Private individuals

---


### Example


Court ordering a government authority to perform a legal duty.


---


# 3. PROHIBITION


Meaning:


**“To forbid.”**


Purpose:


Issued by higher court to **prevent lower court from exceeding jurisdiction**.


---


### Issued against

- Judicial authorities
- Quasi-judicial bodies

---


### When used


Before the lower court completes the case.


---


# 4. CERTIORARI


Meaning:


**“To be certified.”**


Purpose:


Quash the order of lower court.


---


### Issued when


Lower court:

- exceeds jurisdiction
- acts illegally
- violates natural justice

---


### Difference from Prohibition


| Prohibition            | Certiorari              |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Prevents future action | Quashes completed order |


---


# 5. QUO WARRANTO


Meaning:


**“By what authority.”**


Purpose:


Prevent illegal occupation of public office.


---


### Court asks

> Under what authority do you hold this office?

---


### Conditions


Office must be:

- Public office
- Created by law

---


### Result


If appointment illegal:


Court removes the person from office.


---


# WRIT JURISDICTION IN INDIA


Before the Constitution:


Only three High Courts had writ powers:

- Calcutta High Court
- Bombay High Court
- Madras High Court

After Constitution:


All High Courts can issue writs under **Article 226**.
