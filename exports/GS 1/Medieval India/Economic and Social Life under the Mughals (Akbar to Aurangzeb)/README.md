
# A. CHAPTER NOTES


# 1. Overview

- Mughal Empire reached **territorial zenith by end of 17th century**.
- Period from **Akbar to Aurangzeb** shows broad continuity in economic and social structures.
- No fundamental economic transformation, but important developments in:
    - Agriculture
    - Trade
    - Urbanization
    - Foreign commerce
    - Social stratification

---


# 2. Standard of Living: Village Life and Masses


## Evidence from Foreign Travellers


### Babur

- Peasants wore very little clothing.
- Men used **langota**.
- Women used **sari**.

### Ralph Fitch

- Banaras people wore only cloth around waist.
- Mentioned quilted cotton gowns in winter.

### De Laet

- Labourers lacked adequate winter clothing.

### Nikitin

- Deccan people mostly barefoot.

### Moreland

- Shoes uncommon north of Narmada except Bengal.
- Leather costly.

# F. READY-MADE MAINS CONTENT


## Introductions

1. Mughal India witnessed a remarkable expansion of agriculture, trade and urbanization, laying the foundations of a highly commercialized pre-modern economy.
2. The Mughal economy combined agrarian prosperity with extensive domestic and overseas trade networks, making India one of the world's leading manufacturing centres.
3. Between Akbar and Aurangzeb, India experienced economic integration, urban growth and commercialization despite the persistence of feudal structures.

---


## Conclusions

1. Mughal India's economy was dynamic and deeply connected to Asian and global trade networks, though constrained by heavy agrarian extraction.
2. The period reflects both prosperity and inequality, where economic growth coexisted with peasant vulnerability.
3. Mughal institutions promoted trade and monetization, but technological stagnation and political crises later limited sustained transformation.

---


## Value-Added Points

- India was the **"Manufactory of Asia."**
- Bernier called India the **"Sink of Gold and Silver."**
- About **20% rural produce entered markets**.
- Mughal rupee became a standard international currency.
- Nobility increasingly invested in trade and commerce.
- Hundi system integrated regional markets.
- Cash crops accelerated commercialization.
- Urbanization expanded through trade and handicrafts.
- Indigenous merchants successfully resisted European monopoly.
- Composite nobility strengthened political integration.
