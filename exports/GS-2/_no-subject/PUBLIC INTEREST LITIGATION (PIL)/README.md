# 1. ORIGIN OF PUBLIC INTEREST LITIGATION


### In the United States

- The concept of **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)** originated in the **USA during the 1960s**.
- It was designed to **provide legal representation to previously unrepresented groups**.

### Reason for its development


The normal legal system failed to represent certain sections of society.


These included:

- Poor people
- Environmental groups
- Consumers
- Racial and ethnic minorities
- Other disadvantaged groups

Thus PIL became a **tool to represent interests that were ignored by the normal legal market.**


---


# 2. ORIGIN OF PIL IN INDIA


In India, PIL emerged from **Judicial Activism of the Supreme Court**.


### Period of introduction

- **Early 1980s**

### Key Judges who pioneered PIL

1. **Justice V. R. Krishna Iyer**
2. **Justice P. N. Bhagwati**

These judges expanded access to justice by **relaxing procedural rules** and encouraging courts to intervene for **public causes**.


---


# 3. OTHER NAMES OF PIL


PIL is known by several other terms:

1. **Social Action Litigation (SAL)**
2. **Social Interest Litigation (SIL)**
3. **Class Action Litigation (CAL)**

---


# 4. MEANING OF PUBLIC INTEREST LITIGATION


### Traditional Rule: Locus Standi


Under traditional law:

- Only the **person whose rights are violated** could approach the court.

This rule is known as:


**Doctrine of Locus Standi**


---


### Change Introduced by PIL


PIL **relaxes the rule of locus standi**.


Under PIL:

- **Any public-spirited citizen**
- **Social organization**

can approach the court on behalf of:

- Poor
- Illiterate
- Economically disadvantaged
- Socially backward people

who **cannot approach the court themselves**.


Thus, a PIL allows:

> Any member of the public having **“sufficient interest”** to approach the court.

---


# 5. SUPREME COURT DEFINITION OF PIL


The Supreme Court defined PIL as:

> A legal action initiated in a court of law for enforcement of public interest or general interest in which the public or a class of the community has pecuniary interest or legal rights affected.

---


# 6. IMPORTANCE OF PIL


PIL is important because it helps in:


### 1. Maintaining Rule of Law


Ensures government actions remain within the law.


### 2. Access to Justice


Allows **socially and economically weaker sections** to access courts.


### 3. Protection of Fundamental Rights


Accelerates the **realisation of constitutional rights**.


---


# 7. OBJECTIVES OF PIL


The real purposes of PIL are:

1. **Vindication of Rule of Law**
2. **Facilitating access to justice for weaker sections**
3. **Meaningful realization of Fundamental Rights**

---


# 8. FEATURES OF PIL


The important characteristics of PIL are:


---


## 1. Strategic Arm of Legal Aid


PIL is a **part of the legal aid movement**.


Purpose:

- Bring justice to **poor and marginalized groups**
- Reach **low-visibility sections of humanity**

---


## 2. Different from Traditional Litigation


Traditional litigation:

- Two opposing parties
- Adversarial system

PIL:

- Focuses on **public welfare**
- Not a dispute between two individuals

---


## 3. Objective: Public Interest


PIL is **not meant to enforce individual rights against another individual**.


Instead it aims to:

- Protect **public interest**
- Protect **community rights**

---


## 4. Protection of Disadvantaged Groups


PIL ensures that violations of constitutional rights of:

- Poor
- Illiterate
- Socially disadvantaged people

do not remain unnoticed.


---


## 5. Cooperative Effort


PIL involves cooperation between:

1. Petitioner
2. State / Public Authority
3. Court

Goal:


**Social justice and enforcement of constitutional rights**


---


## 6. Protection of Public Rights


PIL aims to:

- Redress public injury
- Enforce public duty
- Protect collective and social rights

---


## 7. Active Role of Court


In PIL:

- Courts take a **more proactive and creative role**
- Unlike traditional passive adjudication

---


## 8. Flexible Procedure


Courts may adopt **flexible procedures**.


But the procedure must still follow:

- Judicial principles
- Legal fairness

---


## 9. No Adjudication of Individual Rights


Unlike traditional cases:

- PIL does **not focus on individual disputes**
- It focuses on **community rights**

---


# 9. SCOPE OF PIL


The **Supreme Court issued guidelines in 1988** regarding which matters can be treated as PIL.


These guidelines were later **modified in 1993 and 2003**.


Only certain categories of petitions are usually accepted as PIL.


---


# Categories Accepted as PIL


The following issues can be entertained as PIL:


### 1. Bonded labour cases


### 2. Neglected children


### 3. Labour exploitation


Including:

- Non-payment of minimum wages
- Violation of labour laws
- Exploitation of casual workers

_(except individual disputes)_


---


### 4. Prisoners’ rights


Includes petitions relating to:

- Jail harassment
- Premature release
- Release after **14 years imprisonment**
- Death in jail
- Transfer of prisoners
- Release on personal bond
- Speedy trial as Fundamental Right

---


### 5. Police harassment


Examples:

- Police refusing to register FIR
- Police harassment
- Death in police custody

---


### 6. Crimes against women


Includes:

- Bride harassment
- Bride burning
- Rape
- Murder
- Kidnapping

---


### 7. Atrocities on SC/ST and weaker sections


Includes:

- Harassment by police
- Harassment by villagers

---


### 8. Environmental matters


Includes:

- Environmental pollution
- Ecological disturbance
- Drug abuse
- Food adulteration
- Protection of heritage
- Protection of culture and antiques
- Forest and wildlife protection

---


### 9. Riot victims


Cases related to victims of riots.


---


### 10. Family pension cases


Petitions relating to **family pension disputes**.


---


# 10. CASES NOT ENTERTAINED AS PIL


The Supreme Court clarified that certain matters **cannot normally be treated as PIL**.


The following categories are **not entertained as PIL**:


### 1. Landlord–Tenant Disputes


Disputes between landlords and tenants are considered **private disputes**, not matters of public interest.


---


### 2. Service Matters


These include disputes related to:

- Promotion
- Transfer
- Salary
- Service conditions

Such issues must be addressed through **service tribunals or regular courts**.


---


### 3. Pension and Gratuity Matters


Personal disputes relating to:

- Pension
- Retirement benefits
- Gratuity

are **not treated as PIL**.


---


### 4. Complaints against Government Departments or Local Bodies


General complaints against:

- Central Government departments
- State Government departments
- Local authorities

are **not treated as PIL** unless they fall within the **recognized PIL categories** listed earlier.


---


### 5. Educational Admissions


Petitions relating to:

- Admission in medical colleges
- Admission in other educational institutions

are **not entertained as PIL**.


---


### 6. Early Hearing of Cases


Petitions requesting **expedited hearing of pending cases in High Courts or subordinate courts** are not PIL matters.


---


# 11. PRINCIPLES OF PIL (SUPREME COURT)


The Supreme Court developed several **guiding principles for PIL**.


These principles were elaborated in the case:


**Guruvayur Devaswom Managing Committee v. C.K. Rajan (2003)**


---


## Principle 1


The court can entertain petitions under:

- **Article 32** (Supreme Court)
- **Article 226** (High Courts)

even when filed by **any interested person**.


Purpose:


To protect **disadvantaged groups** who cannot approach the court themselves.


The court is **constitutionally obligated to protect Fundamental Rights**.


---


## Principle 2


When matters involve **public importance or fundamental rights of many people**, the court may even treat:

- **Letters**
- **Telegrams**

as PIL petitions.


In such cases:

- Procedural laws are **relaxed**
- Technical requirements are **not strictly applied**.

---


## Principle 3


When injustice affects **a large number of people**, the court may invoke:

- **Article 14 (Equality before law)**
- **Article 21 (Right to life and personal liberty)**

The court may also refer to **international human rights conventions** ensuring fair trial.


---


## Principle 4


The rule of **locus standi is relaxed**.


This allows courts to hear petitions filed on behalf of:

- Poor
- Illiterate
- Disabled
- Socially disadvantaged groups

who cannot seek justice themselves.


---


## Principle 5


If the court is satisfied that **constitutional rights are violated**, the State cannot easily challenge the **maintainability of the petition**.


---


## Principle 6


Procedural doctrines such as:


**Res Judicata**


may apply depending on:

- Nature of the case
- Circumstances involved.

### Meaning of Res Judicata


A matter **once decided by a competent court cannot be reopened again** between the same parties.


Purpose:


To ensure **finality of litigation**.


---


## Principle 7


Purely **private disputes between individuals** cannot be converted into PIL.


Example:

- Property disputes
- Personal contractual disputes

---


## Principle 8


Sometimes a petition may start as a **personal grievance**.


But if the court finds **public interest involved**, it may treat the case as **PIL**.


---


## Principle 9


Courts may appoint:

- **Commissions**
- **Committees**
- **Investigative bodies**

to examine allegations and determine facts.


The court may even place **public institutions under such commissions temporarily**.


---


## Principle 10


Courts should normally **avoid interfering in policy matters**.


Judiciary must respect **separation of powers**.


---


## Principle 11


Courts should operate within the **limits of judicial review**.


Important point:


High Courts **do not possess powers similar to Article 142** of the Constitution.


### Article 142


Allows the **Supreme Court to pass orders necessary for complete justice**.


---


## Principle 12


High Courts normally **should not entertain PIL challenging the constitutionality of statutes or statutory rules**.


Such cases should be addressed through **proper constitutional litigation**.


---


# 12. GUIDELINES FOR ADMITTING PIL


Over time, PIL began to be misused.


Many petitions were filed for:

- Publicity
- Political motives
- Personal gain

Because of this, the Supreme Court warned that PIL should not become:

- **Publicity Interest Litigation**
- **Politics Interest Litigation**
- **Private Interest Litigation**
- **Paisa Interest Litigation**
- **Middle-Class Interest Litigation (MIL)**.

---


# 13. SUPREME COURT OBSERVATION


The Supreme Court stated:


PIL **is not a universal solution for all problems**.


It was created primarily to **protect basic human rights of disadvantaged sections of society**.


However, increasing misuse required **clear guidelines to regulate PIL**.


---


# 14. GUIDELINES TO PREVENT MISUSE OF PIL


In the case:


**State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaupal (2010)**


the Supreme Court issued important guidelines.


---


## Guideline 1


Courts should encourage **genuine PIL** and discourage **frivolous or motivated PIL**.


---


## Guideline 2


High Courts should create **formal rules and procedures** for PIL.


Purpose:


To identify genuine petitions and filter out misuse.


---


## Guideline 3


Courts should verify the **credentials of the petitioner** before admitting the PIL.


---


## Guideline 4


Courts must check the **accuracy of facts mentioned in the petition**.


---


## Guideline 5


The court should ensure that **substantial public interest** is involved.


---


## Guideline 6


Cases involving **urgent and serious public issues** should receive priority over other petitions.


---


## Guideline 7


Courts must ensure that PIL seeks **redressal of genuine public harm or public injury**.


There must be **no personal gain or hidden motives**.


---


## Guideline 8


Petitions filed by **busybodies or motivated individuals** should be discouraged.


Courts may impose **heavy costs or penalties** to prevent misuse.


---
