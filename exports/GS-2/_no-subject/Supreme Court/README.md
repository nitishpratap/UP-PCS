
## SUPREME COURT OF INDIA -REVISION

# 1. INDIAN JUDICIARY SYSTEM


**India → Integrated Judicial System**


Hierarchy


**Supreme Court → High Courts → District Courts → Subordinate Courts**


Key Feature


Same courts enforce **Central + State laws**


### India vs USA


| India                        | USA                    |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Integrated judiciary         | Dual judiciary         |
| Single court system          | Federal + State courts |
| Same courts enforce all laws | Separate enforcement   |


**Conclusion:** India = Federal polity + **Unitary judiciary**


---

**Inaugurated:** **28 January 1950**


Replaced


• **Federal Court of India (1935)**


• **Privy Council (London)**


Thus SC became **highest court of appeal in India**

# SUPREME COURT OF INDIA


---


# 1. Integrated Judicial System of India


India has **a single integrated judicial system** with the **Supreme Court at the top**.


Hierarchy of Courts:

1. **Supreme Court**
2. **High Courts**
3. **District Courts**
4. **Subordinate Courts**

Key Feature:

- The **same system of courts enforces both Central and State laws**.

### Contrast with USA


| India                                      | USA                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Integrated judicial system                 | Dual judicial system                                                    |
| One system of courts                       | Separate federal and state courts                                       |
| Same courts enforce central and state laws | Federal courts enforce federal laws and state courts enforce state laws |


Thus, **India is federal but has a unified judiciary**.


---


# 2. Establishment of Supreme Court


The **Supreme Court of India was inaugurated on 28 January 1950**.


It replaced:

- **Federal Court of India (1935)**
- **Privy Council (London)** as the final appellate court.

Thus, the Supreme Court became the **highest court of appeal in India**.


---


# 3. Constitutional Provisions


Articles **124 to 147 (Part V)** deal with the Supreme Court.


They cover:

- Organisation
- Independence
- Jurisdiction
- Powers
- Procedures

Parliament is authorised to **regulate these matters by law**.


---

**Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019**.

# 5. Appointment of Judges


Judges of Supreme Court are **appointed by the President of India**.


### Appointment of Chief Justice


The President appoints the **Chief Justice of India** after consultation with:

- Judges of Supreme Court
- Judges of High Courts (if required)

---


### Appointment of Other Judges


Other judges are appointed by the **President after consultation with**:

- Chief Justice of India
- Other judges of Supreme Court
- Judges of High Courts (if necessary)

Consultation with **Chief Justice of India is compulsory**.


---


# 6. Evolution of Collegium System


The meaning of **“consultation”** was clarified through three major cases.


---


## First Judges Case (1982)


Supreme Court held:


**Consultation ≠ Concurrence**


Meaning:


Government was **not bound by CJI’s opinion**.


---


## Second Judges Case (1993)


Court reversed earlier decision.


Held:


**Consultation = Concurrence**


Meaning:

- **CJI’s advice became binding on President.**
- Advice must be formed after consulting **two senior-most judges**.

This created the **Collegium System**.


---


## Third Judges Case (1998)


Clarified collegium system.


CJI must consult:


**4 senior-most judges of Supreme Court**


Important rule:


If **2 judges oppose the recommendation**,


the CJI **should not send the recommendation to the government**.


---


## Fourth Judges Case (2015)


Government tried to replace collegium with **NJAC (The National Judicial Appointments Commission)**.


### NJAC System


Created by:

- **99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014**
- **National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014**

Purpose:


Replace collegium system.


However in **2015**, Supreme Court declared:

- **99th Constitutional Amendment unconstitutional**
- **NJAC Act void**

Reason:


It **threatened judicial independence**.


Therefore:


**Collegium System continues today.**


---


# 7. Appointment of Chief Justice of India


### Traditional Convention


From **1950 to 1973**:


**Senior-most judge becomes CJI**


---


### Violations of Convention


1️⃣ **1973**


Justice **A.N. Ray** was appointed CJI


superseding three senior judges.


2️⃣ **1977**


Justice **M.U. Beg** appointed CJI


superseding **Justice H.R. Khanna**.

> Justice H.R. Khanna was a legendary Supreme Court of India judge famous for his lone, courageous dissent in the 1976 _ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla_ case, where he upheld fundamental liberties during the Emergency at the cost of his elevation to Chief Justice

Both supersessions caused major controversies.


---


### Present Rule


After **Second Judges Case (1993)**:


**Senior-most judge of Supreme Court is appointed CJI.**


---


# 8. Qualifications of Supreme Court Judge


A person must satisfy **any one of the following**.


### Basic Requirement

- Must be a **Citizen of India**

### Legal Qualifications

1. Served as **High Court judge for 5 years**

OR

1. Served as **High Court advocate for 10 years**

OR

1. A **distinguished jurist** (in President's opinion)

---


### Important Fact


The Constitution **does NOT prescribe minimum age** for Supreme Court judge.


Very important for prelims.


---


# 9. Oath of Supreme Court Judge


Before entering office, judge must take oath before:

- **President**, or
- Person appointed by President.

In oath, judge swears:

1. True faith and allegiance to **Constitution of India**
2. Uphold **sovereignty and integrity of India**
3. Perform duties **without fear or favour**
4. Uphold **Constitution and laws**

---


# 10. Tenure of Judges


The Constitution does **not fix tenure**, but provides:


1️⃣ Judge serves **till age of 65 years**


2️⃣ Judge can **resign** by writing to President


3️⃣ Judge can be **removed by impeachment**


---


# 11. Removal of Judges (Impeachment)


A judge can be removed **only by the President**.


But only after **Parliament passes a removal address**.


Grounds:

1. **Proved misbehaviour**
2. **Incapacity**

---


## Special Majority Required


Each House must pass motion with:

- Majority of **total membership**

AND

- **2/3 majority of members present and voting**

---


# 12. Judges Enquiry Act, 1968 Procedure


Step-by-Step Removal Process


1️⃣ Motion signed by

- **100 Lok Sabha members**

    OR

- **50 Rajya Sabha members**

2️⃣ Submitted to

- Speaker (Lok Sabha)

    OR

- Chairman (Rajya Sabha)

3️⃣ Speaker/Chairman may:

- Admit or reject motion

4️⃣ If admitted → **3 member inquiry committee formed**


Committee consists of:

- Supreme Court judge
- High Court Chief Justice
- Distinguished jurist

5️⃣ If committee finds judge guilty → Parliament debates motion


6️⃣ Both Houses pass motion with special majority


7️⃣ President issues removal order


---


### Important Fact


**No Supreme Court judge has been impeached so far.**


---


### Only Attempted Impeachment


**Justice V. Ramaswami (1991–1993)** 

> A scandal surfaced in the middle of the year 1990 when several media outlets reported about his ostentatious expenditure on his official residence during his tenure as a Chief Justice of Punjab and Haryana.

Inquiry committee found him guilty.


But impeachment failed because **Congress abstained in Lok Sabha**.

# 14. Acting Chief Justice of India


(Article 126)


The **President can appoint a judge of the Supreme Court as Acting Chief Justice**.


This happens in the following situations:

1. **Office of Chief Justice of India is vacant**
2. **Chief Justice is temporarily absent**
3. **Chief Justice is unable to perform duties of office**

Thus, an acting CJI performs all duties of the Chief Justice until the situation normalizes.


---


# 15. Ad Hoc Judges


(Article 127)


Sometimes there may be **lack of quorum of permanent judges** in the Supreme Court.


In such situations:


The **Chief Justice of India can appoint a High Court judge as an ad hoc judge of the Supreme Court**.


Conditions:

1. Must consult **Chief Justice of the concerned High Court**
2. Must obtain **previous consent of the President**
3. Judge must be **qualified to become Supreme Court judge**

---


### Position of Ad Hoc Judge


During this period:

- He enjoys **all powers and privileges of a Supreme Court judge**
- He must **attend Supreme Court sittings in priority to other duties**

---


# 16. Retired Judges


(Article 128)


The **Chief Justice of India may request a retired judge to sit in the Supreme Court**.


Eligible persons:

1. Retired **Supreme Court judge**
2. Retired **High Court judge qualified to be SC judge**

Conditions:

1. Must have **consent of President**
2. Must have **consent of the retired judge**

---


### Status of Retired Judge Sitting in SC

- Enjoys **all powers of Supreme Court judge**
- Gets **allowances determined by President**
- But **not considered a permanent judge**

---


# 17. Seat of Supreme Court


(Article 130)


The Constitution declares:


**Delhi is the seat of the Supreme Court.**


However:


The **Chief Justice of India may appoint another place as seat of Supreme Court**.


Condition:

- Requires **approval of the President**

Important point:


This provision is **optional**, not mandatory.


Thus:


No court can compel the President or CJI to change the seat of the Supreme Court.


---


# 18. Procedure of the Supreme Court


(Article 145)


The Supreme Court can **make rules for regulating its practice and procedure**.


But this requires:


**Approval of the President**


---


### Bench Composition

1. **Constitutional cases**

Bench of **at least 5 judges**


Examples:

- Interpretation of Constitution
- Presidential references
1. **Other cases**

Usually decided by **bench of at least 3 judges**


---


### Judgement Procedure


Important characteristics:

1. Judgements delivered in **open court**
2. Decisions based on **majority opinion**
3. Judges may give **dissenting opinions**

Dissenting opinion means:


A judge disagrees with the majority judgement but records his reasoning.


---


# 19. Independence of the Supreme Court


The Supreme Court plays a **very crucial role in Indian democracy**.


It acts as:

- **Federal Court**
- **Highest Court of Appeal**
- **Guardian of Constitution**
- **Protector of Fundamental Rights**

Therefore:


Its independence is essential.


The judiciary must remain free from:

- Executive interference
- Legislative pressure
- Political influence

To ensure this, the Constitution provides **several safeguards**.


---


# Constitutional Safeguards for Judicial Independence


There are **10 important safeguards**.


These are **very important for UPSC/UPPCS prelims and mains**.


---


# 1. Mode of Appointment


Judges are appointed by **President in consultation with judiciary**.


This ensures:

- Executive cannot appoint judges arbitrarily
- Judicial opinion is involved

Thus political influence is reduced.


---


# 2. Security of Tenure


Judges cannot be removed easily.


Removal possible only through **impeachment by Parliament**.


Thus judges do **not serve at the pleasure of President**.


This protects them from executive pressure.


Important fact:


**No Supreme Court judge has been removed so far.**


---


# 3. Fixed Service Conditions


Parliament determines:

- Salaries
- Allowances
- Privileges
- Leave
- Pension

But after appointment:


These **cannot be reduced to disadvantage of judges**


Exception:


**Financial Emergency**


Thus financial independence is ensured.


---


# 4. Expenses Charged on Consolidated Fund


Expenses of Supreme Court include:

- Salaries of judges
- Staff salaries
- Administrative expenses

These are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of India**.


Meaning:


Parliament **cannot vote on them**.


However, Parliament **may discuss them**.


This prevents political pressure through financial control.


---


# 5. Conduct of Judges Cannot Be Discussed


Parliament or State Legislature **cannot discuss the conduct of judges**.


Exception:


Only during **impeachment proceedings**.


Thus judges are protected from political criticism.


---


# 6. Ban on Practice after Retirement


Retired Supreme Court judges **cannot practice law in any court or authority in India**.


Purpose:


To prevent them from favouring parties during their tenure in hope of future legal practice benefits.


---


# 7. Power to Punish for Contempt


Supreme Court has the power to punish for **contempt of court**.


This protects:

- Authority
- Dignity
- Independence of judiciary

Punishment may include:

- Imprisonment
- Fine

---


# 8. Freedom to Appoint Staff


The **Chief Justice of India appoints officers and staff of Supreme Court**.


Executive cannot interfere.


CJI also decides their **service conditions**.


---


# 9. Jurisdiction Cannot Be Curtailed


Parliament **cannot reduce jurisdiction of Supreme Court**.


However:


Parliament **can expand its jurisdiction**.


Thus the court’s powers remain constitutionally protected.


---


# 10. Separation of Judiciary from Executive


The Constitution directs the State to ensure separation of judiciary from executive.


This principle appears in:


**Article 50 (Directive Principles of State Policy)**.


Implementation happened through:


**Criminal Procedure Code, 1973**


This ensured that executive officers no longer perform judicial functions.


---


# Summary: Safeguards of Judicial Independence

1. Mode of appointment
2. Security of tenure
3. Fixed service conditions
4. Expenses charged on Consolidated Fund
5. Conduct of judges cannot be discussed
6. Ban on practice after retirement
7. Contempt power
8. Freedom to appoint staff
9. Jurisdiction cannot be curtailed
10. Separation from executive

---


## PART 3 – Jurisdiction and Powers


The Constitution has given **very extensive jurisdiction and powers** to the Supreme Court.


The Supreme Court functions as:

1. **Federal Court**
2. **Highest Court of Appeal**
3. **Guardian of the Constitution**
4. **Protector of Fundamental Rights**
5. **Advisory Body to the President**

Alladi Krishnaswamy Ayyar (Drafting Committee member) stated:

> “The Supreme Court of India has more powers than any other Supreme Court in any part of the world.”

---


# Types of Jurisdiction of Supreme Court


The jurisdiction and powers of the Supreme Court can be classified into **seven categories**:

1. Original Jurisdiction
2. Writ Jurisdiction
3. Appellate Jurisdiction
4. Advisory Jurisdiction
5. Court of Record
6. Power of Judicial Review
7. Other Powers

---


# 1. Original Jurisdiction


(Article 131)


Original jurisdiction means:


The Supreme Court has the power **to hear a dispute in the first instance**, not by appeal.


This jurisdiction relates to **federal disputes**.


---


## Federal Disputes Covered


The Supreme Court decides disputes between:

1. **Centre and one or more states**
2. **Centre and one or more states vs other states**
3. **Two or more states**

---


### Nature of Jurisdiction


This jurisdiction is:


**Exclusive Original Jurisdiction**


Meaning:

- **Exclusive** → Only Supreme Court can decide
- **Original** → Case begins directly in Supreme Court

No other court has authority.


---


## Important Conditions


For Supreme Court to hear such disputes:

1. Dispute must involve **a legal right**
2. Questions of **purely political nature are excluded**
3. **Private citizens cannot file such disputes**

---


## Matters Excluded from Original Jurisdiction


Supreme Court’s original jurisdiction **does not extend to** the following:

1. Disputes arising from **pre-Constitution treaties or agreements**
2. Disputes where treaty explicitly excludes Supreme Court jurisdiction
3. **Inter-state water disputes**
4. Matters referred to **Finance Commission**
5. Adjustment of certain **expenses and pensions between Centre and States**
6. **Commercial disputes** between Centre and States
7. Recovery of damages by a State against the Centre

---


### Example


The **first suit under Article 131**:


1961 case:


**State of West Bengal vs Union of India**


Issue:


West Bengal challenged validity of:


**Coal Bearing Areas (Acquisition and Development) Act, 1957**


Supreme Court **dismissed the suit** and upheld the Act.


---


# 2. Writ Jurisdiction


(Article 32)


The Supreme Court acts as the **guarantor and defender of Fundamental Rights**.


Therefore, it can issue **writs**.


These writs are issued to enforce Fundamental Rights.


---


## Five Types of Writs

1. **Habeas Corpus**

Meaning:


“Produce the body”


Used when a person is **illegally detained**.


---

1. **Mandamus**

Meaning:


“We command”


Court orders a public authority to **perform its duty**.


---

1. **Prohibition**

Issued by higher court to **stop a lower court from exceeding jurisdiction**.


---

1. **Certiorari**

Issued to **quash the order of a lower court or tribunal**.


---

1. **Quo Warranto**

Meaning:


“By what authority?”


Court asks a person **to justify holding a public office**.


---


## Nature of Supreme Court Writ Jurisdiction


It is **Original Jurisdiction**.


A citizen can **directly approach the Supreme Court** for protection of Fundamental Rights.


---


## Important Comparison


Supreme Court vs High Court Writ Power


| Supreme Court                               | High Court                              |
| ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Can issue writs only for Fundamental Rights | Can issue writs for FR + other purposes |
| Jurisdiction narrower                       | Jurisdiction wider                      |


Thus:


**High Court writ jurisdiction is wider.**


However:


Parliament may **extend Supreme Court writ power**.


---


# 3. Appellate Jurisdiction


Supreme Court is the **highest court of appeal in India**.


It hears appeals against judgements of lower courts.


Earlier appeals went to **British Privy Council**.


Now Supreme Court is final appellate authority.


---


## Types of Appeals


There are **four types of appeals**.

1. Constitutional appeals
2. Civil appeals
3. Criminal appeals
4. Appeals by Special Leave

---


# (A) Appeals in Constitutional Matters


(Article 132)


Appeal can be made from High Court if:


High Court certifies that:


The case involves **substantial question of law related to Constitution**.


---


# (B) Appeals in Civil Matters


(Article 133)


Civil appeal allowed if High Court certifies:

1. Case involves **substantial question of law of general importance**

AND

1. That question needs decision of Supreme Court.

---


### Earlier Rule


Civil appeals required property value of **₹20,000 or more**.


This requirement was **removed by the 30th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1972**.


---


# (C) Appeals in Criminal Matters


(Article 134)


Appeal lies to Supreme Court if High Court:

1. Reverses acquittal and **sentences accused to death**
2. Withdraws case from subordinate court and **sentences accused to death**
3. Certifies case is fit for appeal

---


### Appeals as Matter of Right


In the first two situations:


No High Court certificate needed.


Appeal automatically allowed.


---


### Important Note


If High Court **reverses conviction and acquits (बरी करना) accused**,


no appeal lies to Supreme Court.


---


# Expansion of Criminal Jurisdiction (1970)


Parliament expanded Supreme Court’s criminal appellate jurisdiction.


Appeal now possible if High Court:

1. Reverses acquittal and gives **life imprisonment or 10-year sentence**
2. Withdraws case and gives **life imprisonment or 10-year sentence**

---


# (D) Appeal by Special Leave


(Article 136)


Supreme Court has power to grant **Special Leave to Appeal (SLP)**.


This is one of the **widest powers of Supreme Court**.


---


## Key Features of Special Leave

1. **Discretionary power**

No one can claim it as a right.


---

1. Can be granted against **any judgement**

Final or interlocutory.


---

1. Applies to **any matter**

Examples:

- Constitutional
- Civil
- Criminal
- Labour
- Revenue
- Income tax

---

1. Can be against **any court or tribunal**

Except:

- **Military courts**
- **Court martial**

---


### Nature of Power


Supreme Court itself stated:


---


# 4. Advisory Jurisdiction


(Article 143)


The Constitution authorises the **President of India to seek the opinion of the Supreme Court**.


This is known as:


**Advisory Jurisdiction** or **Consultative Jurisdiction**.


The President can ask the Supreme Court's opinion in **two types of matters**.


---


# Categories of Matters


### 1. Question of Law or Fact of Public Importance


President may refer:

- Any **question of law**
- Any **question of fact**

Provided it is of **public importance**.


In this case:


The Supreme Court **may or may not give its opinion**.


---


### 2. Disputes Related to Pre-Constitution Agreements


These include disputes arising out of:

- Treaties
- Agreements
- Covenants
- Engagements
- Sanads

These were agreements between the **Government of India and princely states before the Constitution came into force**.


In this case:


The Supreme Court **must give its opinion**.


---


# Nature of Advisory Opinion


Important feature:


The opinion of the Supreme Court **is not binding on the President**.


The President may:

- Accept the advice
- Reject the advice

However, it provides **authoritative legal guidance**.


---


# Presidential References Made So Far


The President has made **15 references** to the Supreme Court.


Chronological order:

1. **Delhi Laws Act (1951)**
2. **Kerala Education Bill (1958)**
3. **Berubari Union (1960)**
4. **Sea Customs Act (1963)**
5. **Keshav Singh Case – Legislative Privileges (1964)**
6. **Presidential Election (1974)**
7. **Special Courts Bill (1978)**
8. **Jammu & Kashmir Resettlement Act (1982)**
9. **Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (1992)**
10. **Ram Janmabhoomi Case (1993)**
11. **Consultation Process for Judges Appointment (1998)**
12. **Natural Gas Legislative Competence (2001)**
13. **Gujarat Assembly Elections Issue (2002)**
14. **Punjab Termination of Agreements Act (2004)**
15. **2G Spectrum Case Issue (2012)**

These are **important for prelim questions**.


---


# 5. Supreme Court as a Court of Record


(Article 129)


The Supreme Court is a **Court of Record**.


This gives it **two powers**.


---


# 1. Records are Permanent Evidence


The following are recorded permanently:

- Judgements
- Proceedings
- Acts of the Court

These records:

- Have **evidentiary value**
- Cannot be questioned in any court

They serve as **legal precedents**.


---


# 2. Power to Punish for Contempt


The Supreme Court can punish any person for:


**Contempt of Court**


Punishment may include:

- Imprisonment up to **6 months**
- Fine up to **₹2000**
- Or both

---


# Contempt Power Expanded (1991)


Supreme Court ruled that it can punish contempt of:

- Supreme Court
- High Courts
- Subordinate courts
- Tribunals

---


# Types of Contempt of Court


## 1. Civil Contempt


Civil contempt means:


**Wilful disobedience of court order**


Examples:

- Ignoring a court order
- Violating an undertaking given to court

---


## 2. Criminal Contempt


Criminal contempt includes acts that:

1. Scandalise or lower authority of court
2. Interfere with judicial proceedings
3. Obstruct administration of justice

---


# What Is NOT Contempt?


The following are not considered contempt:

- Innocent publication
- Fair reporting of court proceedings
- Reasonable criticism of judicial decisions
- Comment on administrative functioning of judiciary

---


# 6. Power of Judicial Review


Judicial Review is the power of the Supreme Court to **examine the constitutionality of laws and executive actions**.


The Court checks whether:

- Parliamentary laws
- State laws
- Executive orders

are consistent with the **Constitution**.


---


### If law violates Constitution


The Supreme Court can declare it:

- **Unconstitutional**
- **Illegal**
- **Null and Void**

Such law **cannot be enforced**.


---


### Purpose of Judicial Review


Judicial review protects:

1. **Supremacy of Constitution**
2. **Fundamental Rights of citizens**
3. **Federal balance**

---


# 7. Other Powers of Supreme Court


Besides the above jurisdictions, the Supreme Court has **several additional powers**.


---


# 1. Election Disputes of President and Vice-President


The Supreme Court decides disputes related to elections of:

- **President**
- **Vice-President**

Nature of authority:

- **Original**
- **Exclusive**
- **Final**

---


# 2. Inquiry Against UPSC Members


The Supreme Court investigates misconduct of:

- Chairman of UPSC
- Members of UPSC

If found guilty:


Court recommends their removal to the **President**.


This advice is **binding on the President**.


---


# 3. Power to Review Its Own Judgement


(Article 137)


Supreme Court can **review its own judgements**.


Thus it is a **self-correcting institution**.


Example:


The Court changed its earlier judgement in:


**Kesavananda Bharati Case (1973)**


which modified the earlier ruling in


**Golaknath Case (1967)**.


---


# 4. Power to Transfer Cases


(Article 139A)


Supreme Court can:

- Withdraw cases from High Courts
- Transfer cases between High Courts

---


# 5. Law Declared by Supreme Court Binding


(Article 141)


The law declared by Supreme Court is **binding on all courts in India**.


---


# 6. Enforcement of Decrees


(Article 142)


Orders and decrees of Supreme Court are **enforceable throughout India**.


All authorities must **assist the Supreme Court**.


---


# 7. Final Interpreter of Constitution


The Supreme Court gives the **final interpretation of the Constitution**.


It defines the **meaning and scope of constitutional provisions**.


---


# 8. Judicial Superintendence


The Supreme Court exercises **superintendence over all courts and tribunals** in India.


This is possible because India has an **integrated judicial system**.


---


# Expansion of Jurisdiction


Parliament may **expand Supreme Court jurisdiction**:

1. In matters related to **Union List**
2. Through **special agreement between Centre and States**

---


# Supreme Court Advocates


There are **three categories of advocates who can practice before the Supreme Court**.


---


# 1. Senior Advocates


Designated by:

- Supreme Court
- High Court

Criteria:

- Ability
- Standing at the Bar
- Special knowledge of law

Restrictions:


Senior advocates:

- Cannot appear without Advocate-on-Record
- Cannot draft pleadings
- Cannot file affidavits

They work with junior advocates.


---


# 2. Advocates-on-Record (AOR)


Only **Advocates-on-Record** can:

- File cases in Supreme Court
- Submit documents
- Represent parties officially

Thus AORs play a **key procedural role**.


---


# 3. Other Advocates


These advocates:

- Are registered with State Bar Councils
- Can **argue cases in Supreme Court**

But cannot:

- File documents
- Initiate cases

---
