
# 1. INTRODUCTION


### Position in Judicial System


India has a **single integrated judicial system**.


Hierarchy:

1. **Supreme Court**
2. **High Courts**
3. **Subordinate Courts**

Thus,


**High Court = Highest court at the state level.**


It supervises the **entire judicial administration of the state**.


---


# 2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND OF HIGH COURTS


### Origin


High Courts in India were established in **1862**.


The first three High Courts:


| High Court | Year |
| ---------- | ---- |
| Calcutta   | 1862 |
| Bombay     | 1862 |
| Madras     | 1862 |


Established under **Indian High Courts Act, 1861**.


Later:


| Court                | Year |
| -------------------- | ---- |
| Allahabad High Court | 1866 |


Gradually every province in **British India** got its own High Court.


---


### After Independence


After **1950**, High Courts of provinces became **High Courts of states**.


The Constitution provides:

> **One High Court for each state.**

But Parliament may establish:

> **Common High Court for two or more states or states + UT**

This power was given by:


**7th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1956**


---


### Present Situation


India currently has:


**24 High Courts**


Important facts:

- **4 are common High Courts**
- **Delhi is the only UT with its own High Court (1966)**
- Other UTs come under nearby state High Courts.

Parliament can:

- extend High Court jurisdiction to a UT
- remove jurisdiction from a UT.

---


# 3. CONSTITUTIONAL PROVISIONS


High Courts are covered under:


### Articles 214 – 231


in **Part VI of the Constitution**


These deal with:

- organisation
- independence
- jurisdiction
- powers
- procedures

---


# 4. ORGANISATION OF HIGH COURT


Each High Court consists of:


1️⃣ **Chief Justice**


2️⃣ **Other Judges**


Important:


The **Constitution does not fix the number of judges**.


The number is decided by:

> **President of India**

based on **workload of the High Court**.


---


# 5. JUDGES OF HIGH COURT


## Appointment of Judges


Judges are appointed by:

> **President of India**

### Appointment of Chief Justice


Consultation required with:

1. Chief Justice of India
2. Governor of the concerned state

---


### Appointment of Other Judges


Consultation required with:

1. Chief Justice of India
2. Governor of the state
3. Chief Justice of the High Court concerned

---


### In Case of Common High Court


President consults:

- Governors of all concerned states.

---


# 6. COLLEGIUM SYSTEM (IMPORTANT FOR EXAMS)


### Second Judges Case (1993)


Case:


**Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v Union of India**


Judgement:

> Appointment of High Court judges must conform with the opinion of **Chief Justice of India**.

This strengthened judicial role in appointments.


---


### Third Judges Case (1998)


The Court clarified:


For High Court appointments:


CJI must consult a **collegium of two senior-most Supreme Court judges**.


Thus:


CJI's individual opinion is **not enough**.


---


### NJAC Attempt


Government tried to replace collegium.


### 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014


### NJAC Act, 2014


Created:


**National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)**.


---


### Fourth Judges Case (2015)


Case:


**Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association vs Union of India**


Supreme Court decision:

- 99th Amendment **unconstitutional**
- NJAC **void**

Reason:

> It threatens **judicial independence**.

Thus,


**Collegium system restored.**


---


# 7. QUALIFICATIONS OF HIGH COURT JUDGES


A person must:


1️⃣ Be a **Citizen of India**


AND


2️⃣ Either


**(a)** Held a **judicial office in India for 10 years**


OR


**(b)** Been an **advocate of a High Court(s) for 10 years**


---


### Important Differences from Supreme Court


High Court judges:

- No **minimum age requirement**
- No provision to appoint **distinguished jurists**

(Unlike Supreme Court).


---


# 8. OATH OR AFFIRMATION


Before assuming office, the judge must take oath before:

> **Governor of the State**

or a person appointed by the Governor.


---


### Contents of Oath


The judge swears to:


1️⃣ Bear **true faith and allegiance to the Constitution**


2️⃣ Uphold **sovereignty and integrity of India**


3️⃣ Perform duties **without fear or favour, affection or ill-will**


4️⃣ Uphold **Constitution and laws**


---


# 9. TENURE OF HIGH COURT JUDGES


The Constitution does **not fix tenure**.


However:


### A judge holds office until:

> **62 years of age**

---


### Retirement Age


Originally:


60 years


Changed by:


**15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963**


Now:


**62 years**


---


### If Age Dispute Occurs


Decided by:

> **President of India**

after consulting **Chief Justice of India**


Decision is **final**.


---


### Other Ways Office Ends


A High Court judge may vacate office if:


1️⃣ Resigns to the **President**


2️⃣ Removed by **President after Parliamentary process**


3️⃣ Appointed as **Supreme Court judge**


4️⃣ Transferred to **another High Court**


---


# 10. REMOVAL OF HIGH COURT JUDGES


A judge can be removed by:

> **President of India**

But only after **Parliamentary impeachment process**.


---


### Grounds for Removal


Two grounds only:


1️⃣ **Proved Misbehaviour**


2️⃣ **Incapacity**


---


### Majority Required


Each House must pass the motion by **Special Majority**.


Meaning:


1️⃣ Majority of **total membership**


AND


2️⃣ **2/3rd of members present and voting**


---


### Same Procedure as Supreme Court Judges


Yes.


Removal procedure is **identical**.


---


# 11. JUDGES ENQUIRY ACT, 1968


This Act regulates the **impeachment procedure**.


---


### Step-by-Step Process


### Step 1


Removal motion signed by:

- **100 Lok Sabha members**

OR

- **50 Rajya Sabha members**

---


### Step 2


Submitted to:

- Speaker (Lok Sabha)

OR

- Chairman (Rajya Sabha)

---


### Step 3


Speaker/Chairman may:

- Admit the motion

OR

- Reject it

---


### Step 4


If admitted:


A **3-member inquiry committee** is formed.


Members:


1️⃣ Supreme Court judge / Chief Justice


2️⃣ Chief Justice of a High Court


3️⃣ Distinguished jurist


---


### Step 5


Committee investigates charges.


---


### Step 6


If guilty:


House considers removal motion.


---


### Step 7


Both Houses pass motion with **special majority**.


---


### Step 8


Address sent to **President**.


---


### Step 9


President issues **removal order**.


---


### Important Fact

> **No High Court judge has been impeached so far.**

---


# 12. SALARIES AND ALLOWANCES


Determined by:

> **Parliament**

They include:

- salary
- allowances
- privileges
- leave
- pension

---


### Protection


They **cannot be reduced** after appointment.


Exception:

> **Financial Emergency**

---


### Salary Revision (2009)


| Position      | Salary        |
| ------------- | ------------- |
| Chief Justice | ₹90,000/month |
| Judge         | ₹80,000/month |


Earlier:

- CJ – ₹30,000
- Judge – ₹26,000

---


### Additional Benefits


Judges receive:

- Sumptuary allowance
- Free accommodation
- Medical facilities
- Car
- Telephone

---


### Pension


Retired judges receive:

> **50% of last drawn salary**

---


# 13. TRANSFER OF HIGH COURT JUDGES


### Constitutional Provision


Transfer of judges is governed by **Article 222**.


### Authority


The **President of India** can transfer a judge from one High Court to another.


But consultation is required with:

> **Chief Justice of India (CJI)**

---


### Compensatory Allowance


When a judge is transferred, he receives:

- Salary
- **Additional compensatory allowance**

This allowance is determined by **Parliament**.


---


### Supreme Court Judgements on Transfer


### 1️⃣ Supreme Court judgement (1977)


The Court ruled:


Transfer of High Court judges should be:

- **Exceptional measure**
- Done only in **public interest**
- **Not used as punishment**

---


### 2️⃣ Supreme Court judgement (1994)


Court held:


Transfer decisions can be **judicially reviewed** to prevent arbitrariness.


However:

> Only the **transferred judge** can challenge the transfer.

---


### 3️⃣ Third Judges Case (1998)


In case of transfer, the **CJI must consult**:

1. **Collegium of four senior-most Supreme Court judges**
2. **Chief Justice of the High Court from which judge is transferred**
3. **Chief Justice of the High Court receiving the judge**

Thus:


The opinion of **CJI alone is not sufficient**.


---


# 14. ACTING CHIEF JUSTICE


### Article 223


The **President appoints an Acting Chief Justice** when:


1️⃣ Office of Chief Justice is **vacant**


2️⃣ Chief Justice is **temporarily absent**


3️⃣ Chief Justice is **unable to perform duties**


In such cases:


A **judge of that High Court** is appointed as Acting Chief Justice.


---


# 15. ADDITIONAL AND ACTING JUDGES


### Article 224


The President can appoint **Additional Judges** or **Acting Judges**.


---


## Additional Judges


Additional judges are appointed when:


1️⃣ There is a **temporary increase in the workload**


2️⃣ There are **arrears (बकाया) of cases**


---


### Duration


Additional judges are appointed for:

> **Maximum period of 2 years**

---


## Acting Judges


Acting judges are appointed when a judge of High Court (not Chief Justice):


1️⃣ Is **temporarily absent**


2️⃣ Is **unable to perform duties**


3️⃣ Is **acting as Chief Justice**


---


### Tenure of Acting Judge


An acting judge serves until:

> The **permanent judge resumes office**

---


### Age Limit


Both **Additional and Acting judges cannot hold office after 62 years**.


---


# 16. RETIRED JUDGES


### Article 224A


The **Chief Justice of High Court** can request a **retired judge** to sit and act as a judge of the High Court.


Conditions:


1️⃣ **Consent of the President**


2️⃣ **Consent of the retired judge**


---


### Rights of Retired Judge


While acting as judge, the retired judge gets:

- Jurisdiction of High Court judge
- Powers
- Privileges

He also receives **allowances determined by the President**.


However:

> He is **not considered a permanent judge of that High Court**.

---


# 17. INDEPENDENCE OF HIGH COURT


Judicial independence is essential because the High Court must:

- Deliver justice **without fear or favour**
- Remain free from **executive or legislative interference**

The Constitution provides **multiple safeguards** to ensure this independence.


---


# SAFEGUARDS OF INDEPENDENCE


## 1. Mode of Appointment


Judges are appointed by:

> **President**

But consultation required with:

- Chief Justice of India
- Chief Justice of High Court

This prevents **political appointments**.


---


## 2. Security of Tenure


High Court judges **do not serve at the pleasure of the President**.


They can only be removed through:

> **Parliamentary impeachment process**

This provides **security of tenure**.


---


## 3. Fixed Service Conditions


Conditions of service like:

- Salary
- Allowances
- Pension
- Privileges

are determined by **Parliament**.


But they **cannot be reduced after appointment**.


Exception:

> **Financial Emergency**

---


## 4. Expenses Charged on Consolidated Fund


The following are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of the State**:

- Salaries of High Court judges
- Salaries of staff
- Administrative expenses

Because they are **charged expenditure**, they are:

> **Not subject to vote of state legislature**

They can only be **discussed**.


---


### Important Exception


The **pension of High Court judges** is charged on:

> **Consolidated Fund of India**

---


## 5. Conduct of Judges Cannot Be Discussed


The Constitution prohibits discussion about conduct of High Court judges:

- in **Parliament**
- in **State Legislature**

Exception:

> When **impeachment motion is under consideration**.

---


## 6. Ban on Practice After Retirement


Retired permanent judges of High Court **cannot practice**:

- in any **court**
- before any **authority in India**

Exception:


They can practice only in:

- **Supreme Court**
- **Other High Courts**

Purpose:


Prevent judges from **misusing position for future benefits**.


---


## 7. Power to Punish for Contempt


High Court has the power to punish:

> **Contempt of Court**

Punishment may include:

- Fine
- Imprisonment
- Both

This ensures:

- Authority
- Dignity
- Respect of judiciary.

---


## 8. Freedom to Appoint Staff


The **Chief Justice of High Court** has authority to:

- appoint officers and servants of the High Court

He also decides:

- conditions of service.

Executive interference is not allowed.


---


## 9. Jurisdiction Cannot Be Curtailed


Jurisdiction provided by the Constitution **cannot be reduced by legislature**.


However:


In other areas, Parliament or State Legislature can modify jurisdiction.


---


## 10. Separation from Executive


The Constitution directs the State to separate:

> **Judiciary from Executive**

This is mentioned under:


**Article 50 (Directive Principles of State Policy)**.


Implementation:


The **Criminal Procedure Code, 1973** separated judicial functions from executive authorities.


---


# IMPORTANT EXAM POINT


UPPCS often asks:


**Why judiciary is independent?**


Answer:


Because of the above **10 constitutional safeguards**.


---


# 18. JURISDICTION AND POWERS OF HIGH COURT


The High Court has **extensive powers** similar to the Supreme Court.


It acts as:

- Highest court of appeal in the state
- Protector of Fundamental Rights
- Interpreter of Constitution
- Supervisor of subordinate courts

---


### Important Constitutional Fact


The Constitution **does not list detailed powers of High Courts**.


Instead, it states that:

> High Courts will have the **same jurisdiction as they had before the Constitution**.

However, the Constitution **added some new powers**, such as:

- Writ jurisdiction
- Judicial review
- Supervisory jurisdiction

Also:

> Parliament and State Legislature may **expand or modify the jurisdiction**.

---


### Sources of High Court Powers


Present powers of High Courts come from:


1️⃣ Constitution of India


2️⃣ Letters Patent


3️⃣ Acts of Parliament


4️⃣ Acts of State Legislature


5️⃣ Indian Penal Code (1860)


6️⃣ Criminal Procedure Code (1973)


7️⃣ Civil Procedure Code (1908)


---


# TYPES OF JURISDICTION


A High Court has **7 major jurisdictions/powers**:


1️⃣ Original jurisdiction


2️⃣ Writ jurisdiction


3️⃣ Appellate jurisdiction


4️⃣ Supervisory jurisdiction


5️⃣ Control over subordinate courts


6️⃣ Court of record


7️⃣ Judicial review


---


# 1. ORIGINAL JURISDICTION


Original jurisdiction means:

> The power to hear cases **directly for the first time**, not on appeal.

---


### Matters Covered


High Courts can hear directly cases related to:


1️⃣ Admiralty cases


2️⃣ Wills


3️⃣ Marriage


4️⃣ Divorce


5️⃣ Company law


6️⃣ Contempt of court


---


### Other Cases


High Courts also hear:

- Election disputes of **MPs and MLAs**
- **Revenue matters**
- **Enforcement of Fundamental Rights**
- Cases transferred from subordinate courts involving **constitutional interpretation**

---


### Special Original Civil Jurisdiction


Four High Courts have special **original civil jurisdiction**:

1. Calcutta
2. Bombay
3. Madras
4. Delhi

These courts can directly hear **high-value civil cases**.


---


### Original Criminal Jurisdiction


Earlier:


Calcutta, Bombay, Madras had criminal original jurisdiction.


But this was **abolished by CrPC 1973**.


---


# 2. WRIT JURISDICTION


### Article 226


High Courts can issue writs.


Types of writs:


1️⃣ Habeas Corpus


2️⃣ Mandamus


3️⃣ Certiorari


4️⃣ Prohibition


5️⃣ Quo Warranto


---


### Purpose of Writs


High Court can issue writs for:


1️⃣ Enforcement of **Fundamental Rights**


2️⃣ Enforcement of **ordinary legal rights**


This is very important.


---


### Important Difference from Supreme Court


| Supreme Court               | High Court            |
| --------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Article 32                  | Article 226           |
| Only for Fundamental Rights | For FR + legal rights |
| Narrow jurisdiction         | Wider jurisdiction    |


Thus:

> High Court writ jurisdiction is **wider than Supreme Court**.

---


### Territorial Jurisdiction


High Court can issue writs to:

- Any **person**
- Any **authority**
- Any **government**

Even **outside its territorial jurisdiction** if:

> Cause of action arises within its territory.

This provision was added by:


**15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963**


---


### Concurrent Jurisdiction


Both **Supreme Court and High Courts** can enforce Fundamental Rights.


Thus:


Citizens can approach either.


---


### Important Case


**L. Chandra Kumar Case (1997)**


Supreme Court ruled:

> Writ jurisdiction of High Courts and Supreme Court is part of the **Basic Structure of the Constitution**.

Therefore:


It **cannot be removed even by constitutional amendment**.


---


# 3. APPELLATE JURISDICTION


High Court is primarily a **Court of Appeal**.


It hears appeals from:

- Subordinate courts
- District courts

This jurisdiction is **broader than original jurisdiction**.


---


## A. Civil Appellate Jurisdiction


### First Appeals


Appeals from:

- District Courts
- Additional District Courts
- Subordinate courts

Allowed when the **amount involved exceeds the prescribed limit**.


Appeal can involve:

- Question of law
- Question of fact

---


### Second Appeals


Allowed only when the case involves:

> **Substantial question of law**

Not questions of fact.


---


### Intra-Court Appeals


In:

- Calcutta
- Bombay
- Madras

If a case is decided by a **single judge**, appeal can be made to:

> **Division Bench of the same High Court**

---


### Appeals from Tribunals


Appeals from decisions of:

- Administrative tribunals
- Other tribunals

go to the **Division Bench of the High Court**.


---


### Important Judgement


**L. Chandra Kumar Case (1997)**


Supreme Court ruled:


Tribunals are subject to:

> **Writ jurisdiction of High Courts**

Therefore:


Citizens must first approach **High Court before Supreme Court**.


---


## B. Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction


High Court hears appeals against decisions of:

- Sessions courts
- Additional sessions courts

---


### Appeals Allowed When


If sentence is:

> **More than 7 years imprisonment**

---


### Death Sentence Rule


If Sessions Court awards:

> **Death sentence**

It must be **confirmed by High Court** before execution.


Even if there is **no appeal**.


---


### Other Criminal Appeals


Appeals from:

- Assistant Sessions Judges
- Metropolitan Magistrates
- Judicial Magistrates

may also go to High Court.


---


# 4. SUPERVISORY JURISDICTION


### Article 227


High Court has **superintendence over all courts and tribunals** within its territory.


Exception:

> Military courts.

---


### Powers Under Supervisory Jurisdiction


High Court may:


1️⃣ Call for returns from subordinate courts


2️⃣ Issue rules for their functioning


3️⃣ Prescribe forms for records and registers


4️⃣ Fix fees for officials and legal practitioners


---


### Nature of Power


This power is **very broad** because:

- Applies to all courts and tribunals
- Includes **administrative and judicial supervision**
- Can be exercised **suo motu**

---


### But Used Carefully


Used only in cases of:

- Excess of jurisdiction
- Violation of natural justice
- Error of law
- Disregard of higher court rulings
- Perverse findings
- Manifest injustice

---


# 5. CONTROL OVER SUBORDINATE COURTS


High Court has administrative control over subordinate judiciary.


---


### Powers Include


1️⃣ Governor consults High Court for:

- Appointment of District Judges
- Judicial service recruitment (except district judges)

---


2️⃣ High Court manages:

- Posting
- Promotion
- Transfers
- Leave
- Discipline of judicial officers

---


3️⃣ High Court may withdraw cases involving:

> **Substantial constitutional question**

Then either:

- Decide the case itself

OR

- Return case after deciding legal question.

---


4️⃣ Decisions of High Court are **binding on subordinate courts**.


Just like Supreme Court decisions bind all courts in India.


---


# 6. HIGH COURT AS COURT OF RECORD


### Article 215


High Court is a **Court of Record**.


---


### Meaning


Court of Record means:


1️⃣ Its records have **evidentiary value**


They cannot be questioned in any subordinate court.


2️⃣ It has the power to punish for **contempt of court**.


---


# CONTEMPT OF COURT


Defined in:

> **Contempt of Court Act, 1971**

Two types:


---


### 1. Civil Contempt


Wilful disobedience of:

- Court orders
- Judgements
- Writs
- Undertakings to court

---


### 2. Criminal Contempt


Acts that:

- Scandalize the court
- Lower court authority
- Interfere with judicial proceedings
- Obstruct administration of justice

---


### Not Contempt


These are allowed:

- Fair reporting of judicial proceedings
- Fair criticism of judicial acts
- Comments on administrative functioning

---


### Power of Review


High Courts can:

> Review their own judgments

Even though the Constitution does **not explicitly grant review power**.


But Supreme Court has **explicit review power under Article 137**.


---


# 7. POWER OF JUDICIAL REVIEW


Judicial review means:

> Examining the constitutionality of laws and executive actions.

If found unconstitutional:


They are declared:

- **Ultra vires**
- **Null and void**

---


### Articles Providing Judicial Review


Although the phrase is not mentioned in Constitution, power comes from:

- **Article 13**
- **Article 226**

---


### Grounds of Judicial Review


High Court can invalidate laws if:


1️⃣ Violation of **Fundamental Rights**


2️⃣ Authority exceeded its **constitutional competence**


3️⃣ Law is **repugnant to Constitution**


---


### 42nd Amendment (1976)


It tried to **limit High Court judicial review**.


It prevented High Courts from reviewing **Central laws**.


---


### 43rd Amendment (1977)


Restored **original powers of High Courts**.
