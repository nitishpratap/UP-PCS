# 1. TERRITORIAL EXTENT OF LEGISLATION


## Parliament


Can legislate for:

- Whole of India
- Any part of India

**Territory of India includes**

- States
- Union Territories
- Territories acquired later

---


## State Legislature


Can legislate for:

- Whole state
- Any part of state

Normally cannot operate outside state.


### Doctrine of Territorial Nexus


State law may apply outside state if **sufficient connection exists**.


Example: Company registered in one state operating in another.


---


## Extra Territorial Legislation


Only **Parliament** can make laws affecting:

- Indian citizens abroad
- Indian property abroad

# LIST SUPREMACY


Hierarchy


```plain text
Union List > Concurrent List > State List
```


Meaning

- Union vs State → **Union prevails**
- Union vs Concurrent → **Union prevails**
- Concurrent vs State → **Concurrent prevails**

Rule:


➡ Central law prevails


Exception:


If

- State law reserved for President
- President gives assent

Then


➡ State law prevails **in that state**


BUT


➡ Parliament can override later

# 3. PARLIAMENTARY LEGISLATION IN STATE FIELD


Normally Parliament **cannot legislate on State List**


Allowed in **5 situations**


---


## 1. National Interest Resolution


**Article 249**


Rajya Sabha may allow Parliament to legislate.


Conditions

- **2/3 majority**
- Valid **1 year**
- Can be renewed

Law valid **6 months after expiry**


---


## 2. During National Emergency


**Article 250**


If **Art 352 emergency**


Parliament can legislate on State List.


Law valid **6 months after emergency ends**


---


## 3. When States Request


**Article 252**


Two or more states request Parliament.


Applies to

- Requesting states
- Other states adopting later

States **cannot amend or repeal**


Only Parliament can.


Examples

- Prize Competition Act 1955
- Wildlife Protection Act 1972
- Water Pollution Act 1974
- Urban Land Ceiling Act 1976
- Transplantation of Human Organs Act 1994

---


## 4. International Agreements


**Article 253**


Parliament may legislate to implement:

- Treaties
- Conventions
- International agreements

Examples

- UN Privileges Act 1947
- Geneva Convention Act 1960
- Anti-Hijacking Act 1982

---


## 5. During President’s Rule


**Article 356**


Parliament legislates on State List for that state.


Law continues after President's Rule.


State legislature may later:

- Amend
- Repeal
- Re-enact

---

---


### 3. Financial Emergency


**Article 360**


President may require states to reserve:

- Money bills

# CENTRE–STATE RELATIONS (UPPCS NOTES)


## 1. Introduction


India has a **federal system of government**, where powers are divided between:

- **Union (Centre)**
- **States**

The Constitution divides powers into:

1. Legislative powers
2. Executive powers
3. Financial powers

However:


⚠️ **Judicial powers are NOT divided.**


India has an **Integrated Judicial System** where:

- **Supreme Court** is at the top
- **High Courts** below it

This system enforces both:

- Central laws
- State laws

---


## Why Centre–State Coordination is Important


Even though both Centre and States are **supreme in their respective spheres**, harmony is necessary for the **successful working of federalism**.


Therefore the Constitution contains **detailed provisions regulating Centre–State relations**.

# 1. Territorial Extent of Central and State Legislation


The Constitution defines **territorial jurisdiction** of laws.


---


## Parliament


Parliament can make laws for:

- Whole of India
- Any part of India

**Territory of India includes**

- States
- Union Territories
- Any other territory included later

---


## State Legislature


State legislature can make laws for:

- Whole state
- Any part of the state

⚠️ Normally **state laws cannot operate outside the state**.


However, they may apply outside the state if there is a **sufficient nexus**.


Example:


If a company registered in State A operates in State B, State A law may apply.


---


## Extra Territorial Legislation


Only **Parliament** can make **extra-territorial laws**.


Meaning:


Indian laws can apply to:

- Indian citizens abroad
- Indian property abroad

---


## Restrictions on Parliament's Territorial Power


Parliamentary laws **may not apply automatically** in certain areas.


### 1. Union Territories Regulations by President


President can make regulations for:

- Andaman & Nicobar Islands
- Lakshadweep
- Dadra & Nagar Haveli
- Daman & Diu

These regulations have the **same force as an Act of Parliament**.


They can also:

- Amend
- Repeal Parliamentary Acts.

---


### 2. Scheduled Areas


Governor may direct that:


Parliamentary laws

- **do not apply**
- or apply **with modifications**

in **Scheduled Areas**.


---


### 3. Tribal Areas


Governor of **Assam** can modify application of Parliamentary laws in tribal areas.


President has this power for:

- Meghalaya
- Tripura
- Mizoram

---

# List Supremacy (Very Important for Prelims)


Hierarchy:


**Union List > Concurrent List > State List**


Meaning:


1️⃣ If Union List and State List conflict → **Union prevails**


2️⃣ If Union List and Concurrent List conflict → **Union prevails**


3️⃣ If Concurrent List and State List conflict → **Concurrent prevails**


---


# Conflict in Concurrent List


Normally:


**Central law prevails over state law.**


### Exception


If:

- State law reserved for President
- President gives assent

Then:


State law prevails **in that state only**.


BUT


Parliament can later override it.


---


# 3. Parliamentary Legislation in State Field


Normally Parliament cannot legislate on State List.


But **5 exceptional situations allow it**.


---


# 1. Rajya Sabha Resolution (Article 249)


Rajya Sabha may declare:


Legislation on State List is necessary in **national interest**.


Conditions:

- Passed by **2/3 majority**
- Valid for **1 year**
- Can be renewed repeatedly.

Law remains valid **6 months after resolution expires**.


---


# 2. During National Emergency (Article 250)


If **National Emergency (Article 352)** is in force:


Parliament can legislate on **State List subjects**.


Law becomes invalid **6 months after emergency ends**.


---


# 3. When States Request Parliament (Article 252)


If **two or more states pass resolutions**, Parliament can legislate.


Law applies only to:

- Those states
- States adopting it later

States **cannot amend or repeal** it.


Only Parliament can.


Examples:

- Prize Competition Act 1955
- Wildlife Protection Act 1972
- Water Pollution Act 1974
- Urban Land Ceiling Act 1976
- Transplantation of Human Organs Act 1994

---


# 4. To Implement International Agreements (Article 253)


Parliament can legislate on **State List subjects** to implement:

- Treaties
- Conventions
- International agreements

Examples:

- UN Privileges and Immunities Act 1947
- Geneva Convention Act 1960
- Anti-Hijacking Act 1982
- Environmental laws

---


# 5. During President’s Rule (Article 356)


If President's Rule is imposed:


Parliament can legislate on **State List for that state**.


The law continues even after President's Rule ends.


But the state legislature can later:

- Repeal
- Modify
- Re-enact it.

---


# 4. Centre’s Control Over State Legislation


The Centre exercises indirect control in several ways.


---


## 1. Governor Reserving Bills


Governor can reserve state bills for **President’s consideration**.


President can exercise **absolute veto**.


---


## 2. Prior Presidential Sanction


Certain bills require **President’s prior approval** before introduction.


Example:


Bills restricting **freedom of trade and commerce**.


---


## 3. During Financial Emergency


President may direct states to reserve:

- Money bills
- Financial bills

for his consideration.


---


# Federal Supremacy


The Constitution ensures **Union supremacy** to avoid conflicts.


According to **Sarkaria Commission**:


Union supremacy prevents:

- Legal chaos
- Conflicting laws
- Administrative confusion.

9. Relations during emergencies
10. Other constitutional provisions

---


# 1. Distribution of Executive Powers


The **distribution of executive powers** generally follows the **distribution of legislative powers**.


### Executive Power of the Centre


The executive power of the Union extends to:


1️⃣ Matters on which **Parliament has power to legislate (Union List)**


2️⃣ Matters concerning **rights, authority and jurisdiction derived from treaties or agreements**


Thus, the executive authority of the Union extends **throughout India**.


---


### Executive Power of the States


The executive power of a state extends to:

- Matters on which the **state legislature can make laws (State List)**.

---


### Concurrent List


For matters in the **Concurrent List**:


Normally,


➡ **Executive power lies with the states**


Even if the law is made by Parliament.


However,


Parliament or the Constitution may assign executive authority to the **Centre** in specific cases.


---


# 2. Obligation of States and the Centre


The Constitution places **restrictions on state executive power** to ensure smooth functioning of the Union.


The executive power of states must be exercised:


1️⃣ **To ensure compliance with parliamentary laws** applicable in that state.


2️⃣ **Not to impede the executive power of the Union**.


Thus, states cannot act in a way that obstructs the Union's authority.


---


### Centre’s Enforcement Power


If necessary, the Centre can issue **directions to states**.


These directions are **binding**.


---


### Article 365 (Important)


If a state **fails to comply with Union directions**, the President may conclude that:

> The government of the state cannot be carried on according to the Constitution.

This may lead to **President’s Rule under Article 356**.


---


# 3. Centre’s Directions to States


Apart from the above obligations, the Union can give directions to states in specific matters.


These include:


### 1️⃣ Means of Communication


Construction and maintenance of **roads and communications of national or military importance**.


---


### 2️⃣ Protection of Railways


States must take necessary measures for the **protection of railways** within their territory.


---


### 3️⃣ Linguistic Minorities


States must provide **facilities for instruction in mother tongue at primary level** for linguistic minorities.


---


### 4️⃣ Welfare of Scheduled Tribes


States must implement **schemes for the welfare of Scheduled Tribes**.


---


# 4. Mutual Delegation of Functions


The legislative division between Centre and States is **rigid**.


However, in executive matters, some flexibility is allowed.


Thus the Constitution allows **delegation of executive functions**.


---


## Delegation by President


The **President may entrust Union functions to a state government**.


Conditions:

- Requires **consent of the state government**

Example:


Union schemes implemented by states.


---


## Delegation by Governor


The **Governor may entrust state functions to the Centre**.


Conditions:

- Requires **consent of the Central Government**.

---


## Delegation by Parliament


Parliament may:

- Confer powers on states
- Impose duties on states

Even **without state consent**.


This happens through **legislation on Union List subjects**.


---


### Important Point


States **cannot impose duties on the Centre through legislation**.


Only the Union has this authority.


---


# 5. Cooperation Between Centre and States


The Constitution provides mechanisms to promote cooperation.


---


## 1️⃣ Inter-State River Water Disputes


Parliament may provide for **adjudication of disputes regarding inter-state rivers**.


Example:


Cauvery dispute tribunal.


---


## 2️⃣ Inter-State Council


Under **Article 263**, the President may establish an **Inter-State Council**.


Functions:

- Investigate disputes between states
- Discuss subjects of common interest
- Recommend policies

The **Inter-State Council was established in 1990**.


---


## 3️⃣ Full Faith and Credit Clause


Public acts, records and judicial proceedings of:

- Union
- States

must be **recognized throughout India**.


---


## 4️⃣ Inter-State Trade Authority


Parliament can appoint an authority to implement **freedom of trade and commerce between states**.


However,


⚠️ **No such authority has been created yet**.


---


# 6. All-India Services


India has three types of services:


| Type               | Example                    |
| ------------------ | -------------------------- |
| Central Services   | IRS, IFS (Foreign Service) |
| State Services     | State civil services       |
| All-India Services | IAS, IPS, IFS (Forest)     |


---


## All-India Services


Officers serve:

- Both **Centre and States**

But they are:

- **Recruited and trained by the Centre**.

---


### Existing All-India Services


1️⃣ Indian Administrative Service (IAS)


2️⃣ Indian Police Service (IPS)


3️⃣ Indian Forest Service (IFS)


---


### Historical Background


After independence:

- **ICS → IAS**
- **Indian Police → IPS**

IFS was created in **1966**.


---


### Article 312


Parliament can create **new All-India Services**.


Condition:


➡ Rajya Sabha must pass a **2/3 majority resolution**.


---


### Importance of All-India Services


They ensure:


1️⃣ High administrative standards


2️⃣ Uniformity in administration


3️⃣ Better Centre-State coordination


---


### Criticism


They are criticised because:


They **limit state autonomy**.


But they remain essential for national integration.


---


# 7. Public Service Commissions


The Constitution regulates Centre-State relations regarding PSCs.


---


### 1️⃣ Removal of State PSC Members


Members of **State PSC**:

- Appointed by **Governor**
- Removed only by **President**.

---


### 2️⃣ Joint State Public Service Commission


Parliament may create **Joint PSC** for multiple states.


Members are appointed by **President**.


---


### 3️⃣ UPSC Serving States


UPSC may serve a state if:

- Governor requests
- President approves.

---


### 4️⃣ Joint Recruitment Schemes


UPSC may assist states in **joint recruitment for specialised services**.


---


# 8. Integrated Judicial System


Unlike many federations, India does **not have dual judicial systems**.


India has **one unified judicial system**.


Structure:


Supreme Court


↓


High Courts


↓


Subordinate Courts


This system enforces both:

- Central laws
- State laws.

---


### Appointment of High Court Judges


High Court judges are appointed by the **President** after consultation with:

- Chief Justice of India
- Governor of the state.

---


### Common High Court


Parliament can establish **one High Court for multiple states**.


Examples:

- Punjab & Haryana High Court
- Bombay High Court (serving Maharashtra & Goa).

---


# 9. Administrative Relations During Emergencies


Centre-State relations change significantly during emergencies.


---


## 1️⃣ National Emergency (Article 352)


During National Emergency:

- Centre can give directions to states **on any matter**.

States remain in existence but **operate under Union control**.


---


## 2️⃣ President’s Rule (Article 356)


When imposed:


President can assume:

- Functions of state government
- Powers of governor or state executive.

---


## 3️⃣ Financial Emergency (Article 360)


Centre can direct states to:


1️⃣ Follow financial discipline


2️⃣ Reduce salaries of state officials


3️⃣ Reserve financial bills for President


---


# 10. Other Constitutional Provisions


### Article 355 – Duty of the Union


Union must:


1️⃣ Protect states from **external aggression and internal disturbance**


2️⃣ Ensure **state governments follow the Constitution**


---


### Role of Governor


Governor is:

- Appointed by the **President**
- Holds office during **President’s pleasure**

He also acts as **agent of the Centre**.


He sends **reports about state administration to the Centre**.


---


### State Election Commissioner


Appointed by the **Governor**.


But removed only by the **President**.


---


# 11. Extra-Constitutional Devices


Apart from constitutional provisions, several **non-constitutional bodies** promote coordination.


---


### Major Bodies

- Planning Commission (now **NITI Aayog**)
- National Development Council
- National Integration Council
- Zonal Councils
- North Eastern Council
- University Grants Commission

These facilitate **policy coordination between Centre and States**.


---


# PART 3 — FINANCIAL RELATIONS


## Constitutional Articles


**Articles 268 – 293 (Part XII)** deal with **financial relations between the Centre and the States**.


These relations cover:

1. Allocation of taxing powers
2. Distribution of tax revenues
3. Distribution of non-tax revenues
4. Grants-in-aid
5. Finance Commission
6. Protection of states’ financial interests
7. Borrowing powers
8. Inter-governmental tax immunities
9. Effects of emergencies

---


# 1. Allocation of Taxing Powers


The Constitution divides **taxation powers between Centre and States**.


### 1️⃣ Union Government


Parliament has exclusive power to levy taxes on subjects in the **Union List**.


➡ Total: **15 taxes**


Examples:

- Customs duties
- Corporation tax
- Income tax (except agricultural income)
- Excise duties

---


### 2️⃣ State Governments


States have exclusive power to levy taxes on **State List subjects**.


➡ Total: **20 taxes**


Examples:

- Land revenue
- Tax on agricultural income
- Tax on vehicles
- Tax on entertainment
- Excise on alcoholic liquor

---


### 3️⃣ Concurrent List Taxes


Both Centre and States can levy taxes on **3 subjects** in the Concurrent List.


---


### 4️⃣ Residuary Taxation Power


Residuary taxation powers belong to **Parliament**.


Example taxes introduced using this power:

- Gift tax
- Wealth tax
- Expenditure tax

---


# Important Constitutional Principle


The Constitution distinguishes between:


1️⃣ **Power to levy and collect tax**


2️⃣ **Power to appropriate tax revenue**


Example:


Income tax is:

- **Levied and collected by Centre**
- **Shared with states**

---


# Restrictions on State Taxation


States cannot exercise unlimited taxation power.


---


## 1️⃣ Tax on Professions


States may levy tax on:

- Professions
- Trades
- Callings
- Employment

But maximum tax allowed:


➡ **₹2500 per year**


---


## 2️⃣ Tax on Sale/Purchase of Goods


States can impose **sales tax** with restrictions.


States cannot tax sales:

1. Outside the state
2. During import/export
3. During inter-state trade
4. Goods declared by Parliament as important in interstate trade

---


## 3️⃣ Tax on Electricity


States can tax electricity consumption or sale.


However, they **cannot tax electricity**:

- Consumed by the Centre
- Used for railways

---


## 4️⃣ Tax on Inter-State River Projects


States can tax water/electricity generated from inter-state river projects.


But such laws must:

- Be reserved for President’s consideration
- Receive President’s assent

---


# 2. Distribution of Tax Revenues


Two important amendments changed the revenue sharing system:


### 80th Amendment Act (2000)


Implemented **10th Finance Commission recommendations**.


Key change:


➡ **29% of total central taxes to states**


Called:


**Alternative Scheme of Devolution**


It placed taxes like:

- Corporation tax
- Customs duties

at par with income tax for sharing with states.


---


### 88th Amendment Act (2003)


Introduced:

- **Service tax**
- **Article 268A**
- Entry **92C in Union List**

Service tax:

- Levied by Centre
- Collected and shared by Centre and states

---


# Current Distribution Categories


Tax revenues are divided into **six categories**.


---


# A. Taxes Levied by Centre but Collected by States


(Article 268)


Includes:

1. Stamp duties on financial instruments
2. Excise duty on medicinal and toilet preparations containing alcohol/narcotics

Revenue goes **entirely to states**.


These revenues **do not form part of Consolidated Fund of India**.


---


# B. Service Tax


(Article 268A)


Service tax:

- Levied by Centre
- Collected and appropriated by Centre and States

Principles determined by **Parliament**.


---


# C. Taxes Levied and Collected by Centre but Assigned to States


(Article 269)


Includes:

1. Taxes on inter-state sale or purchase of goods
2. Taxes on consignment of goods in inter-state trade

Revenue assigned to **states**.


---


# D. Taxes Levied and Collected by Centre but Shared with States


(Article 270)


Includes most **Union taxes** except:

1. Taxes under Articles 268, 268A, 269
2. Surcharge under Article 271
3. Cesses for specific purposes

Distribution is decided by:


➡ **President on Finance Commission recommendation**


---


# E. Surcharge on Certain Taxes


(Article 271)


Parliament may impose **surcharge** on taxes in Articles 269 & 270.


Revenue goes **only to Centre**.


States receive **no share**.


---


# F. Taxes Levied and Retained by States


These belong **exclusively to states**.


Examples:

- Land revenue
- Agricultural income tax
- Tax on vehicles
- Tax on entertainment
- Tax on gambling
- Excise on alcohol
- Entry tax
- Electricity tax
- Toll tax
- Stamp duty
- Sales tax

---


# 3. Distribution of Non-Tax Revenues


Apart from taxes, governments receive **non-tax revenue**.


---


## Centre’s Non-Tax Revenues


Major sources:

- Posts and telegraphs
- Railways
- Banking
- Broadcasting
- Currency and coinage
- Central PSUs
- Escheat and lapse

---


## States’ Non-Tax Revenues


Major sources:

- Irrigation
- Forests
- Fisheries
- State PSUs
- Escheat and lapse

---


# 4. Grants-in-Aid to States


The Constitution provides grants from Centre to states.


Two main types:


1️⃣ Statutory grants


2️⃣ Discretionary grants


---


# Statutory Grants


(Article 275)


Parliament provides grants to states **in need of financial assistance**.


Features:

- Different amounts for different states
- Charged on **Consolidated Fund of India**
- Based on **Finance Commission recommendations**

---


### Special Grants


Also provided for:

- Welfare of Scheduled Tribes
- Development of Scheduled Areas

---


# Discretionary Grants


(Article 282)


Both Centre and states can provide grants **for public purposes**.


Even if the subject is outside their legislative power.


These grants help:


1️⃣ States achieve plan targets


2️⃣ Centre influence national policies


Discretionary grants form **major portion of central assistance**.


---


# Temporary Grants (Historical)


The Constitution also provided **temporary grants**.


Example:


Grants in lieu of **export duties on jute and jute products** to:

- Assam
- Bihar
- Orissa
- West Bengal

These lasted **10 years after Constitution commencement**.


---


# 5. Finance Commission


### Article 280


Finance Commission is a **quasi-judicial body**.


It is constituted by the **President every 5 years**.


---


### Functions


The Finance Commission recommends:


1️⃣ Distribution of tax revenues between Centre and states


2️⃣ Allocation of funds among states


3️⃣ Principles governing **grants-in-aid**


4️⃣ Measures to augment **Consolidated Fund of states**


for:

- Panchayats
- Municipalities

5️⃣ Any other matter referred by President


---


### Additional Role (Historical)


Until 1960 it also recommended grants to:

- Assam
- Bihar
- Orissa
- West Bengal

in lieu of export duty on jute.


---


### Importance


Finance Commission is called:


➡ **Balancing wheel of fiscal federalism**


However its importance reduced due to **Planning Commission**.


---


# 6. Protection of States’ Financial Interests


Certain financial bills require **President’s recommendation before introduction in Parliament**.


These include bills that:


1️⃣ Impose tax affecting states


2️⃣ Change definition of agricultural income


3️⃣ Affect distribution of revenues


4️⃣ Impose surcharge on shared taxes


---


### Net Proceeds


Meaning:


**Tax revenue – cost of collection**


It is calculated by **CAG (Comptroller and Auditor General)**.


His certification is **final**.


---


# 7. Borrowing Powers


---


## Borrowing by Centre


The Central Government may borrow:

- Within India
- Outside India

On security of **Consolidated Fund of India**.


Limits are determined by **Parliament**.


---


## Borrowing by States


States may borrow:

- Only **within India**
- On security of **Consolidated Fund of State**

Limits determined by **State Legislature**.


---


## Loans from Centre


Centre may:

- Give loans to states
- Guarantee loans taken by states

---


## Restriction on States


If a state has an **outstanding loan from Centre**, it must obtain **Centre’s consent** before borrowing.


---


# 8. Inter-Governmental Tax Immunities


The Constitution provides **mutual tax immunity** between Centre and states.


---


# Exemption of Central Property from State Tax


State governments cannot tax:


➡ Property of the Union


Includes:

- Land
- Buildings
- Shares
- Debts
- Movable and immovable property

However:


Parliament may remove this exemption.


---


### Exception


Central government corporations **can be taxed by states**.


Because they are **separate legal entities**.


---


# Exemption of State Property from Central Tax


Property and income of states are **exempt from Union taxation**.


However:


Centre can tax **commercial activities of states** if Parliament provides.


---


### Supreme Court Opinion (1963)


State immunity does **not apply to customs or excise duties**.


Centre can impose:

- Customs duty
- Excise duty

on goods produced or imported by states.


---


# 9. Financial Relations During Emergencies


---


# National Emergency (Article 352)


President may modify financial relations.


Centre may:

- Reduce tax transfers
- Cancel grants

These changes last **till end of financial year after emergency ends**.


---


# Financial Emergency (Article 360)


Centre can direct states to:


1️⃣ Follow financial discipline


2️⃣ Reduce salaries of state officials


3️⃣ Reserve financial bills for President


---


# PART 4 — TRENDS IN CENTRE–STATE RELATIONS


## Early Phase (1950–1967)


For almost two decades after independence:

- The **Congress party ruled both the Centre and most states**.
- Therefore **Centre–State relations were smooth**.

There were **very few political conflicts**.


---


## Turning Point – 1967 Elections


In the **1967 general elections**:

- Congress lost power in **9 states**.
- Non-Congress governments came to power in several states.

This led to:

- Rising demands for **greater state autonomy**
- Increasing **conflicts between Centre and states**

States began demanding:

- More powers
- Greater financial resources
- Reduced central interference

---


# Major Areas of Tension in Centre–State Relations


The following issues caused conflicts between the Centre and states:


1️⃣ **Appointment and dismissal of Governors**


2️⃣ **Partisan role of Governors**


3️⃣ **Imposition of President’s Rule**


4️⃣ **Deployment of Central forces in states**


5️⃣ **Reservation of state bills for President**


6️⃣ **Discrimination in financial allocations**


7️⃣ **Role of Planning Commission in approving projects**


8️⃣ **Control of All-India Services**


9️⃣ **Use of electronic media for political purposes**


10️⃣ **Appointment of enquiry commissions against Chief Ministers**


11️⃣ **Financial sharing disputes**


12️⃣ **Encroachment by Centre on State List**


---


# Committees and Commissions on Centre–State Relations


Since the 1960s, many committees were formed to review Centre–State relations.


Important ones:

1. Administrative Reforms Commission (1966)
2. Rajamannar Committee (1969)
3. Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973)
4. West Bengal Memorandum (1977)
5. Sarkaria Commission (1983)
6. Punchhi Commission (2007)

---


# 1. Administrative Reforms Commission (1966)


### Chairman


➡ **Morarji Desai**


Later replaced by **K. Hanumanthaiah**.


---


### Study Team


A study team headed by **M.C. Setalvad** examined Centre–State relations.


---


### Report Submitted


➡ **1969**


---


### Important Recommendations


1️⃣ Establish **Inter-State Council under Article 263**


2️⃣ Governors should be **experienced and non-partisan persons**


3️⃣ Maximum **delegation of powers to states**


4️⃣ More **financial resources to states**


5️⃣ Deployment of central armed forces **even without state request**


---


### Implementation


The Central government **did not implement these recommendations**.


---


# 2. Rajamannar Committee (1969)


### Formed By


➡ **Tamil Nadu Government (DMK)**


---


### Chairman


➡ **Dr. P.V. Rajamannar**


---


### Report Submitted


➡ **1971**


---


### Reasons for Increasing Centralisation


The committee identified four reasons:


1️⃣ Constitutional provisions favouring Centre


2️⃣ One-party rule in Centre and states


3️⃣ Financial dependence of states


4️⃣ Role of Planning Commission


---


### Major Recommendations


1️⃣ Establish **Inter-State Council**


2️⃣ **Finance Commission should be permanent**


3️⃣ **Planning Commission should be abolished**


4️⃣ **Articles 356, 357, 365 should be removed**


5️⃣ State ministries should **not depend on Governor’s pleasure**


6️⃣ Some subjects from **Union and Concurrent List → State List**


7️⃣ **Residuary powers → States**


8️⃣ **All-India Services should be abolished**


---


### Result


➡ Central government **ignored the recommendations**.


---


# 3. Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973)


Adopted by **Akali Dal in Punjab**.


---


### Main Demand


The Centre’s jurisdiction should be limited only to:

- Defence
- Foreign affairs
- Communications
- Currency

All other powers should go to **states**.


---


### Objective


To make the Constitution **truly federal**.


---


# 4. West Bengal Memorandum (1977)


Prepared by **Communist Government of West Bengal**.


---


### Major Suggestions


1️⃣ Replace word **“Union” with “Federal”**


2️⃣ Limit Centre’s powers to:

- Defence
- Foreign affairs
- Currency
- Communications
- Economic coordination

3️⃣ **Residuary powers → States**


4️⃣ Repeal:

- Article 356
- Article 357
- Article 360

5️⃣ **State consent required for creation of new states**


6️⃣ **75% of central revenue to states**


7️⃣ Rajya Sabha should have **equal power with Lok Sabha**


8️⃣ **All-India Services should be abolished**


---


### Outcome


The Central government **rejected these demands**.


---


# 5. Sarkaria Commission (1983)


One of the **most important commissions** on Centre–State relations.


---


### Chairman


➡ **Justice R.S. Sarkaria**


Former judge of Supreme Court.


---


### Report Submitted


➡ **1987**


---


### Key Observation


The Commission stated:

> Federalism is a functional arrangement for cooperative action.

It emphasised **cooperative federalism**.


---


### Position on Centre’s Power


The Commission said:

- A **strong Centre is necessary** for national unity.
- But **over-centralisation should be avoided**.

---


### Total Recommendations


➡ **247 recommendations**


---


### Major Recommendations


1️⃣ Establish **permanent Inter-State Council**


2️⃣ Article 356 should be used **rarely and as last resort**


3️⃣ **Strengthen All-India Services**


4️⃣ Residuary taxation powers should remain with **Parliament**


5️⃣ Reasons must be given if **President rejects state bills**


6️⃣ National Development Council should become


**National Economic and Development Council**


7️⃣ Reactivate **Zonal Councils**


8️⃣ Centre may deploy armed forces **even without state consent**


9️⃣ States should be consulted before making laws on **Concurrent List**


10️⃣ Chief Minister should be consulted in **appointment of Governor**


11️⃣ Share **corporation tax with states**


12️⃣ Governor should not dismiss ministry having majority


13️⃣ Governor should normally complete **5-year term**


14️⃣ Enquiry commissions against state ministers only on **Parliament demand**


15️⃣ Surcharge on income tax should be used **only for limited time**


---


### Implementation


Out of 247 recommendations:


➡ **180 implemented**


Major achievement:


➡ **Inter-State Council established in 1990**.


---


# 6. Punchhi Commission (2007)


Second major commission on Centre–State relations.


---


### Chairman


➡ **Justice Madan Mohan Punchhi**


Former Chief Justice of India.


---


### Report Submitted


➡ **2010**


The report consisted of:


➡ **7 volumes and 1456 pages**


---


### Key Principle


The Commission emphasised:


➡ **Cooperative Federalism**


as essential for India's future.


---


### Total Recommendations


➡ **More than 310 recommendations**


---


# Major Recommendations


### Legislative Relations


1️⃣ Greater consultation with states before passing **Concurrent List laws**


2️⃣ Centre should exercise restraint in **using parliamentary supremacy**


---


### Role of Governor


3️⃣ Governor should:

- Be eminent
- Be from outside the state
- Be politically neutral

4️⃣ Governor should have **fixed 5-year tenure**


5️⃣ Governors should not hold **additional statutory posts**


---


### Chief Minister Appointment (Hung Assembly)


Order of preference:


1️⃣ Pre-poll alliance with majority


2️⃣ Largest party with support


3️⃣ Post-poll coalition


4️⃣ Minority government supported from outside


---


### President’s Rule


Article 356 should be used **only as last resort**.


Guidelines from **S.R. Bommai case (1994)** should be incorporated.


---


### Localised Emergency


A new concept suggested:


➡ **Localised emergency**


for situations requiring central intervention without dismissing state government.


---


### Inter-State Council


Make it a **strong constitutional body**.


---


### Zonal Councils


Meet **at least twice a year**.


---


### New All-India Services


Create services for:

- Health
- Education
- Engineering
- Judiciary

---


### Rajya Sabha Reform


States should have **equal representation**.


---


### Finance Commission


States should participate in determining **terms of reference**.


---


### Taxes


1️⃣ Review **cesses and surcharges**


2️⃣ Remove ceiling on **profession tax**


---


### Planning Commission


Role should be **coordination**, not micro-management.


---


# Key Conclusion of Punchhi Commission


The Commission concluded that:


➡ **Cooperative federalism is essential for India’s unity, integrity and development.**
