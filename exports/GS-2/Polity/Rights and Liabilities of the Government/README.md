
# Rights and Liabilities of the Government


(Articles **294–300** + **361**)


This part of the Constitution deals with the **property, contracts, rights, liabilities, obligations and suits of the Union and the States**.


The Constitution **treats the Union and the States as juristic (legal) persons**, meaning they can **own property, enter contracts, and sue or be sued**.


---


# 1. Property of the Union and the States


The Constitution explains how property and assets of the government are determined.


---


## 1. Succession (Articles 294 & 295)


After the commencement of the Constitution:


### Property Transfer


All property and assets that previously belonged to:

- **Dominion of India**
- **Provinces**
- **Indian Princely States**

became vested in:

- **Union Government**
- **Corresponding State Governments**

### Rights, Liabilities and Obligations


Similarly:


| Before Constitution | After Constitution  |
| ------------------- | ------------------- |
| Dominion of India   | Government of India |
| Provinces           | State Governments   |
| Indian States       | Corresponding State |


Thus all **rights, liabilities (देनदारियों) and obligations (दायित्वों) were transferred accordingly**.


---


## 2. Escheat, Lapse and Bona Vacantia (Article 296)


These are **three situations where property has no rightful owner**, so it goes to the government.


### (1) Escheat


When a person dies **without any legal heir**, his property goes to the government.


Example:


A person dies intestate (**बिना वसीयतनामा मारा हुआ**)(without a will) and has **no heirs**.


### (2) Lapse


Property rights **terminate due to non-use or failure to follow legal procedure**.


### (3) Bona Vacantia


Property **found without any owner**.


Example:

- Unclaimed bank money
- Unclaimed assets

### Ownership Rule


| Location of Property | Who Gets It      |
| -------------------- | ---------------- |
| Within a State       | State Government |
| Otherwise            | Union Government |


Reason: **No rightful claimant exists**.


---


## 3. Sea Wealth (Article 297)


All resources under the sea belong to the **Union Government**.


These include:

- Lands under the sea
- Minerals
- Other valuable things

Located in:

1. **Territorial Waters**
2. **Continental Shelf**
3. **Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ)**

Thus **coastal states cannot claim these resources**.


---


### Territorial Limits of India


| Zone                    | Distance               |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Territorial Waters      | **12 nautical miles**  |
| Exclusive Economic Zone | **200 nautical miles** |


These limits are defined under:


**Territorial Waters, Continental Shelf, Exclusive Economic Zone and Other Maritime Zones Act, 1976**.


---


## 4. Compulsory Acquisition by Law


Both:

- **Parliament**
- **State Legislatures**

can make laws to:

- **Acquire private property**
- **Requisition (मांग) private property**

---


### Impact of the 44th Constitutional Amendment Act (1978)


This amendment:

- **Removed Right to Property from Fundamental Rights**
- Made it a **Legal Right**

Now the Constitution **does not mandate compensation** in most cases.


---


### Two Exceptions (Compensation Must Be Paid)


Compensation must be paid when government acquires:


1️⃣ **Property of a minority educational institution**


2️⃣ **Land personally cultivated by a person**


when the land is **within statutory ceiling limits**. → 18–27 acres for partially irrigated land, and up to 54 acres for dry/uncultivable land. Limits vary by state


---


## 5. Acquisition under Executive Power (Article 298)


The **Union and States can acquire property without legislation** under their **executive powers**.


They can:

- Acquire property
- Hold property
- Dispose (निपटान) property

They can also:

- Carry on **trade**
- Conduct **business**

Even **outside their own state territory**.


Example:


A state government company operating in another state.


---


# 2. Suits by or Against the Government


(Article 300)


Article 300 explains **how the government can sue or be sued**.


---


### Legal Identity


The Constitution states:


| Entity             | Legal Name         |
| ------------------ | ------------------ |
| Central Government | **Union of India** |
| State Government   | **State of X**     |


Example:

- State of Uttar Pradesh
- State of Andhra Pradesh

Thus **Union and States are juristic persons** in legal proceedings.


---


### Continuity with Pre-Constitution System


Article 300 states:


Government liability will be the **same as before the Constitution**, unless Parliament or State Legislature makes a law.


But **no such law has been made yet**.


Therefore **old principles still apply**.


---


# Government Liability


Government liability is mainly of two types:


1️⃣ **Liability for Contracts**


2️⃣ **Liability for Torts**


---


# 1. Liability for Contracts


The Union and States can enter contracts under their **executive powers**.


These contracts may relate to:

- Acquisition of property
- Holding property
- Disposal of property
- Trade or business
- Any governmental purpose

---


### Constitutional Conditions for Government Contracts (Article 299)


Every government contract must satisfy **three mandatory conditions**:


1️⃣ It must be **expressed in the name of the President or Governor**


2️⃣ It must be **executed on behalf of the President or Governor**


3️⃣ It must be **executed by an authorised person**


Failure to satisfy these conditions makes the contract:

- **Void**
- **Unenforceable in courts**

These conditions are **mandatory**, not merely directory.


---


### Liability of President or Governor


The **President and Governor are NOT personally liable** for government contracts.


Similarly:


The **officer executing the contract is also not personally liable**.


Instead:


👉 **Government itself becomes liable**


Thus the Union and States are **suable for contractual liability like ordinary individuals**.


This principle has existed since the **East India Company era**.


---


# 2. Liability for Torts


### Meaning of Tort


A **tort** is a **civil wrong committed by a person that causes injury or damage to another**.


Examples:

- Negligence
- Wrongful detention
- Damage to property
- Misuse of authority

---


## Historical Background


Initially the **East India Company** was only a **trading organization**.


Later it:

- Acquired territories in India
- Became a **sovereign authority**

Thus two types of functions emerged:


| Function Type                    | Liability                 |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| **Trading/Commercial Functions** | Company could be sued     |
| **Sovereign Functions**          | Company could NOT be sued |


This principle was based on the English maxim:


### “The King Can Do No Wrong”


Meaning:

- The **sovereign (State) cannot be held liable for wrongs committed by its servants**.

This doctrine provided **immunity to the government**.


---


## Position in Britain


This immunity **no longer exists in Britain**.


It was abolished by the:


### Crown Proceedings Act, 1947


Now the British government **can be sued like any ordinary person**.


---


## Position in India


In India, **sovereign immunity still exists in principle**, but courts have **gradually limited it**.


Government liability depends on **nature of function**.


---


# Sovereign vs Non-Sovereign Functions


| Type                        | Meaning                                  | Liability             |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | --------------------- |
| **Sovereign Functions**     | Functions essential to State sovereignty | Government NOT liable |
| **Non-Sovereign Functions** | Commercial or welfare activities         | Government liable     |


---


### Examples of Sovereign Functions

- Administration of justice
- Defence operations
- Maintaining law and order
- Conduct of war
- Commandeering goods during war
- Building military roads

For these functions, **government traditionally enjoyed immunity**.


---


# Important Judicial Cases


These cases are extremely important for **UPPCS Prelims and Mains**.


---


# 1. P & O Steam Navigation Case (1861)


### Full Case Name


**Peninsular and Oriental Steam Navigation Company vs Secretary of State**


### Significance


This case **established the distinction between sovereign and non-sovereign functions**.


### Principle Established


Government:


| Function Type | Liability  |
| ------------- | ---------- |
| Non-sovereign | Liable     |
| Sovereign     | Not liable |


This became the **foundation of state liability doctrine in India**.


---


# 2. Kasturilal Case (1965)


### Case


**Kasturilal vs State of Uttar Pradesh**


### Facts


Gold seized by police was **lost due to negligence of police officer**.


### Supreme Court Judgment


Court held:


Government **not liable** because the act was done while performing **sovereign function**.


### Importance

- Reaffirmed **sovereign immunity doctrine**.

However later cases **criticised this judgment**.


---


# 3. Nagendra Rao Case (1994)


### Case


**N. Nagendra Rao & Co. vs State of Andhra Pradesh**


### Supreme Court's Approach


The Court adopted a **liberal approach** and criticised the sovereign immunity doctrine.


### Key Principles


The Court stated:


1️⃣ State cannot escape liability if **damage occurs due to negligence of government officials**.


2️⃣ Distinction between **sovereign and non-sovereign functions is outdated**.


3️⃣ Modern welfare state performs **numerous functions affecting citizens' lives**.


4️⃣ State should not be **above law**.


### Key Observation


No civilized system should allow government to:

> “play with people and claim immunity because it is sovereign.”

---


### Important Conclusion


Only a **few core functions remain immune**, such as:

- Administration of justice
- Maintenance of law and order
- Repression of crime

Most other activities **should not enjoy immunity**.


---


# 4. Common Cause Case (1999)


### Case


**Common Cause, Registered Society vs Union of India**


### Supreme Court Observations


Court declared:

- Doctrine from **P & O Steam Navigation case is outdated**.
- Distinction between sovereign and non-sovereign functions **is very difficult in modern times**.

Reason:


Modern state performs activities in almost **every sphere of life**.


Examples:

- Education
- Economy
- Social welfare
- Commercial activities

### Conclusion of Court


State liability must match the **concept of welfare state**.


Thus the State should be **liable for torts of its employees**.


---


# 5. Prisoner’s Murder Case (2000)


### Case


**State of Andhra Pradesh vs Challa Ramkrishna Reddy**


### Supreme Court Observation


The Court stated:


The **Kasturilal judgment has lost significance**.


Thus sovereign immunity doctrine has been **greatly weakened**.


---


# Key Trend in Indian Law


The modern judicial trend is:


➡ **Reducing sovereign immunity**


➡ Increasing **state accountability**


---


# 3. Suits Against Public Officials


The Constitution also provides **immunities and liabilities of officials**.


---


# 1. President and Governor


(Article 361)


The Constitution grants **special protections** to:

- President of India
- Governors of States

These immunities relate to:


1️⃣ Official Acts


2️⃣ Personal Acts


---


## Immunity for Official Acts


President and Governor **cannot be sued** for acts done:

- In exercise of official powers
- During their tenure
- Even after leaving office

However:


Their official conduct **can be reviewed** during impeachment proceedings.


Also:


A person can file suit against:


| Instead of | Case Filed Against |
| ---------- | ------------------ |
| President  | Union of India     |
| Governor   | Concerned State    |


---


## Immunity for Personal Acts


### Criminal Proceedings


During their term:

- **No criminal proceedings can be initiated**
- They **cannot be arrested**
- They **cannot be imprisoned**

This immunity **ends after their tenure**.


---


### Civil Proceedings


Civil cases can be filed **during tenure** but only after:


**Two months prior notice**.


---


# 2. Ministers


The Constitution **does not give immunity** to ministers.


However:


They are **not liable for official acts of President or Governor**.


Reason:


Courts **cannot inquire into ministerial advice**.


This is based on **Article 74 and 163 principles**.


---


### Personal Liability of Ministers


Ministers are:

- Liable for **crimes**
- Liable for **torts**

Just like **ordinary citizens**.


---


# 3. Judicial Officers


Judges enjoy **complete protection for judicial acts**.


This protection comes from:


### Judicial Officers Protection Act, 1850


Provision:


No judge, magistrate, collector or person acting judicially can be sued for acts done **in discharge of official duties**.


---


# 4. Civil Servants


Civil servants also have **certain protections**.


---


## Liability for Government Contracts


Civil servants executing contracts **are not personally liable**.


Instead:


👉 Liability lies with the **government**.


However:


If the contract **violates constitutional conditions**, the civil servant **may become personally liable**.


---


## Liability for Torts


Civil servants enjoy immunity for acts done:

- While performing **sovereign functions** of government.

For other acts:


They are liable like **ordinary citizens**.


---


## Civil Proceedings


Civil proceedings against civil servants for official acts require:


### Two Months Prior Notice


However:


No notice is needed if the act was **outside official duty**.


---


## Criminal Proceedings


Criminal proceedings can be initiated only after:


### Prior Sanction


From:


| Officer                    | Permission Required From       |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Central Government servant | President / Central Government |
| State Government servant   | Governor / State Government    |


This rule comes from **Criminal Procedure Code**.
